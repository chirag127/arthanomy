Almost every financial headline carries a hidden claim about who is in charge of the thing being reported. A change in the policy interest rate, a new cap on a lending product, a rule about how quickly a bank must credit a failed transaction — each belongs to a different part of the state, and knowing which part is the difference between understanding a story and merely repeating it. The Reserve Bank of India is the largest single piece of that machinery. This opening article in the Indian Financial System series maps what the RBI does and, just as usefully, where its remit stops.

## Explain simply

A central bank is the bank of the banks and the bank of the government. It issues the currency, sets the price at which banks can borrow short-term money from it, supervises the banks and the payment systems they use, administers the rules on moving money abroad, and stands ready to lend when the plumbing seizes up. It does not decide which shares rise, does not regulate insurance or pensions, and does not guarantee that any investment will not lose money. The RBI is the referee and the emergency plumber of the money system, not the coach of your portfolio.

## One institution, several mandates

The RBI was created by statute in the 1930s, before independence, and its remit has widened since. It is useful to think of it as several institutions sharing one balance sheet:

- a **monetary authority**, which sets the terms on which short-term money is available;
- a **note-issuing authority**, which supplies the currency in your wallet;
- a **regulator and supervisor** of banks, non-banking financial companies and payment systems;
- a **manager of the external account**, administering the rules under which rupees become foreign currency and back again;
- a **banker to the government**, holding its accounts and managing its borrowing;
- a **developer**, in the sense that it has historically built institutions and pressed for financial inclusion.

Each mandate has its own tools, constraints and vocabulary. Most confusion about the RBI comes from carrying a sentence from one column into another.

## Monetary policy: the price and the quantity of money

Monetary policy is the part of the RBI's work that reaches ordinary borrowers fastest. Two levers matter.

The first is the **price** of short-term funds. The policy repo rate is the rate at which the RBI lends to banks against government securities; the standing deposit facility and related windows are the rates at which it absorbs surplus cash. Around those sits a corridor of standing facilities — the marginal standing facility at the upper end, the absorption rate at the lower end — together with the Bank Rate, a long-standing reference rate. Open market operations buy and sell government securities to add or drain liquidity.

The second is the **quantity** of money banks must hold back. The cash reserve ratio requires banks to hold a share of their deposits as balances with the RBI; the statutory liquidity ratio requires a share to be held in specified liquid assets, historically government securities. Raising either shrinks the funds available to lend.

Setting the policy rate is a committee decision rather than a single governor's. Under the flexible inflation-targeting framework, the Government notifies a numerical inflation target and a tolerance band, and a Monetary Policy Committee with both RBI and external members votes on the policy rate to pursue it. This article deliberately does not quote the target or the current policy rate: both are set by notification, both change, and the operative numbers belong in the notifications rather than in a summary. What matters for reading the news is the *structure* — a committee, a mandate, a target expressed as a number, and a set of instruments used to move short-term rates toward it.

Transmission to retail borrowers runs largely through floating-rate loans that are contractually linked to an external benchmark, so a policy change mechanically resets the benchmark. Transmission is neither instantaneous nor complete; the pace depends on deposit competition, credit demand and how much of the change banks pass through. That gap is one of the most reported and least well understood parts of the system.

## The currency: who actually issues the rupee

The rupee in your pocket arrives through two doors. Coins and the one-rupee note are issued by the Government of India; all other banknotes are issued by the RBI. Both enter circulation through the RBI's network of currency chests and bank branches, and both are liabilities whose supply the central bank manages against its reserve assets.

Two consequences follow. First, note issue is not "printing money" in the loose sense used at dinner tables; it is the supply of a liability against defined reserve assets, with a statutory minimum reserve requirement behind it. Second, the design of the currency — its security features, the withdrawal of old series, the handling of mutilated notes — is an operational responsibility that shapes everyday trust in money.

## Banker to the government, and manager of the public debt

The RBI holds the government's accounts, processes receipts and payments, and provides short-term accommodation through ways-and-means advances when the government's cash flow and its spending are temporarily out of step. It also runs the auctions through which the government borrows for longer periods, and operates the retail channel that lets individuals bid directly for government securities.

Whether debt management should sit inside the central bank or in a separate office is a long-running institutional debate. The argument for separation is that a debt manager has an interest in cheap funding while a monetary authority has an interest in stable prices, and the two can conflict. The argument for keeping them together is operational: the central bank already holds the accounts and knows the market. The direction of travel favours a phased separation; the unresolved questions are timing and design.

## Regulator and supervisor

This is the mandate most likely to affect whether you are protected. The RBI licenses and supervises scheduled commercial banks, many cooperative banks, non-banking financial companies and the operators of payment systems. It sets prudential rules — how much capital a bank must hold against its loans, how it must classify a loan that has stopped performing — and it has powers to restrict a weak institution's business before depositors are exposed.

The framework's headline capital measure is the capital-to-risk-weighted-assets ratio. Under the RBI's rules, scheduled commercial banks must maintain a minimum of 9%, above the 8% Basel III international floor, with additional buffers for the largest institutions. The ratio measures a bank's ability to absorb losses ahead of its depositors, but it is not a guarantee, because it depends on the risk weights the bank itself assigns to its assets. That dependence is precisely why the regulator also runs periodic asset-quality reviews.

Alongside the rulebook sits a supervisory escalation ladder: as capital, asset quality or profitability deteriorate, an institution moves through progressively tighter restrictions on lending and expansion. The RBI also publishes a periodic Financial Stability Report surveying system-level stress, bank asset-quality trends and macro-financial risks — the semi-annual document to read if you want the regulator's own assessment rather than a journalist's summary of it.

## The external account

Under the foreign-exchange framework, the RBI administers the rules governing how residents and non-residents hold, earn and move foreign currency. For individuals, the best-known element is the annual allowance under which a resident may remit funds abroad for permitted purposes — the Liberalised Remittance Scheme. The scheme sits in the RBI's remit, but it does not stand alone: the tax code attaches collection-at-source obligations to some remittances and a reporting obligation on foreign assets, which is why the LRS article on this site treats the regulatory and tax sides together.

The RBI also manages the country's foreign-exchange reserves. The stated priorities are safety and liquidity before return, and intervention in the currency market is aimed at smoothing disorderly moves rather than defending a particular level. Treat any claim that the central bank "targets" a specific exchange rate with scepticism.

## Payment and settlement

Modern money is mostly a set of messages between institutions, and the RBI regulates that messaging. Under the payment-systems statute it authorises and oversees operators, sets standards for settlement finality and risk, and runs the large-value and retail transfer systems that sit beneath the interbank layer. The retail instant-payment rails households use are operated by an entity owned by the banking system under the RBI's oversight rather than by the RBI directly — a division of labour that explains why a consumer complaint about a payment sometimes travels through a different door than a complaint about a bank.

## A developer, on purpose

The RBI has never treated itself as purely a rule-setter. It has incubated development finance institutions, pushed banks toward lending to small business and agriculture through mandated sector targets, and used regulatory tools to widen access to basic banking and digital payments. Whether that developmental role is a proper function of a central bank or a distraction from price stability is a genuine and unresolved argument. The point for a reader is that some RBI decisions are justified in financial-inclusion terms rather than monetary terms, and are best judged against that objective.

## What the RBI does not control

The boundaries matter as much as the mandate:

| If the subject is... | The relevant authority is usually... |
| --- | --- |
| Shares, mutual funds, exchanges, brokers, insider trading | SEBI |
| Insurance policies and the insurance ombudsman | IRDAI |
| Pensions under the national pension system | PFRDA |
| Insolvency and creditor recovery | The tribunals under the insolvency code |
| A consumer dispute over a purchase, including a mis-sold product | The consumer commissions |
| Cheating, fraud and unregulated deposit-taking schemes | The police and the economic offences wings, in parallel with regulators |

Deposit insurance is an example of a function that sits *next to* the RBI rather than inside it. Cover is administered by the deposit-insurance corporation, a wholly owned subsidiary of the RBI, and protects a depositor up to a statutory limit per depositor per bank that is revised from time to time, covering principal and interest as the statute defines them while excluding certain categories of depositor. The mechanism — a statutory limit, a per-bank computation, a claim process — is what a reader should internalise; the current figure belongs in the current notification.

Grievance redress is similar. The RBI's integrated ombudsman scheme provides a free forum for complaints against banks and non-bank lenders once the institution itself has had a defined period, commonly around thirty days, to respond, and an award is binding on the institution up to a statutory limit. The design principle is common across financial regulators: complain to the firm first, escalate to the regulator's forum only when the firm has failed to resolve.

## Worked example: what reserves support

Why do central-bank balance-sheet actions matter so much? A simple, deliberately hypothetical calculation shows the leverage.

Suppose, purely to illustrate the arithmetic, that a banking system were required to hold reserves equal to one-tenth of its deposits, and that no other constraint applied. For every ₹100 of central-bank reserves the system held, it could support ₹1,000 of deposits:

$$\text{maximum deposits} = \frac{\text{reserves}}{c} = \frac{100}{0.1} = 1{,}000$$

This is an illustrative calculation, not observed data. The assumed ratio is a hypothetical number chosen to make the arithmetic visible; it is not the prevailing requirement, and the real system is constrained by capital, liquidity and the demand for loans as well as by reserves. The point of the exercise is directional: small changes in a reserve ratio imply large changes in the deposit base, which is why a change that looks like a rounding error in a headline can be material to credit conditions.

## Reading a central-bank headline: four questions

1. **Which mandate is this?** Monetary policy, currency, supervision, external account or payments. The mandate determines whom the decision affects and how fast.
2. **Is this the rule or the number?** A framework that says the target is set by notification is durable; the number in force today may not be.
3. **Whom does it bind?** A direction to banks is not a direction to you. The obligation usually lands on an intermediary, and your remedy, if any, runs through that intermediary.
4. **What is outside the RBI?** If the subject is a listed security, an insurance contract or a pension, you are reading about someone else's regulator.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes institutional architecture, not current policy. It does not state the policy rate, the inflation target, the reserve ratios, the deposit-insurance limit or the ombudsman award ceiling, because every one of those is a number that changes by notification and belongs in the notification. It does not evaluate whether the RBI's decisions have been correct, and it does not tell you what any policy change means for a particular portfolio. Institutional roles are stable; the numbers attached to them are not.

The step from a savings account to a debt fund is usually taken in the wrong order. Investors move straight into a scheme whose label promises safety, without asking what the scheme adds to cash. Ultra-short duration schemes are the honest answer to that question: they sit just above the cash end of the range, holding short-term debt with a maturity profile that is deliberately a little longer than a liquid scheme's. That extra length is the whole product. It earns a bit more than the shortest instruments when the curve is upward-sloping, and it costs a bit more when short rates rise. This article sets out what the band constrains, what it adds, and how to decide whether money belongs in it at all.

## Explain simply

Think of short-term money as a small ladder of jobs. At the bottom is money you might need this week, which belongs in same-day access and should not be exposed to any price movement. The next rung is money you definitely will not need for a few months. Ultra-short funds occupy that rung: they hold debt that matures soon, but not quite as soon as a liquid fund, so their unit value moves slightly when interest rates change. In exchange they can pursue a somewhat higher return than the shortest category. The point of the product is not to be safe in the way a deposit is safe; it is to earn a little more on money that has a real, but short, horizon.

## Why the category exists

The categorisation framework organises debt schemes along a maturity axis, from schemes holding instruments that mature in days out to schemes built for long-dated interest-rate positions. Between the two extremes it defines several bands, and the ultra-short band sits immediately above the liquid and overnight categories. The framework's purpose is comparison: because each band imposes a portfolio rule, two schemes in the same band are comparable in kind, even if their returns diverge.

That is why the category is best understood as a constraint rather than a description. A manager cannot make an ultra-short scheme as sensitive as a long-dated gilt fund, and cannot make it behave exactly like a liquid scheme either. The band fixes a range of behaviour; the return inside that range is decided by portfolio choice, trading and cost.

## What the maturity band constrains

Two structural limits define the band, and neither is a number this article will state.

- **The weighted-average maturity of the portfolio.** The framework caps how long the portfolio's average maturity may be. This is the single rule that produces the category's characteristic behaviour: because the average is short, the whole portfolio's sensitivity to a change in yields is small.
- **The instruments the portfolio may hold and their quality.** Money-market and short-term debt instruments of stated minimum quality make up the body of the portfolio, with limits on what else may be used.

The operative numbers behind both rules — the maturity band and the quality floor — are set in the categorisation circular and have been amended over time. A summary cannot substitute for the current text, and any figure quoted from memory should be treated as needing verification. What can be said structurally is that the band is short by design, longer than the cash-like categories and shorter than the short-duration category that sits above it.

## What "modest interest-rate sensitivity" actually means

Interest-rate sensitivity is measured by duration. Yield to maturity states a level; modified duration states how much the price moves when that level changes. For a small change in yield, the approximate change in value is the modified duration multiplied by the change in yield, with the sign reversed:

$$ \Delta P \approx -D \times \Delta y \times P $$

The important word in the category's description is *modest*. Because the weighted-average maturity is short, the modified duration is small, so the price effect of a rate change is small. A scheme whose modified duration is a fraction of a year will barely register a change that would be material to a fund with a duration measured in years. That is the trade the category makes: it accepts a little price movement in return for instruments that yield more than the very shortest ones.

Two qualifications keep that description honest. First, modified duration is an approximation that understates the effect of large yield moves unless a convexity term is added. Second, a short portfolio does not escape mark-to-market valuation; it simply changes less per unit of yield change. Both points matter when the money has a fixed date of use.

## The four risks, separated

As in every debt category, four risks operate at once, and the label does not separate them.

- **Credit risk** is the risk that an issuer fails to pay. The band's quality floor limits it, but a downgrade or default in a holding shows up in the NAV immediately, and in a short portfolio a single event can dominate a year's interest income.
- **Interest-rate risk** is the risk that a change in market yields revalues the portfolio. In this band it is small but positive, and it is the reason the category yields more than a liquid scheme.
- **Liquidity risk** is the risk of selling at a poor price when cash is needed. Short-term instruments can trade thinly, and a redemption-heavy period can force sales at unfavourable levels.
- **Mark-to-market behaviour** is the mechanism that turns the first three into a change in unit value. There is no accrual cushion that hides the effect; the NAV reflects market prices each day.

## Why a higher yield than cash is compensation

An ultra-short scheme usually offers more than a liquid scheme, and the difference is not skill alone. It is the price of something.

- Some of the extra comes from **carrying more duration**: the portfolio is longer, so it earns a term premium when the curve slopes upward and loses when short rates rise.
- Some comes from **credit spread** on holdings that are permitted but not sovereign, which is payment for default risk.
- Some comes from **holding less liquidity** than the safest category, which pays until a moment of stress and then becomes a cost.
- Some is given back through the **expense ratio**, which reduces the NAV growth the investor receives.

A yield above a peer is a statement about which of these a scheme has accepted. Comparing two ultra-short funds therefore means comparing their duration, their credit quality and their cost — not their trailing returns alone.

## Ultra-short against its neighbours

| Category | Maturity posture | Dominant risk | Typical role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Overnight | Shortest available | Minimal | Same-day cash management |
| Liquid | Very short, tightly capped | Small credit and rate risk | Parking money for weeks |
| Ultra-short | Short, but longer than liquid | Small rate risk plus credit | Money with a months-long horizon |
| Short duration | Longer band again | Balance of rate and credit | Money with a one-to-three-year horizon |

The rows differ by degree rather than by kind, which is why the categories are easy to confuse. The useful discipline is to read the row as a horizon, not as a score. A scheme is not "better" because it sits lower in the table; it is better suited to a particular length of time.

## Worked example: an illustrative rate move and credit event

The following is an illustrative calculation from assumed inputs, not a forecast and not any scheme's observed behaviour. Suppose an investor holds ₹1,00,000 in an ultra-short scheme whose portfolio is assumed to have a modified duration of 0.5 years.

**A rate move.** If short-term yields rise by half a percentage point, the approximate NAV change is the modified duration multiplied by the yield change, sign reversed:

$$ \Delta NAV \approx -D \times \Delta y $$

That is 0.5 multiplied by 0.5%, giving an assumed fall of about 0.25%, or roughly ₹250 on ₹1,00,000, before the interest the portfolio continues to accrue. Compare that with the assumed modified duration of 0.15 used for a liquid scheme elsewhere in this series: the same rate move would cost roughly ₹75 there. The difference is the price of the longer band.

**A credit event.** Assume separately that one holding representing 2% of the portfolio defaults, with a recovery of 40% assumed, so the loss given default is 60%. The loss is the weight multiplied by the loss given default:

$$ \text{Loss} \approx w \times LGD $$

That is 0.02 multiplied by 0.60, or 1.2% of NAV, about ₹1,200 on ₹1,00,000. In a short-maturity portfolio, which earns only a thin spread above the risk-free rate, a loss of that size can erase a substantial part of a year's income. The illustration is the reason the quality floor, not the maturity cap, deserves the closest reading when the category is chosen.

## Who it is for, and for how long

Ultra-short schemes are for money with a defined horizon of roughly a few months — money that is not needed without warning and is not needed for years. Three tests decide whether the placement is honest.

1. **Can the date be named?** If the money has no date, the category has no answer, and the money belongs lower in the ladder.
2. **Does the horizon exceed the sensitivity?** A horizon comfortably longer than the portfolio's modified duration means a rate move can be waited out rather than realised.
3. **Is a higher return actually needed?** The extra yield over cash is small; if the money is a buffer against surprises, the buffer's job is availability, and yield is secondary.

## The four lanes, in this context

- **Contractual.** Coupons and money-market instrument yields are promises with a legal basis, backed by specific issuers. They are the part of the return that does not depend on price change.
- **Model-estimated.** The duration arithmetic above is a model estimate from stated assumptions, as is any projection of a portfolio's income from its yield.
- **Historical realised.** What ultra-short schemes delivered over a past window, computed from scheme-level NAV data. It describes that window and does not transfer to the next.
- **Speculative.** The gain from a fall in short rates and the loss from a rise. This lane is what the slightly longer maturity band makes possible, and it is the reason the category exists above cash.

## What this does not tell you

- **It does not state the band.** The weighted-average maturity range and the quality floor are set in the categorisation circular and have been amended; the operative figures must be read there.
- **It does not rank schemes.** Two schemes in the band can differ in duration, credit and cost, and only the disclosures show the difference.
- **It does not handle tax.** Gains from debt-oriented schemes are taxed under rules that depend on holding period and change over time; the post-tax arithmetic belongs with the dated tax reference.
- **It does not replace same-day access.** The emergency layer sits below this category, and no amount of category discipline changes that.
- **It does not promise an outcome.** The category constrains holdings, not results.

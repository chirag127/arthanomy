An annual report is written in an order that suits the company and read, ideally, in an order that suits the sceptical holder. The financial statements are not the natural starting point, because their meaning depends on what the auditor says about them. The directors' report is not the natural starting point either, because it is the part written to be read first and it is the least constrained by accounting rules.

This article in the Equity Mastery series sets out a reading procedure: what to check in the auditor's report, the related-party note, the contingent liabilities, the cash-flow statement and the segment disclosures, and the specific questions a reader who is not an accountant can still ask.

## Which document governs

A listed company publishes its annual report and files its annual accounts with the company-law registry. Both should describe the same financial year with the same numbers, and the audited financial statements are the version with statutory backing. Where the glossy published report and the filed accounts appear to differ, the filed accounts are the document to trust, and the difference itself is a question worth asking.

The reading order below assumes the audited statements and their notes are in hand. It is deliberately not the order in which the report is printed.

## A reading order that pays off

1. **The auditor's report** — opinion, key audit matters, going-concern statements and any qualifications.
2. **The cash-flow statement** — profit reconciled to cash across operating, investing and financing activities.
3. **The notes on related-party transactions** — who the company deals with, on what basis and what remains outstanding.
4. **The notes on contingent liabilities and commitments** — guarantees, claims and tax demands that are not yet provided for.
5. **The segment note** — where the revenue and the profit actually come from.
6. **The balance sheet and the income statement** — now read with the four items above as context.
7. **The directors' report and the management discussion** — read last, because it is a narrative that should be reconciled against the numbers rather than accepted as a summary of them.

## The auditor's report

The opinion is the single most important paragraph in the document. An unmodified opinion states that the financial statements give a true and fair view in accordance with the applicable framework. A modified opinion — qualified, adverse, or a disclaimer — changes how much weight the numbers can carry, and the basis for the modification paragraph explains what the auditor could not accept.

Beyond the opinion, five things are worth checking.

- **Key audit matters.** These are the areas the auditor judged most significant in the audit, and they are a shortlist of where the accounting is least mechanical. A change in the list from the previous year is itself informative.
- **Going concern.** Where there is material uncertainty about the company continuing as a going concern, the auditor is expected to say so. The absence of such a statement is not a guarantee; its presence is a serious matter.
- **Emphasis of matter and other matter paragraphs.** These draw attention to something without modifying the opinion. They are worth reading as a marker of what the auditor wanted on the record.
- **The prescribed company-law observations.** The auditor's report also carries the observations required under company law on matters such as fixed assets, inventories, loans to related parties and statutory dues. Adverse remarks in these paragraphs deserve the same attention as a qualification.
- **Continuity and independence.** A change of auditor, a resignation mid-term, or audit fees that are dwarfed by fees for other services are governance signals about the audit relationship rather than about the accounts.

## Questions to ask of the auditor's report

- Is the opinion unmodified, and if not, exactly what is qualified and how large is the affected figure?
- Did the key audit matters change from last year, and what does the change point to?
- Is there any reference to going concern, even a reassuring one?
- Did the auditor change, and was the change explained?
- What proportion of the total fee paid to the auditor was for non-audit services?

None of these questions requires accounting knowledge. They require reading the report rather than skipping to the numbers.

## Related-party transactions

A related-party transaction is a dealing between the company and someone connected to it — a group company, a promoter entity, a director, a key manager or their relatives or firms. Such transactions are common and are not improper in themselves. What matters is whether they are disclosed, whether the terms are stated to be at arm's length, and whether their scale relative to the rest of the business is material.

The disclosure sits in the notes to the accounts, with a policy and audit-committee oversight under the listing-disclosure framework. Four things are worth extracting.

- **Volume and direction.** How much revenue comes from related parties, and how much of the cost base is paid to them?
- **Pricing basis.** Is the basis on which the price was set stated, and is it consistent year to year?
- **Outstanding balances.** Are amounts owed by or to related parties growing, and are they being settled on normal terms?
- **Who exactly.** Are the counterparties connected to those who control the company, which affects how much weight the "arm's length" statement can carry?

## Questions to ask of the related-party note

- What share of total revenue and total purchases involves related parties?
- Are receivables from related parties growing faster than revenue, which would mean the company is effectively funding its own group?
- Are any loans or advances to related parties material, and is any provision held against them?
- Are the transactions approved by the audit committee, and is the pricing basis stated rather than asserted?

## Contingent liabilities and commitments

A contingent liability is a possible obligation whose outcome depends on a future event, such as a disputed tax demand, a lawsuit, a guarantee given for a group entity, or a claim under a contract. It is not recognised as a liability on the balance sheet when the outflow is not probable or cannot be measured reliably, but it is disclosed in the notes. Commitments describe amounts the company is contractually obliged to spend, such as capital expenditure already contracted for.

The test is relative rather than absolute. A tax demand is not alarming in itself; a tax demand that is large against net worth, or one that recurs across years, is a different matter.

## Questions to ask of the contingent liability note

- What is the total disclosed amount, and how does it compare with net worth?
- Are the largest items tax demands under appeal, guarantees for group companies, or operational claims?
- Has the total grown over several years faster than the business?
- For guarantees, who is the beneficiary, and is the underlying borrower able to pay?

## Cash flow versus profit

Profit is an accrual number: revenue is recognised when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of when cash moves. The cash-flow statement expresses the same year in cash. Reading them together is the most productive comparison available to a non-accountant, because a business that reports profit while generating no cash is accumulating either receivables it cannot collect or inventory it cannot sell.

The operating section begins from profit before tax and adjusts for non-cash items such as depreciation, then for changes in working capital, and then for interest and tax paid. A profit figure that repeatedly converts into a much smaller operating cash flow is a signal worth investigating rather than a verdict in itself, since growing companies legitimately absorb working capital.

## Questions to ask of the cash-flow statement

- Is operating cash flow positive, and does it track reported profit over five years?
- Is revenue growth accompanied by receivables or inventory growing faster still?
- Is capital expenditure funded from operations or from fresh borrowing?
- Do the interest and tax actually paid in cash match the amounts charged in the income statement?
- What portion of profit came from non-operating items such as asset sales or write-backs?

## Segment disclosures

Where a company has more than one line of business or operates in more than one geography, the segment note splits revenue and profit accordingly. This is where a headline growth rate gets decomposed. A company can report respectable consolidated growth while one segment is contracting, another is being funded by the first, and a third is described as strategic for several years without earning a profit.

The questions are structural rather than accounting-based.

- Which segment generates the profit, and is it the same segment that generates the revenue?
- Do segment margins differ materially from the blended margin, and is the difference widening?
- Is the segment definition consistent with last year, and has it been redrawn?
- Which common costs are allocated to segments, and on what basis?
- What is described in the strategy section as the growth engine, and how large is it in the segment note?

## Cross-checks that catch inconsistencies

Three cross-references are cheap and effective. Compare the directors' report narrative with the segment note, since a claim about a business line should be visible in the numbers for that line. Compare the auditor's report with the statements it covers, because a key audit matter usually points to a note worth reading in full. And compare secured borrowings in the balance sheet with the charge registrations the company has filed with the company-law registry, since a lender's registered charge and the disclosed borrowing should tell the same story.

## Explain simply

An annual report is a company describing its own year. Read the auditor's opinion first, because it tells you whether the numbers can be relied on and what the auditor found difficult. Then read the cash-flow statement against the profit figure, because profit is a promise and cash is a fact. Then read three notes: who the company is doing business with inside its own group, what obligations it might face that are not yet on the balance sheet, and how much of the profit each part of the business actually produced. Read the glossy directors' report last, and check its claims against the numbers rather than the other way round.

## Worked example

An illustrative reconciliation for a fictional company, not figures from any real accounts.

Suppose a company reports a profit after tax of ₹100 crore for the year. Depreciation charged during the year is ₹30 crore, which reduced profit but involved no cash. Over the same year, trade receivables rose by ₹60 crore, inventory rose by ₹35 crore, and trade payables rose by ₹15 crore.

| Line | Amount | Effect on cash |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profit after tax | ₹100 crore | Starting point |
| Add back depreciation | ₹30 crore | Non-cash charge |
| Subtract increase in receivables | -₹60 crore | Cash tied up with customers |
| Subtract increase in inventory | -₹35 crore | Cash tied up in stock |
| Add increase in payables | ₹15 crore | Cash retained by delaying suppliers |
| Cash generated from operations before interest and tax | ₹50 crore | Result |

The reconciliation shows the point precisely. A reported profit of ₹100 crore produced ₹50 crore of cash from operations, because ₹80 crore was absorbed by receivables and inventory and ₹15 crore was released by payables. Deducting interest and tax paid in cash would reduce the figure further.

On its own, this is not a problem: a company growing quickly will absorb working capital. The question is whether the same pattern repeats year after year, because a persistent gap means the growth is being financed by the company's own balance sheet rather than by its customers.

## What this does not tell you

This is a reading procedure, not a valuation method and not a fraud test. It cannot tell you what a company is worth, and it cannot detect a misstatement that the auditor did not detect, because it operates on the same statements the auditor signed.

Three limits follow. Audited accounts are an opinion expressed within an accounting framework, and within that framework there is legitimate room for judgement in estimates, provisioning and revenue timing; an unusual treatment is a question, not necessarily an error. An annual report is retrospective: it describes the year that has ended, and the business may already have changed. And disclosure is a floor rather than a ceiling — a company that satisfies every disclosure requirement has still chosen how prominently to present what it has to disclose, which is why the notes and the cross-checks matter more than the narrative sections.

## Open questions

- Does the growth rate of related-party balances relative to third-party balances predict later write-offs, and over what horizon?
- Can the gap between reported profit and operating cash flow be calibrated into a usable early-warning indicator without generating excessive false positives?
- Do companies redraw segment definitions around the time performance deteriorates, and is that pattern measurable across a large sample?

Every fraud article in this series ends in the same uncomfortable place: prevention is the whole game, and recovery is rare. But prevention failing is not the end of the story. There is an administrative layer between a loss and a lawsuit, and it exists precisely so that ordinary investors do not have to hire counsel to be heard. For complaints against listed companies and registered market intermediaries, that layer is SEBI SCORES — the securities regulator's centralised grievance-redress system.

Used properly, a complaint is neither a plea nor a protest. It is a structured record: a statement of what happened, supported by documents, addressed to the right counterparty, escalated in the right order. Used badly, it is a long email that nobody can act on. The difference between the two is almost entirely preparation.

## Explain simply

SCORES — SEBI Complaints Redress System — is the official complaints desk for India's securities market. If a listed company, a broker, a mutual fund, an adviser or another SEBI-registered firm has treated you badly, you register the complaint there; it is routed to the firm, which must resolve it within a stipulated period; if it does not, the complaint is automatically escalated, first to a designated body and then to SEBI for review. After that, a further route exists through online dispute resolution. It is not a court, and it does not order anyone to pay you. What it does is force a response and put your version of events on the regulator's record — which is often the only leverage an individual has.

## What SCORES is

SCORES is an online platform operated by SEBI to let investors lodge and track complaints against listed companies, registered intermediaries and other market participants. Complaints are lodged electronically, forwarded to the named entity, tracked through to closure, and, where the entity fails to resolve them, escalated for further review. SEBI describes three objectives: transparency, speed and effective resolution.

The design has three parts, and it helps to see them as a pipeline rather than a form:

1. **Lodgement.** The investor registers and files a complaint against a specific entity that is within the regulator's remit.
2. **Entity response.** The complaint is auto-routed to the firm, which must resolve it and file an action-taken report.
3. **Escalation.** If the firm does not resolve the matter, the complaint is auto-escalated for review, and the record becomes part of a pattern that informs supervision and, in serious cases, enforcement.

That third step is why the portal matters even when a single complaint seems to go nowhere. One unresolved complaint looks like a bad experience; a cluster of them across many investors in the same firm is intelligence.

## Who is in scope, and who is not

This is the distinction that causes the most wasted effort, so state it plainly.

**In scope** are the participants the regulator licenses and supervises: listed companies in their dealings with shareholders, stock brokers and their authorised persons, depository participants, asset management companies, registrar and transfer agents, merchant bankers, portfolio managers, investment advisers and research analysts, and similar registered intermediaries. If your counterparty holds a registration with the market regulator, a service or conduct complaint against it belongs here.

**Out of scope** are entities and disputes the regulator does not control, and SEBI's own guidance lists the submissions it will not treat as complaints:

- **Unregistered entities.** A firm that never registered, or whose registration has lapsed, is not a participant the regime can discipline through this channel. Unregistered collective investment schemes, pyramid and multi-level marketing structures, and money-multiplier arrangements are examples.
- **Criminal conduct.** Cheating, forgery, criminal breach of trust and organised fraud are matters for the police and the economic offences wings, not for an administrative grievance desk.
- **Anonymous, incomplete or unsupported complaints.** A complaint without supporting documents, or one that is merely a suggestion or a request for guidance, is not treated as a complaint at all.
- **Matters belonging to another forum.** Disputes against companies under another regulator, complaints against a company under a moratorium or struck off from the register of companies, complaints about the trading price of a listed share, and matters already pending in a court, in a quasi-judicial proceeding, or before the online-dispute-resolution mechanism are routed elsewhere.
- **Market intelligence.** Information that a person has violated securities law is treated as market intelligence and is lodged on the market-intelligence portal rather than as an investor complaint.

Deciding which of these you are in is the first and most important step. Filing a criminal allegation through a complaints portal wastes time; by the time you discover the mistake, evidence may have decayed and deadlines may have passed.

## The entity-first design

The framework expects you to raise the matter with the firm before escalating, and it is also a precondition for the online-dispute-resolution route described later. The reason is practical: most complaints are resolvable at source, and a centralised regulator drowning in complaints that the firm never saw would resolve fewer of them. So the sequence is:

**Approach the firm formally → lodge on SCORES within one year → the firm resolves within 21 calendar days → escalate through review if unresolved.**

The word *formally* carries weight. A telephone call is not a record. A written complaint — email is sufficient — to the firm's designated grievance channel, quoting the account or folio, stating the facts and the remedy sought, is a record. It is also the document that proves, at the escalation stage, that the firm was given its chance.

## Filing and escalating, step by step

The mechanical steps are deliberately mundane, and the portal guides them.

1. **Register** on the SCORES portal using the identity details it requires — name, address, email, mobile number and PAN. The platform is integrated with the KYC registration database, so details can be fetched after PAN and mobile-OTP validation.
2. **Identify the entity** you are complaining about, and confirm its registration status rather than assuming it.
3. **Describe the facts** in dated, specific terms, and select the complaint category that fits. Supporting documents are uploaded as PDFs within the portal's stated size limit.
4. **Record the registration number** the portal generates on submission, and use it to track the complaint.
5. **Wait for the action-taken report.** Under SCORES 2.0, the timeline for the entity to resolve the complaint is 21 calendar days, reduced from the earlier 30. Complaints are auto-routed to the relevant entity and auto-escalated if unresolved within the period.
6. **Seek a first-level review** from the designated body within 15 days of receiving the entity's action-taken report, if you are not satisfied with it.
7. **Seek a second-level review** from SEBI within 15 days of receiving the designated body's action-taken report, if you remain dissatisfied.
8. **Consider online dispute resolution** if the matter is still unresolved, as described below.

None of that is difficult. What defeats complaints is not the form; it is the evidence and the drafting behind it.

## The documents that make a complaint actionable

A complaint works when a third party can verify it from paper. Assemble, in chronological order:

- **A written chronology.** One page, dated entries, no narrative flourishes. The reader should be able to follow it in a minute.
- **Account and holding records.** Statements, folios, holdings and transaction ledgers covering the period, with the disputed entries highlighted.
- **Contract notes and confirmations.** The trade and transfer documents that show what was executed, on whose instruction and when.
- **Payment evidence.** Bank statements, receipts and transaction references connecting your money to the counterparty.
- **The firm's own communications.** Emails, letters, chat transcripts and, critically, its complaint acknowledgement and any response, however unsatisfactory.
- **Proof of the first approach.** The dated written complaint you sent to the firm, and evidence it was received.
- **Onboarding and advisory documents.** KYC forms, risk disclosures, suitability records and any advice given, which establish what was represented to you.
- **A specific remedy.** Vague requests cannot be granted even by a willing respondent.

Read that list as the answer to a single question: *if a stranger wanted to check my account of events, what would they need?* Every document that shortens that stranger's work improves the odds. Keep the drafting discipline too: one issue per complaint, dates and amounts rather than adjectives, and adherence to what you can actually evidence.

## What SCORES can and cannot achieve

**What it can do:** ensure the complaint reaches the entity and is tracked; require the entity to resolve it and file an action-taken report within 21 calendar days; escalate an unresolved complaint through first-level and then second-level review; and create a record that feeds supervision and, where warranted, enforcement.

**What it cannot do:** decide a contested question of fact with finality, award damages, or recover money from a firm that has none. An enforcement order is a statement of findings; the money it may require an entity to disgorge is a separate question, and enforcing it against an insolvent counterparty is a further problem still. Where a firm has collapsed, the securities redress system gives way to the insolvency framework, and the ranking of creditors determines what is left for anyone.

The honest expectation is therefore layered. The portal is good at getting an answer and creating accountability. It is not a substitute for a court, and even a court's judgment is only as good as the defendant's ability to pay.

## When the counterparty is not registered: a different map

For complaints outside the regulated perimeter, the forum changes. The table below is a starting map, not legal advice:

| The problem | The usual forum |
| --- | --- |
| Service or conduct failure by a listed company or registered intermediary | SEBI SCORES, then review, then online dispute resolution |
| An unregistered scheme, unregistered adviser or guaranteed-return pitch | Regulator alerts and the enforcement route, alongside the police |
| Information that securities law has been violated | SEBI's market-intelligence portal |
| Cheating, criminal breach of trust, organised fraud | The police and the economic offences wings |
| A bank, deposit or non-bank lending complaint | The banking ombudsman mechanism |
| Mis-selling or deficient service in a consumer purchase | The consumer commissions |
| A failed counterparty and disputed recovery | The insolvency tribunals |

Two lessons sit under that table. First, **registration status decides your route** — which is why checking it before handing over money, not after, is the single highest-value habit in this series. Second, **routes are not exclusive**. A criminal complaint and a consumer claim can run alongside each other, and a regulator's enforcement action can proceed independently of your own claim. Suspected insider trading, price manipulation or front-running is a market-integrity matter rather than a personal grievance; route it through the market-intelligence channel and preserve the evidence — screenshots, timestamps, order records — that makes it checkable.

## From SCORES to Online Dispute Resolution

If the SCORES route does not produce a satisfactory outcome, SEBI's framework provides a further step: the SMART (Securities Market Approach for Resolution Through) Online Dispute Resolution mechanism, accessed through the SMART ODR portal. It is mandated for regulated entities with a largely direct individual-investor interface — stock brokers, depository participants, mutual fund asset management companies, registrar and transfer agents, investment advisers, research analysts, portfolio managers, merchant bankers, and the managers of alternative investment funds, REITs and InvITs, among others.

The process has three stages:

1. **Pre-conciliation.** The portal contacts both parties to gather information and attempt an amicable resolution. This stage is free for both the investor and the entity.
2. **Conciliation.** If pre-conciliation fails, neutral conciliators facilitate discussion through an ODR institution empanelled by the market infrastructure institutions. The conciliation fee is borne by the market participant: SEBI's published structure is a higher amount where conciliation succeeds and a lower amount where it does not, plus applicable taxes, while the investor pays nothing at this stage.
3. **Arbitration.** If conciliation fails, arbitration can be initiated, with fees that depend on the value of the claim and are shared between the parties. The arbitrator's decision is binding on both parties.

The governing framework is set out in SEBI's master circular on online dispute resolution dated 31 July 2023. As with SCORES, the prerequisite is that the investor has first taken the complaint up with the intermediary.

## Worked example: assembling an evidence bundle

This is an illustrative reconstruction, not a real dispute.

An investor is sold a product over the phone with an assurance she now believes was false, and the money has not come back. What turns this from a grievance into a file?

- **The onboarding record**, which shows what was disclosed and what was not.
- **The call or message record**, which captures the assurance in the seller's own words.
- **The payment trail**, from her bank to the counterparty, with dates and references.
- **The product documents**, which state what the product actually was, as against what was described.
- **The firm's first response**, which either concedes the facts or contradicts them.
- **Her dated written complaint** to the firm, and the acknowledgement that proves it arrived.

Then a one-page chronology ties the six together, ending in a single requested remedy. The complaint now has a shape an administrator can process, and it can be lodged on SCORES within a year of the cause of action. The same grievance, described in three frustrated paragraphs without attachments, has almost none.

Notice that nothing in the list requires legal skill. It requires having kept things and having written them down. That is the part within every investor's control, and it is why the earlier articles in this series emphasise documentation before a problem arises rather than after.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes a process, not a statute. It does not reproduce every complaint category, the evidence thresholds, or the penalties that may follow enforcement, because those are set in the current procedure and the law and change over time. It does not tell you whether your specific dispute is admissible, does not evaluate any firm, and does not guarantee any outcome. And it does not pretend that filing a complaint is a recovery strategy: it is a step toward accountability, taken with clear eyes about what accountability can and cannot deliver.

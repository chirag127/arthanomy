The Cooperative Bank Deposits article opens with a number that everyone has memorised and almost no one has analysed: five lakh rupees of deposit insurance per depositor per bank. The figure is the same whether the bank is a large private lender, a small finance bank licensed recently, or a cooperative bank with a century of local history. That sameness is the most important fact in Indian retail deposit structure, because it means that the only thing that differentiates the return on a bank deposit from its risk is everything above the insurance line. A cooperative bank offering one or two percentage points more than a large bank is paying for its money, and the saver who takes the offer is supplying that money. The exercise is to understand what is being priced and whether the price is adequate.

## Explain simply

A cooperative bank is a bank run under cooperative law, usually with local or community ownership and a mandate to serve its members. It is licensed and supervised by the central bank in the ways that matter for a depositor, and it sits inside the deposit-insurance scheme alongside commercial banks. The deposit is protected up to five lakh rupees per depositor per bank. Everything above that is an ordinary claim in an insolvency. The extra rate you are offered is paying for the risk and the inconvenience above the line, for weaker funding, and sometimes for weaker governance. Some of that compensation is genuine and fair. Some of it is simply the price of funding a bank that other sources of money will not fund. The difference is revealed by reading the bank, not the rate.

## What a cooperative bank is and how it differs

Cooperative banks in India are banks organised on cooperative principles, serving members and borrowers in a defined area or sector. They were built to bring banking to communities and to small borrowers that commercial banks might not serve, and in many regions they are the only banking presence. Their ownership is mutual rather than shareholder-based, and their governance is a mix of elected boards and professional management.

That structure cuts both ways. A local base gives deep knowledge of the borrower and strong community accountability. It also concentrates decision-making in small boards, concentrates exposure in local economies and single sectors, and has historically produced governance failures in a minority of institutions. The central bank's supervisory role exists precisely because a cooperative structure does not by itself produce prudential behaviour. Supervision can be exercised through registered amalgamations, supersession of boards, and resolution of weak banks, but the history of cooperative-bank distress in India shows that these powers are often exercised after the weakness has accumulated rather than before.

A small finance bank is a different animal: a recently licensed bank with a mandate to serve small borrowers and unbanked segments, subject to full bank regulation from inception. A payments bank is more restricted still, taking deposits and providing payments but not lending, and required to deploy deposits in safe and liquid assets. All three categories, when they are banks, sit inside the deposit-insurance scheme, and all three face the same five-lakh floor on protection. The licence type is a starting fact, not a verdict on safety. The relevant question is always how the specific bank is run, and that answer has to be read rather than assumed from its category.

## Deposit insurance: what five lakh actually means

The deposit-insurance scheme covers deposits with insured banks up to a statutory limit that functions, in depositor-facing material, as five lakh rupees per depositor per bank. Cover is applied to principal and interest together, and it is calculated by aggregating the depositor's deposits in that bank rather than by counting accounts, branches or products. The aggregation is done by right and capacity, which is the principle that decides how joint and single accounts, accounts held as trustee, and accounts held as guardian for a minor are attributed.

The most common error in deposit structuring is to assume that every account multiplies cover. A saver who places two fixed deposits in the same name at the same bank holds one depositor's worth of cover, not two. A second error is to treat the interest that accrues as comfortably inside the limit when, over a long tenor at an attractive rate, the accumulated balance can push past it. The insurance applies to the total at the moment the bank fails, principal and interest together.

The scheme does not cover every kind of obligation. Deposits of certain entities and categories are excluded in whole or in part, and the boundary between a covered and an uncovered claim is stated in the insurer's own disclosure, which is where it has to be checked. Cash held in a bank's locker is not a deposit and is not insured at all, and most safe-keeping arrangements are outside the scheme. A depositor who has not read the exclusion list cannot honestly say that they know their own cover.

Above the limit the depositor is an unsecured creditor of the bank. That is not a theoretical status. It determines the recovery available in any insolvency, the priority of the claim relative to secured creditors and to the deposit insurer's subrogated claim, and the time it takes. The limit is a floor on loss, not a remedy that erases it.

## What happens when a bank fails

Bank failure in India is resolved through a mix of supervisory action, reconstruction, and in extreme cases moratorium and amalgamation. The experience of a depositor is usually a sequence: a period of restricted access while the regulator intervenes, a resolution that transfers the deposits to another bank or reconstructs the institution, and a settlement of insured deposits in parallel. Depositors with balances above the limit depend on the resolution and on the residual value of the failed bank's assets, which is typically unknown for months or years.

Two points matter more than the mechanics. First, insurance pays the depositor; it does not restore the convenience of banking. A frozen account, an interrupted business, and a family liquidity squeeze are real costs that the eventual repayment does not reverse. Second, the time between failure and insured repayment is itself a cost, and it falls unevenly. A depositor who depends on the interest for living expenses experiences the failure differently from one who does not. The deposit size that a saver can afford to have trapped at a weak institution is therefore smaller than the insured limit, and institution selection matters even when individual deposits are inside the scheme.

The exact settlement process and its timelines are described by the insurer and can change with regulation. This article does not reproduce them. A saver who depends on the figure should read the insurer's current disclosure before relying on it, especially for less common categories of deposit or for deposits held as trustee or guardian.

## Governance and the cooperative-bank supervision problem

Cooperative banks are supervised, but their features create recurring difficulties. Three are relevant to a depositor.

Concentrated local exposure. A bank whose borrowers and depositors are drawn from the same small region is a correlated portfolio, and a regional shock impairs both sides of the balance sheet at once. Commercial banks with national footprints do not have this property to the same degree.

Close-knit boards. An elected board that is also composed of borrowers is a conflict-of-interest structure. It does not guarantee bad outcomes, and it does not require a bad outcome in any given bank, but it is the main reason cooperative-bank fraud headlines recur.

Delayed recognition. Cooperative banks have historically been weaker than commercial banks at recognising losses promptly and at writing them down. A bank that recognises late has, in the meantime, been paying depositors from a capital position weaker than the statements suggested.

The central bank's supervisory toolkit has been strengthened for this category over time, and cooperative banks are more closely supervised than they were. That is genuine progress, and it must be weighed against the fact that supervision is backward-looking at its best and that the weaker institutions were already weak when the rules tightened. A depositor should not invest in the quality of regulation as if it were a guarantee.

## Small finance banks and payments banks: newer, different, same floor

Small finance banks are licensed to convert from microfinance or other local origins into fully regulated banks, and their deposits are insured in the same scheme and on the same terms. Their strength is an explicit small-borrower mandate and a regulator that applied bank standards from the start. Their recurring challenge is the credit risk of a loan book built on small, often unsecured, exposures to borrowers with limited collateral and cyclically variable incomes. Asset quality in that segment can deteriorate quickly in macro stress, which is one reason these banks carry capital buffers and charge a premium for deposits above large-bank levels.

Payments banks take deposits but do not lend, and they must put most of the deposits in safe, liquid assets. Their credit risk on the asset side is low by design. Their business-model risk is rather a revenue question, and their deposit products are often shaped by channel and technology partnerships rather than by a branch network. The deposit-insurance limit is the same. A depositor's choice among these categories is therefore not a choice between higher and lower insurance but between different operating and credit profiles that the insurance does not alter.

## The extra yield as a credit spread

The spread between a cooperative or small finance bank's deposit rate and the large-bank rate is a credit spread in everything but name. It is the market price of funding that institution against the alternative of funding a bank that the market considers safer. Like any credit spread, it compensates for several distinct things at once.

Expected loss. If the weaker bank defaults more often or recovers less, the spread is partly compensation for that expected loss. Recovery rates and default probabilities in this segment are published in the central bank's financial stability material, and any expectation should be anchored there rather than inferred from the headline.

Information asymmetry. Smaller banks disclose less, and depositors have less capacity to assess them. Part of the spread is the price of that information gap, which is not a compensation for risk so much as a compensation for the trouble of measuring it.

Liquidity and access. Smaller banks can be harder to transact with, slower to service, and more geographically concentrated. A rate premium can reflect friction rather than risk.

Regulatory burden. Smaller banks bear proportionally heavier compliance costs, and they may price deposits to cover them. That is a real cost, but it is not default risk.

Splitting the spread into these parts is the discipline. A spread that mostly reflects expected loss is fair payment for risk. A spread that mostly reflects information asymmetry and friction is being sold at a price the buyer cannot verify. The depositor cannot do this split with precision, but can usually form a judgement by reading the bank's own annual report and asset-quality disclosures.

## Structuring deposits across institutions: an illustrative exercise

Consider a saver with fifteen lakh rupees of deposits and a five-lakh insurance limit, an illustrative case used to show the arithmetic rather than quote an actual scheme. Three structures are possible.

The first is to hold all fifteen lakh in one bank. The protected share is five lakh and ten lakh sit above the line. The single-account convenience is high. The estate is exposed to that institution through the full balance.

The second is to split fifteen lakh as five lakh at each of three independent banks. The protected share, on the assumption that each holding is in the same right and capacity for the same depositor, is the whole fifteen lakh, with no amount above the line at any single bank. The protection gain is real, and it costs nothing in rate terms if all three banks pay the same. Against it are the operational cost of three relationships, three sets of paperwork, three sets of reinvestment decisions at maturity, and the possibility that all three, if they are in the same peer group, stress together in a regional or sectoral crisis.

The third is to hold the deposits in joint names, which requires care. Joint attribution depends on the capacity in which the funds are held and on the combinations of holders at the same bank. A second name can create more than one insured entitlement only where the holders genuinely fund the deposit in separate capacity, and the rules on this are precise. Assumed multiplication of cover by adding names is a common and expensive error.

$$ \text{Premium forgone by splitting} = \text{deposit amount} \times \text{rate differential} \times \text{time without splitting} $$

The expression above is trivial algebra, but it frames the real question. If the weaker bank paid half a percentage point more and the saver instead split the money across three banks at the lower rate, the forgone premium over a year on ten lakh rupees is roughly five thousand rupees, which is the price of the insurance above the line. That trade is not obviously irrational either way. It is a number that belongs in the decision rather than an afterthought.

The arithmetic assumes independent institutions. If the three banks share an owner, a sector or a geography, the risk reduction is smaller than the arithmetic suggests, and the structures that matter are not the accounts but the banks themselves.

## What depositors can and cannot control

The saver controls the amount per institution, the names and capacities in which deposits are held, the tenor and renewal, the diversification of counterparties, and the attention paid to early warning. The saver does not control the supervisor's timetable, the resolution in a stress event, the recovery on the uninsured portion, or the macro conditions that stress small banks as a group. Recognising that boundary is what keeps deposit structuring honest. Moving money to a higher rate does not create safety, and moving it away from a weak bank does not guarantee there will not be another weak bank next.

## A thirty-minute annual-report drill for a bank

A small bank's annual report is not a novel, but it is long, and a saver who tries to read it cover to cover will miss the important lines. The reading order below gets to the substance in half an hour, and it is the right order because each item is disqualifying on its own.

First, the auditor's report and its opinion. A qualified opinion, an emphasis of matter, or a change of auditor should stop the decision regardless of the rest of the document. Unqualified with emphasis on a specific matter deserves a slower reread of that matter.

Second, the asset-quality table. Gross and net non-performing assets, restructured advances as a proportion of total advances, and the movement in provisions. Rising slippage while reported profits look comfortable is the classic cooperative-bank pattern, because provisioning that lags reality flatters earnings for a while.

Third, the concentration of exposure. The top ten borrower exposures relative to capital, and the sectoral and geographic distribution of advances. A small bank with a heavy concentration in a single district, industry or borrower group is not being cautious, however friendly its credit culture looks.

Fourth, the liability side. The deposit base composition, the cost of deposits, and reliance on bulk deposits or borrowings rather than retail. A bank whose funding is concentrated, or which has been buying deposits at rates well above peers, is a bank under funding stress even if it says otherwise.

Fifth, capital. The capital ratios as disclosed, and the trend. A capital cushion that is thin and shrinking is not compatible with sustained higher deposit rates, because earnings have to fund both the capital build and the spread.

Sixth, governance disclosures. Board composition, the presence of independent voices, the number of committees, and whether the annual general meeting was held. These are boring signals, and that is why they work: governance is most visible when it is absent.

Last, the notes to the accounts. Contingent liabilities, related-party transactions, and off-balance-sheet exposures sit there, and they are the lines that the main statements do not announce.

If any three of these eight show movement in the wrong direction over two years, the correct response is not to wait for a better price. It is to look elsewhere.

## The checklist before opening an account

1. Confirm the bank's membership of the deposit-insurance scheme from the insurer's published disclosures rather than from the branch.
2. Read the bank's latest annual report and asset-quality summary, and note the trend over three years rather than the latest year.
3. Look at the concentration of advances by sector and geography, and note whether deposits are also concentrated in the same region.
4. Compute the amount that would be above the insurance line in your intended name and identify the largest joint holders.
5. Check the range of penalties for premature withdrawal and the conditions under which interest is recomputed.
6. Test the operational channel: nominal fixed-deposit statement, interest credit timing, premature-closure process, and complaint escalation.
7. Place the deposits so that no single institution holds more than you are willing to have frozen or restructured, regardless of the insurance line.
8. Re-check the bank's condition and the insurance position before every renewal, because both change.

Done this way, deposit insurance is a floor properly used: it makes tail loss bounded, it does not make small banks identical to large ones, and it does not remove the need to read the institution that holds the money.

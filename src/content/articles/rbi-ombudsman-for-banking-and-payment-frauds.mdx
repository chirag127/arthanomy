A fraud victim quickly discovers that payment routes, banks and regulators each point to a different door, and several of the doors only open after a date has passed or a period has elapsed. The Reserve Bank's integrated ombudsman scheme is one of the more important doors, and it is the one that most often gets used late, because victims assume that complaining to the bank and escalating are the same act. They are not. This article sets out who the scheme covers, what has to happen before a complaint can be filed, in what order and by when, how the complaint itself is made, what can and cannot be awarded, and where it sits alongside every other route available.

## Explain simply

If your problem is with a bank, an NBFC or a payments business that the scheme covers, the Reserve Bank's ombudsman is the free escalation channel. It is not the first step. You must first complain to the business, in writing, before the relevant time limit runs out. Then you wait at least 30 days, or the longer period that the rules or your specific regulator require. Only if the answer is unsatisfactory, or there is no answer at all, can you file with the ombudsman, and only within 90 days of that point. Filing has no fee, there is no cap on how much money the complaint can be about, but the extra money the ombudsman can give you for consequential loss and for the trouble is capped at published amounts. The helpline number is only for questions; it cannot take the complaint. And remember that the award can be appealed by either side, within 30 days.

## Who is covered, and who is not

The scheme exists to resolve complaints against specific kinds of regulated entity, and the coverage test sets out with the entity and its status rather than with the subject of the complaint.

Covered entities include banks, including co-operative banks with deposits of Rs 50 crore and above; certain NBFCs with an asset size of Rs 100 crore and above, or that accept deposits; non-bank issuers of prepaid payment instruments; and credit information companies. Major exclusions include housing finance companies and core investment companies, together with a number of other categories that the scheme spells out. If the entity you are dealing with is not covered, the scheme cannot hear the complaint against it, and the effort spent filling the form is lost.

Coverage of a fraud by this scheme also depends on what actually happened, not merely on which entities were involved. The scheme is about the conduct of the entity towards a complainant. Where a private fraudster used an account at a bank to receive stolen funds but the bank itself did nothing wrong and followed instructions, that is a fraud case that runs through the bank and through the payment system rather than through this scheme. The bank complaint route, however, can still be used to report the misuse of the account, to ask for a lien or marker, or to preserve the trail. Confusing the two situations leads to a rejected complaint and a lost month.

A realistic classification in the first hour matters more than the quality of the form. If the organisation you are dealing with is a bank or NBFC, or the payments problem involves one, plan the entity-first sequence. If it is a securities market problem, a consumer matter, or an unregulated scheme, this route may apply only tangentially, and perhaps not at all.

## What kinds of complaint are admissible

A complaint has to fall within the categories the scheme defines and must not be excluded. Matters outside the scheme, matters previously handled, or fully settled by agreement, or already decided and subject to pending appeal in court, fall outside it. Critically, the complaint must not relate to a matter that a civil court has already decided, or that the complainant has already taken to another ombudsman or forum for the same grievance. Parallel fora are available in general, but the same matter should not be pursued in two places at once without taking advice.

Two further exclusions matter for fraud victims. Complaints that are vague, unsupported or factually implausible are weeded out; the regulator is explicit that frivolous complaints are not entertained. And complaints that have already been decided, and are the subject of an estoppel, are out.

In practice, most fraud-related bank complaints fail or succeed on the same questions: whether the entity followed its own protocols, whether the complaint timeline was met, and whether the evidence shows a breach of the entity's obligation to the customer rather than merely the fact that a fraudster got access to the victim's money.

## Step one: complain to the entity first

The scheme is built on the principle that the entity itself must first have the opportunity to resolve the matter. The sequence is as follows.

The complaint must be made to the entity in writing, through the entity's published complaint channel, before the limitation period under the Limitation Act, 1963 expires. A complaint made by email directly to a branch relationship manager is not how one proves a complaint, because later it may be disputed. It is worth being methodical: a written complaint, sent to the nodal office or the principal grievance officer, with a copy to the customer's own email, stating dates, amounts and the remedy sought, with the evidence attached, and an acknowledgement sought.

If the entity's reply is unsatisfactory, or if no reply is received within 30 days from the date of the complaint or such higher period, up to 60 days, as the RBI, NPCI, card network or other applicable guidelines may specify, then the escalation window to the ombudsman opens.

If a proposal for resolution or compensation is accepted by the complainant, the matter is closed. Acceptance is binding, so a verbal or informal agreement in the moment should be avoided when an appeal or escalation may be needed.

Where the entity rejects the complaint, it should state reasons. A rejection without reasons is a weaker position for the entity and can itself be grounds for the ombudsman to intervene, so obtaining and retaining the rejection in writing matters for the escalation.

## Step two: the 90-day escalation window

The escalation window is the single most-often-missed step in the entire scheme. After the wait, the complainant must file with the ombudsman within 90 days of the date the waiting period ends, or of the date of the last communication received from the entity, whichever is later. A filing made on day 91, or on day 90 through a channel that does not create a record, is outside the scheme and cannot be brought back in.

Sufficient cause, in the scheme's terms, does not include a late filing arising simply because the reason slipped from view. The 90-day period, combined with the 30-day wait, implies a real outside limit that victims in shock are unlikely to remember without a check, and it is the reason an escalation diary note dated on the day the complaint is made is worth keeping. Write the escalation rule and the computed date in that complaint, before anything else is forgotten.

The limitation period under the Limitation Act, 1963 is set as a gatekeeping rule for the matters concerned; the scheme specifies that the complaint must be made to the entity before that period expires.

## How to file

The portal is cms.rbi.org.in, the centralised portal operated by the Reserve Bank for registering complaints. Paper complaints may also be submitted through the Centralised Receipt and Processing Centre. Complaints are not accepted by telephone. The helpline 14448 is for information only: it should not be used to file a complaint, and it should not be treated as evidence that a complaint has been filed.

The complaint should follow the entity's grievance case number and refer to it; the evidence should be attached in a structured way, because the ombudsman decides on the record and a complaint whose chronology is visible in the first page is much easier to process. Gaps and unlabelled exhibits are a common reason for delay.

The essentials of a strong complaint are specific. Complainant details and the entity details, matching the account records exactly. The grievance in two or three sentences, no emotions, plain figures. The entity first-complaint date and a copy of it. The entity's reply or, if none, a statement of non-receipt after the required period. The evidence bundle, in date order, with a contents page at the front. The relief sought, stated in monetary terms where possible, and clearly linked to specific misconduct. An undertaking that the same matter is not pending elsewhere.

The portal issues a complaint number. Retain it, together with the filing acknowledgement, in the same folder as the original complaint and all the supporting records.

## Who can also file

Complaints may be filed through authorised representatives, including regulated intermediaries, bank employees in a fiduciary capacity, and court-appointed receivers, subject to the scheme's own conditions. Power of attorney holders may file where properly authorised. Third-party agents who take a fee to file are not required by the scheme, and any arrangement to pay one should be assessed against the same rules that apply to all other advance fees.

## What the ombudsman can and cannot do

The ombudsman investigates and can issue an award directing the entity to take specific action. The substantive scope includes requirements to pay compensation for loss arising from the act or omission, and to make redress for the specific service failure.

Compensation for consequential loss arising from the deficiency in service is capped at Rs 30 lakh, and compensation for loss of time, expenses and harassment is capped at Rs 3 lakh, per the published ceilings. These limits apply to the total compensation of that type that may be awarded, even where the original exposure or the actual loss is larger, and they may be revised by the regulator from time to time.

The ombudsman is not a court of last resort. Certain matters are excluded from the scheme, including matters where the conduct is under criminal investigation, matters already finally decided by a civil court for the same transaction, and matters that do not fit the defined categories. The ombudsman may not award exemplary or punitive damages, and cannot order the entity to act outside its regulatory powers or beyond its jurisdiction. The scheme complements and does not displace civil remedies.

Where the amount in dispute exceeds the ceiling that the ombudsman can award, or where the claim raises questions of law that a civil court or a consumer commission is better placed to decide, or where the entity is not covered, the scheme is the wrong tool and the appropriate forum has to be chosen.

If the award is accepted within the period provided, the entity implements it and the matter concludes. If the award is rejected, the scheme allows an appeal pathway rather than a fresh complaint.

## The appeal

Either party may appeal to the Appellate Authority against the award within 30 days of its receipt, extendable by up to a further 30 days for sufficient cause. This is the same window for both sides, so a complainant should not assume that a disappointing award is simply final, nor that a favourable one is secure.

The appeal grounds and the Authority's powers are set out in the scheme and the appeal should be filed in writing, with the full record attached, and should be limited to the errors in the award. Compliance with or challenge to an award becomes longer and more formal once it reaches this stage, at which point licensed professional representation is appropriate.

The scheme's design at this stage is to correct error in the application of the scheme's own rules, not to relitigate facts freely. Appeals that raise new matters not previously presented are generally not entertained.

## Worked example: filing within the 90 days

An illustrative scenario, based only on the mechanics set out above. All details are illustrative.

A customer complains to a bank on 1 August about an unauthorised UPI withdrawal that the bank refuses to reverse. The bank replies unsatisfactorily on 5 September. The waiting period of 30 days from the complaint had in fact ended on 31 August, but the reply arriving on 5 September becomes the last communication. Depending on the specific guideline period applicable and the date the waiting period actually ends, the 90-day escalation window runs to early December. Filing the complaint on the portal in late November leaves time to spare; filing in late December removes it from the scheme entirely and leaves only the civil route.

Where the bank receives the complaint and does not answer at all, the waiting period may run to 60 days if the higher period set by applicable guidelines applies. The same outer 90-day logic applies from the end of that wait. Uncertainty about which period applies requires a note in the complaint record, and where possible a written confirmation from the entity.

The illustrative point is the deadline itself: the 90 days is measured from the point the first-complaint phase ends, not from the date of the fraud. Two different events, one early and one late, define the window, and confusing them is the most common way to miss it. The choice of whichever is later gives some protection but does not abolish the risk.

## Other routes that run in parallel

The banking route is rarely the only one. Where a fraud has a securities element — an unregistered adviser, a guaranteed-return scheme or a pooling scheme — SEBI's alert and complaint routes are the appropriate channel. Where a misleading representation caused the loss to a consumer, a consumer commission claim runs in parallel. Where the sums involved are large, or where the loss is hard to prove on a service-level record, a civil suit may be the practical route despite the cost. And where the conduct is criminal — cheating, criminal breach of trust, forgery under the provisions in force since July 2024 — a police or Economic Offences Wing complaint registers the matter and may result in a freeze, which is the only step that reliably improves the recovery position.

A classification decision made in the first hour shapes which of these routes is open. Doing both routes is common and, in India, frequently the most complete approach: the ombudsman route for the bank conduct, and the criminal and regulator routes for the operator.

The interaction matters because a complainant cannot pursue the same matter in two fora simultaneously without cost. Accepting a fresh award in the ombudsman route where the matter is also a pending civil matter requires disclosure. It is sensible to ask, before filing, which channel is most likely to deliver the most usable relief, and to commit to one where the rules require exclusivity.

## Record-keeping as a practical discipline

The single factor that most separates the complaints that succeed from those that do not is the complainant's own record. Three habits make a material difference and cost very little.

The first is a dedicated folder for the matter, created on the day of the fraud, containing every message, screenshot, receipt, reference number and statement that touches the case, in date order, with a note of what happened on each date. The folder is the evidence bundle; nothing should be thrown away, including screenshots of messages that later disappeared, which are admissible as evidence of what was said.

The second is a dated diary note at the end of each day on which anything relevant happens, recording the contact made, the response received, the promise given and the deadline set. The diary note converts memory into a record, and memory is the thing that fails first under stress.

The third is the practice of confirming by writing what was agreed verbally. Any phone or chat promise from a bank officer should be followed by an email restating it and asking for confirmation. If no confirmation arrives, the email is still evidence that the representation was made and that there was no response.

These habits are unglamorous and they are the difference between a complaint that the ombudsman can decide and one that cannot be decided on its facts. They are worth more than any legal letter, and they cost nothing.

## Choosing among overlapping remedies

It is worth restating the choice question, because victims rarely have time to deliberate. The ombudsman route is the right one where the entity is within coverage and the deficiency is in a service the entity owed. The consumer commission is the right one where the loss arose from a misleading representation, a mis-sale or an unfair trade practice, and where the quantification of loss is permitted. The criminal route is the right one where the conduct is criminal, particularly where a freeze or a search is needed to preserve funds. The civil route is the right one where the entity has assets and a contractual or tortious claim exists and the amount is large enough to justify the cost. In many fraud cases all four apply to some extent, and the choice is then a question of which route delivers the most usable relief at an acceptable cost, with sequencing that does not foreclose the others unnecessarily.

Where the entity is not covered by the ombudsman scheme, the escalation element simply does not apply, and that determination should be made first, with a phone call or a published list check, before accumulating time inside a process the scheme cannot hear.

## Worked example, revisited: running the deadline diary

An illustrative chronology, using only the mechanics set out above.

- Day 0, 5 August: fraud discovered, first written complaint filed with the bank's nodal officer; diary note placed in the folder with the computation rule attached. Escalation earliest date: 4 September, or 60 days if the higher period applies. Escalation latest date: 3 December or 2 January on the latter calculation.
- 18 August: bank calls to collect more documents; email sent same day restating the call and requesting confirmation; confirmation received.
- 31 August: bank replies rejecting the complaint in three lines without reasoning; written follow-up sent asking for reasons.
- 5 September: follow-up unanswered; escalation window has opened under the 30-day rule.
- 1 October: complaint filed on the portal with the evidence bundle; number issued; folder updated.

The discipline that matters is that each of these dates was recorded on the day it happened rather than reconstructed later, and the rule computation was done in advance. A complaint whose record is built in arrears reads differently, and under cross-examination — which a civil claim may involve — reconstructed chronologies fail.

## What this does not tell you

This article does not tell you how to complain to your specific entity effectively on the facts — that depends on the entity, on your records, and possibly on licensed advice. It does not tell you what compensation you will receive, because that is decided per case within the published limits. It does not tell you whether the scheme will cover the entity in your case, because coverage depends on the entity's current status and category, which change over time. It does not tell you what a particular appellate decision or civil ruling will follow, because those are shaped by the evidence.

It does not replace legal advice. Where the amounts are large and the facts are contested, the cost of a licensed professional early is usually less than the cost of a wrong turn late.

## Open questions

Three questions remain open. When both the banking ombudsman and consumer forums appear to apply, what is the principled way for a victim to decide? Would putting filing routes inside the banking app, next to the transaction that is in dispute, raise the quality of evidence that victims bring? And whether the appeal window is proportionate for complainants who only discover that an award understates their loss after receiving it is a policy question worth asking, and properly the domain of the regulator.

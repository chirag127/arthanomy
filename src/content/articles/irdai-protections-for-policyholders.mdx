Insurance is bought on a promise that will be tested years later, at the worst possible time for the buyer. That asymmetry is the reason the market is regulated in detail. The safeguards around an Indian policy — limits on commission, rules on how benefits must be illustrated, the right to cancel, the treatment of surrender, and the machinery for complaints — exist to narrow the distance between what a policyholder was told and what the contract actually says. They are real and useful. They are also a floor, not a substitute for reading the terms, because regulation sets how a product must be sold and serviced, not whether it is good value.

This article, part of the Insurance and Pensions Decoded series, sets out the main protections a policyholder holds, what each one does, and where each one stops.

## Regulation promises process, not performance

The first thing to fix is what regulation can and cannot do. It can require that a product be filed and approved, that an illustration disclose its guaranteed and non-guaranteed parts, that charges stay within prescribed limits, that the insurer maintain solvency, and that a complaint be heard through a defined channel. What it cannot do is make an insurer's investment performance good or force a declared bonus to materialise. A policyholder who understands this distinction stops expecting regulation to validate the product and starts using it to hold the insurer to the process.

That is why the safeguards are best read as rights to information and rights of remedy. The information rights tell a buyer what is being sold. The remedy rights tell a buyer what can be done when the sold product and the contract disagree. Both matter, and both depend on the buyer acting within the prescribed steps and times.

## Commission and the conflict it represents

When an intermediary sells a policy, the commission is paid out of the insurer's expenses, which are funded from premiums, which come from the policyholder. The commission is therefore not a cost the insurer absorbs on the buyer's behalf; it is a cost the buyer's money pays, over time. Regulation addresses this in two ways: it caps what may be paid, and it prescribes disclosure of how much of the premium goes to expenses in the early years.

The cap disciplines the channel. It limits how much of a policyholder's premium can be diverted to acquisition before any benefit is funded, and it reduces the incentive to sell the product that pays the most rather than the product that fits. It does not remove the conflict, because within the cap the intermediary still earns more on some products than others and more on larger premiums. The practical response for a buyer is to ask what the intermediary earns, and to compare products on their internal economics rather than on the relationship.

Limits of this kind differ by product family. Linked products, which invest in market funds, carry prescribed caps on premium-allocation charges, fund-management charges, policy-administration charges and discontinuance charges, plus a lock-in before surrender or partial withdrawal is allowed. Non-linked savings products carry their own prescribed requirements, including a minimum guaranteed element. The rule that matters to a buyer is that the ceilings are structural: no insurer may charge above them, so a comparison between two products can begin from the fact that both are bounded by the same framework.

## Benefit illustrations: guaranteed versus non-guaranteed

The illustration a seller hands over is the document most likely to be misremembered, because it usually shows a large maturity figure. The regulation's response is to require that the illustration distinguish the guaranteed benefit from the non-guaranteed benefits, typically by showing scenarios rather than a single projected number.

For a traditional with-profits policy, the maturity value is the sum assured plus bonuses. The sum assured is contractual. The bonuses are declared by the insurer from time to time and are not guaranteed; the illustration's projections are assumptions about those bonuses, not promises. For a linked product, the value is the fund value, which depends on the performance of the chosen funds after charges are deducted, and the illustration's assumed growth rates are scenario assumptions rather than forecasts.

The discipline this yields is simple. A policyholder should treat only the guaranteed element as an amount that will be received, and should treat every projection as a scenario that may not occur. Where the guaranteed element is small relative to the projected total, the product is effectively a bet on an insurer's future bonus declarations, and it should be read as such.

## The free-look right

The free-look right allows a policyholder to cancel a policy within a prescribed window after receiving the policy document and to receive a refund of premium subject to specified deductions. Its purpose is to give the buyer a chance to read the actual contract rather than the sales description.

Two features of the right matter. First, the window runs from receipt of the document, not from the date of sale, which is why the date the document is received should be noted. Second, the refund is premium less deductions, not the full premium, because the insurer has already incurred the cost of issuing the policy and providing cover for the intervening period. The exact window and the deductions are prescribed and vary by product type, and they should be read from the policy and the current rules rather than assumed.

The honest reading of the free-look right is that it is a corrective, not a decision tool. A window of days is not enough time to evaluate a decades-long contract from scratch. It helps a buyer who discovers a misdescription; it does not repair a decision made without reading the terms.

## Surrender and discontinuance

If the policy is not cancelled within the free-look window but the policyholder later stops paying or wants to exit, the surrender and discontinuance rules apply. These are prescribed rather than negotiated, and they differ by product family.

For traditional policies, the surrender value generally has a guaranteed component and, after a minimum premium-paying period, a special surrender value, with the formula set out in the policy document. For linked products, the position is the fund value less discontinuance charges after the lock-in, and the discontinuance rules govern what happens when premiums stop before that point, including the treatment of the fund and any revival window.

The economics are deliberately discouraging. Early exit usually destroys a material share of the premiums paid, because the acquisition costs have already been incurred and there is no accumulated value large enough to absorb them. This is not a defect to be worked around; it is the reason cover and savings should never be bought without the intention to fund them as intended. A buyer who might need the money back soon should not put it into a long-tenure contract at all.

## The grievance ladder

When a policyholder and an insurer disagree, the framework provides an escalation path, and using the right stage at the right time is what makes the process work.

1. **The insurer's own grievance-redressal machinery.** A complaint is filed with the insurer, acknowledged, and answered within a prescribed framework, with a grievance-redressal officer responsible for it. Written records — the complaint reference, the dates, the correspondence — matter more here than anywhere else in the process.
2. **The regulator's grievance mechanism.** If the insurer does not resolve the matter, the policyholder can escalate through the channel IRDAI maintains for consumer grievances, which records and routes the complaint.
3. **The Insurance Ombudsman route.** For disputes that fall within its scope and monetary and other eligibility conditions, the ombudsman provides a forum for resolution. The conditions, limits and timelines are prescribed and should be checked against the current rules.

The logic of the ladder is that each stage filters the next, so a policyholder who starts at the top of their own escalation, without the insurer's reference number and the paper trail, tends to be sent back down. Keeping the certificate, the illustration, the policy wording, the correspondence and the payment record together is the cheap habit that makes the later stages usable.

## What regulation does not decide for you

The safeguards narrow the gap between description and contract, but they do not choose the product, size the cover, or decide whether to buy at all. A policy can be fully compliant with every disclosure rule and still be a poor fit for the household buying it. Commission limits reduce the pull of the highest-paying product, but they do not tell a buyer what to compare. The free-look right gives a few days to check the document, but it does not supply the analysis. And the grievance ladder resolves disputes about what was promised, not disputes about what the buyer wished had been promised.

The useful posture is therefore to use the protections as instruments. Read the guaranteed element, ask what the intermediary earns, note the date the document arrives, keep the file, and know the escalation path before it is needed.

## Explain simply

Indian insurance is regulated to make sure the policy you are sold is described honestly and that you have a way to complain if it is not. Intermediaries can only be paid within set commission limits, the illustration must show what is guaranteed separately from what is only projected, and you have a short free-look window to cancel after the document arrives. If you later disagree with the insurer, you complain first to the insurer, then through the regulator's channel, and then possibly to the Insurance Ombudsman. None of this makes a bad product good; it makes the process fair and gives you a route to a remedy when something goes wrong.

## Worked example: reading an illustration

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast. The figures are invented to show the method and are not taken from any policy.

Suppose an illustration for a traditional savings policy shows an illustrative maturity value of ₹20,00,000. It is made up of a guaranteed sum assured of ₹8,00,000 and projected bonuses of ₹12,00,000. The guaranteed element is the ₹8,00,000; the ₹12,00,000 is a scenario about future bonus declarations.

If the insurer declares no bonuses over the term, the maturity value is the guaranteed element alone: ₹8,00,000. The difference between the headline and the guarantee is ₹12,00,000, or 60 per cent of the illustrated total. That is the portion of the figure that depends on an assumption rather than a promise.

The arithmetic is illustrative, but the method is general. Whatever the product, separate the amount that is contractual from the amount that is projected, and judge the product on the contractual part plus a defensible assumption about the rest. A product whose guarantee is a small fraction of its headline is not thereby disqualified, but it should be understood for what it is.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the shape of the protections, not their current parameters. The commission ceilings, the illustration format, the free-look window, the surrender formulas, the discontinuance rules and the ombudsman eligibility limits are all prescribed in the framework and are revised from time to time, so the specific numbers must be read from the current regulations and the policy document rather than from any summary. Where a period or a limit is needed and is not stated here, that is deliberate: it is a figure to verify, not a figure to infer.

The tax treatment of premiums, benefits and surrenders is governed by the Income-tax Act as amended by successive Finance Acts and is not restated here; recent renumbering should be treated as pending verification. This article is educational. It recommends no product, insurer or intermediary, and it promises no outcome.

Most households have a vague sense of whether they are getting ahead. A net-worth tracking system replaces the sense with a monthly number and, more importantly, with the arithmetic that explains how the number changed. The system is deliberately dull: a fixed sheet, a fixed date, a fixed column set, and a routine short enough that it actually happens.

This is the third entry in the Operating a Portfolio series. The first wrote the policy, the second gave every rupee a purpose and a date. This one builds the scoreboard — the household balance sheet — and the small operational habits that keep it from going stale.

## Explain simply

A net-worth statement is a list of everything owned minus everything owed, taken at one date. On its own it is only a number; its value comes from being updated the same way every month, so that the change can be explained by income, spending, interest, tax and investment gains. It does not measure how much cash can be reached quickly, so it is read alongside the emergency reserve. Nor is it a score, because a large net worth locked in property behaves very differently from the same figure held in reachable assets.

## Net worth is a balance sheet, not a score

Net worth is the value of everything owned minus everything owed, at a single date. It is a stock measure, not a flow: it says what has accumulated, not how well the household is doing this month. That distinction matters, because a rising net worth can hide a thin cash position, and a flat net worth can sit on top of healthy habits.

Read on its own, the number also says nothing about purpose. Two households with the same net worth can be in very different positions if one holds it in a property that cannot be reached and the other in assets aligned to near-term goals. That is why the statement is read together with two other measures: the liquidity coverage of the reserve — the months of required outflow the same-day assets cover — and the alignment of holdings to goals, which is the bucketing exercise described in the previous article.

## What counts as an asset, and at what value

A balance sheet is only as good as its valuation rules, and the rule that matters is consistency. The workable approach is to assign each line a valuation basis and never change it without recording the change:

- **Financial assets held at market value** — deposits, mutual fund units, direct equity, bonds, and retirement balances where a value is available. These can be pulled from statements and net asset value records, which is what makes the monthly update cheap.
- **Property at a documented estimate.** Since the value is subjective, the method should be recorded: a purchase price held constant, an index, or a professional estimate refreshed on a schedule.
- **Gold and other physical holdings at quantity times rate**, with the rate and its date recorded.
- **Cash and balances at face value.**

Two exclusions are worth stating because they are the most common sources of inflation. Life insurance cover is a protection contract, not an asset; the sum assured is payable on an event, not available to the household. Expected inheritances and unvested future income are expectations, not holdings. Including either makes the statement cheerful and useless.

## What counts as a liability, including the invisible ones

Liabilities should be listed from lender statements rather than memory: home and vehicle loans, personal loans, education loans, credit-card balances, informal borrowings, and any tax that has accrued but not yet been paid. Accrued tax is easy to forget and is genuinely owed.

Contingent exposures deserve their own line and their own treatment. A guarantee on a relative's loan, a co-signed obligation, or a legal claim is not a liability on the balance sheet until it crystallises, but it is a real exposure that the policy should acknowledge. The honest approach is to record it in a notes column with the amount and the conditions, so it cannot be forgotten at the moment it matters, without pretending it is already owed.

## Why tracking systems decay

Systems fail for operational reasons, and the reasons repeat. The first is that the routine is too long to run frequently, so it is run rarely and then abandoned. The second is that the date is arbitrary, so the comparison between two statements spans an unknown period. The third is stale values: a property carried unchanged for years, or a fund balance typed in from memory. The fourth is multiple accounts with no inventory, so the same holding is counted twice on one occasion and missed on the next. The fifth is that the system lives in one person's head, so it dies with their attention.

Each of these has a cheap fix, and the fixes are the design of the routine below.

## A monthly routine that takes minutes

The routine is short by design. Its discipline is that most of it is automated and the rest is a single sitting on a fixed date.

1. **Fix the date.** Use the last day of the month. Every statement therefore covers the same span, and the change between two statements means something.
2. **Update financial assets from statements.** Deposits, fund values, equity holdings and retirement balances are read from the monthly statements and monthly portfolio disclosures that these providers already publish. This is transcription, not valuation.
3. **Update liabilities from lender statements.** The outstanding principal on each loan is taken from the statement, not estimated.
4. **Apply the bridge** to check that the change is explained before it is accepted.
5. **Check the reserve line.** Note whether the same-day assets still cover the intended number of months of required outflow.
6. **File the statement.** The monthly figures become a series, and the series is more informative than any single month.

Property, gold and other hard-to-value assets are updated quarterly or half-yearly on a noted date, not monthly, and the estimate's source is recorded. Annual validation is a separate, longer sitting described below.

## The template: one sheet, fixed columns

The template should be plain enough to survive changes of software. Six columns are enough:

| Account | Side | Type | Valuation basis | Value | As at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Savings account | Asset | Cash | Face value | as recorded | month end |
| Liquid fund | Asset | Financial | Net asset value | as recorded | month end |
| Retirement account | Asset | Financial | Statement balance | as recorded | month end |
| Residential property | Asset | Real | Documented estimate | as recorded | review date |
| Home loan | Liability | Borrowed | Outstanding principal | from statement | month end |
| Credit card | Liability | Borrowed | Outstanding balance | from statement | month end |

The Account column should correspond to a maintained inventory of every account the household holds, including dormant ones. Securities and fund units are identified at the depository and scheme level, so the inventory should record where each holding actually sits; that is the operational thread that makes the statement checkable against the underlying records rather than against memory. The same inventory underpins nomination and record-keeping, which the series covers separately.

## The bridge: explaining the change between two statements

A net-worth figure that moves without explanation teaches nothing. The bridge decomposes the monthly change into its causes:

$$ \Delta \text{Net worth} = \text{income} - \text{consumption} - \text{interest paid} - \text{tax paid} + \text{investment gains} $$

Two definitions make this correct. Consumption here means spending that is neither debt repayment nor tax. And debt principal repayment appears nowhere in the bridge, because repaying principal reduces cash and reduces the liability by the same amount, leaving net worth unchanged. What genuinely costs the household is the interest, which is subtracted explicitly. Getting this right is what stops the statement from appearing to rise every time a loan instalment is paid.

The bridge is also the diagnostic. If the change does not reconcile, one of the five terms is missing, and the usual candidates are unrecorded spending, an accrued but unpaid obligation, or a valuation that was updated inconsistently between months.

## Annual validation: the things checked once a year

Once a year, the statement is checked against the world rather than against last month. The audit covers: a complete walk-through of every account to remove closed ones and find forgotten ones; reconciliation of each financial balance to a statement; a refreshed property estimate with its method recorded; physical gold weighed or verified against a record; loan balances confirmed with lenders; a review of contingent exposures; and a check that nominations on demat accounts, fund folios and deposits are current, because a balance sheet that is accurate and unclaimable is not much use.

The annual sitting is also where the household asks whether the statement is now measuring something it did not intend to. A large single-employer position, or a single property that dwarfs everything else, will show up here as concentration that the policy should have an opinion about.

## Worked example: a monthly statement and its bridge

The following is an illustrative calculation, not observed data. Suppose a household's net worth at the start of the month is ₹62,00,000. During the month it records income of ₹2,40,000, consumption excluding debt repayment of ₹92,000, loan interest of ₹7,000, tax paid of ₹22,000, and investment gains of ₹35,000. The bridge gives:

$$ \Delta = 2{,}40{,}000 - 92{,}000 - 7{,}000 - 22{,}000 + 35{,}000 = 1{,}54{,}000 $$

so the closing net worth is ₹63,54,000. The home loan instalment paid during the month included ₹38,000 of principal. That ₹38,000 appears nowhere in the bridge, correctly: it reduced the cash balance and the outstanding principal equally, and left net worth unmoved. A system that recorded the whole instalment as an expense would understate the month's progress and, over a repayment schedule, cumulatively misstate the household's position.

Two checks follow from the same sheet. The Emergency Fund Sizing calculator answers whether the same-day assets in the reserve cover the intended months of required outflow. The Rebalancing Planner compares the investment lines against their targets, so that any allocation drift is caught in the same sitting; the Opportunity Cost calculator quantifies what the cash lines give up by staying in the reserve, which is the honest other side of holding liquidity.

## What this does not tell you

A net-worth statement does not measure income adequacy, because a household can go a long time drawing down a large asset base. It does not measure liquidity, because the number includes assets that cannot be reached in a month. It does not measure future obligations, such as a child's education or a pension shortfall, which are claims on the balance sheet that no rule of accounting forces onto it. And it does not measure the quality of what is held: a concentration in a single employer's stock and a diversified portfolio can produce the same headline figure.

It also cannot be more trustworthy than its valuations. A property estimate is an opinion, and two honest households can produce materially different net worths from the same facts by choosing different bases. That is not a flaw to eliminate; it is a reason to record the basis, keep it stable, and care about the trend rather than the level.

## Open questions

- How much does the choice of valuation basis for property change the practical decisions a household makes, as opposed to the headline figure?
- Do monthly updates improve the decisions households make, or only the confidence they feel in the numbers?
- What is the right treatment for contingent liabilities such as guarantees and co-signed loans — noted, partially provisioned, or treated as fully owed?
- Should the reserve be reported as a separate headline alongside net worth, so that liquidity and accumulation are never confused?

The system's value is not in the number it prints. It is in the fact that the number can be explained, in the same way, every month, and in the mismatches it makes visible before they become expensive.

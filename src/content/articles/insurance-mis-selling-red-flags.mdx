Mis-selling is a word that sounds like a single act. It is usually not. It is the accumulated mismatch between the product that was described at the point of sale and the product that arrives in the envelope. The product that arrives is, in insurance, the policy document, and it is the only thing that has legal effect. What was said in the conversation is a promise that can only be corroborated by evidence. The work of detecting mis-selling, and of remedy if it is found, is therefore about documentation rather than about memory.

This is the thirtieth article in the Fraud and Investor Protection series. It is about the structure rather than the individual complaint: what gets distorted, why the product's economics support the distortion, the free-look period as the escape that is cheapest and has an expiry, the grievance ladder, and the evidence that each rung requires. It uses the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India's framework where the product structure is concerned, and the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 where the consumer redressal structure is concerned.

## Why insurance is easy to mis-sell

Insurance enjoys a credibility that ordinary savings products do not, because the state regulates it, the agent is licensed, and the product carries the language of protection, which is a word that closes questions rather than opens them. Three properties of insurance product economics make it unusually easy to mis-sell into.

The first is timing. An insurance premium produces a promise that only matures at death, at a term date, or on a claim that may never happen. The absence of frequent, verifiable feedback means the policyholder is unlikely to learn for years that what was bought does not match what was described.

The second is complexity. A typical savings or unit-linked policy contains a premium allocation charge, a policy administration charge, a fund management charge, a mortality charge, and a surrender or policy discontinuance charge. Each of these reduces the capital at work, and the net result, in contribution-based products, is dominated by them in the early years. None of these numbers is difficult to read; none of them is ever the subject of the sales conversation.

The third is the incentive structure. The intermediary is paid, in most products, for the sale and not for the service, and the commission on a first-year premium is typically a substantial multiple of the renewal commission. The premium that funds the product's economics is the same premium that funds the intermediary's incentive to sell the largest possible policy in the shortest possible time. The regulator's commission and expense-of-management limits, including those applied to unit-linked insurance plans, exist precisely because these incentives do not self-correct.

Mis-selling exploits all three: it delays the feedback, it hides the charges, and it leans on the licence as if it were an endorsement.

## The common distortions

The distortions are recognisable by their shape and do not require a financial degree to spot. They cluster into five types.

1. **Product substitution.** A mutual fund, a public provident fund contribution, a fixed deposit or a retirement account is replaced at the last moment by an endowment or unit-linked policy presented as the same thing with an added benefit. The tell is that the substitute has a larger commitment, a longer term and materially worse liquidity, none of which is volunteered.
2. **Guaranteed return claims.** A return is quoted as if it were assured, when the product either has no guarantee, a guarantee only of the sum assured rather than of any market-linked value, or a guaranteed element that is far smaller than the quoted number. The tell is a single, smooth, large number presented without its component parts.
3. **Illiquidity dressed as stability.** The product's multi-year lock-in and surrender penalties are described as discipline or as a benefit. The tell is a product whose exit cost on a low-yield, early-exit scenario is larger than the policyholder could reasonably absorb.
4. **Cover inflation.** The life cover is set at whatever premium is being paid rather than at the household's need, which in savings products usually produces an under-insured policy at a premium that commits the buyer for decades. The tell is cover treated as an output of premium rather than an input.
5. **Premium inflation.** A small stated objective is used to sell a large premium, often through the device of a premium that will be paid for a short period rather than for life. The tell is a premium that looks affordable for the term quoted and would be unaffordable for the full term.

Each of these is visible from the benefit illustration. The illustration, not the conversation, is the document that matters.

## What the IRDAI frame sets and limits

The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India's product regulations govern the design, pricing, charges, expense-of-management limits, commissions and disclosures of life and general insurance products. The authority requires a benefit illustration for linked and non-linked products, setting out the premium, charges and intended benefits under at least two assumed rates of return, so that the buyer can see the gap between premium paid and benefit received. Insurers are required to publish these on their websites and to file the product before sale.

What the regulator's frame does not do is guarantee that the sales conversation matches the illustration. The regulator defines the product and its maximum charges, and it enforces against insurers and intermediaries who violate the rules. It does not attend the sale. The consequence is that a fully compliant product can still be mis-sold, and frequently is. The buyer's protection lies in reading the document, using the free-look window, and documenting the mismatch.

The expense-of-management and commission limits on unit-linked plans are a genuine consumer protection, but their existence is not a reason to trust the sale. They are a cap on a cost, not a floor on a return.

## The free-look period

The free-look period is the statutory window during which a newly delivered policy can be returned for a refund of premium, less a small deduction. It is the single most valuable protection a policyholder has, because it is the last moment at which the contract can be unwound without adjudication. Its exact length, the deductions permitted and the other conditions are prescribed in the regulation and restated in the policy's terms, and the applicable figures change from time to time, so the current number must be taken from the current document rather than from memory.

The practical advice, which does not depend on a number, is to treat delivery as the start of a countdown rather than as the end of the sale. Actions during the free-look window:

- Read the policy document against what was agreed.
- Check the premium, the sum assured, the term, the premium payment term, the charges and the surrender and paid-up values.
- Compare the benefit illustration that was actually shown with the document that arrived. A different illustration handed over at the sale is a serious red flag.
- If there is a mismatch, return the policy in writing within the window, by a method that produces proof of delivery, and retain a copy.
- Do not let the intermediary talk the policyholder into waiting for an explanation that will arrive after the window has closed.

Once the free-look period has passed, the policy is in force until it is surrendered, lapses or matures, and any remedy has to come through the grievance channels.

## Reading the proposal form and the benefit illustration

These two documents contain almost everything that matters, and they are rarely checked. The proposal form is the foundation on which the underwriting rests: effect dates, health declarations, nominee details, premium and bank mandate. An error here can be used against the claimant at claim time, under the disclosure obligations that apply to insureds. The benefit illustration shows the intended flow of premium, charges and benefit at assumed rates.

A short review list:

- Is the insured's name and date of birth right?
- Is the premium frequency, amount and currency what was agreed?
- Is the term and premium payment term what was agreed?
- Are the nominee details correct and current?
- Does the sum assured match the stated objective, or is it whatever the premium bought?
- Do the charges total, in the first policy year, more than the policyholder understood?
- Are the surrender, paid-up and revival values what was said?
- Does the illustration carry the agent's or distributor's code and signature, if one was shown at sale?

A proposal form signed in blank and filled in later is a fraud, not a mis-understanding, and any variation between the copy held and the copy signed should be reported in writing immediately.

## Red flags that distinguish mis-selling from ordinary disagreement

Borrowed from the regulator's emphasis on disclosure and from the consumer forum's approach to misrepresentation, the following signals carry the most information.

- **A money-back promise attached to an insurance product.** Insurance is a contract of protection plus a savings component where one exists; it does not guarantee principal in the way a deposit does, and a pitch that conflates the two is mis-selling an ordinary superiority.
- **A return quoted as a percentage of a single term figure rather than as a net, annualised figure.** The annualisation convention, the charges and the timing of cash flows change what the number means.
- **A gap between the documented sum assured and the verbally promised amount.** The document governs at claim time.
- **A savings product sold to pay for a liability that is already known and short-term**, such as a car loan or an anticipated education fee, where a short-tenor, low-cost instrument would serve.
- **A signed proposal form that the buyer never read**, or one signed in blank.
- **Any pressure to act before the free-look window or during it**, on the grounds that the offer is time-limited.
- **No written record of the sale, when the practice normally produces one.**

None of these signals is proof of mis-selling on its own. They are filters: each one selects for a structure in which the sales record will not survive scrutiny.

## The grievance ladder

The route for a consumer in India is layered, and each layer requires the previous one to have been used. Each layer also has its own documented scope and timelines, which change and should be taken from the current official source rather than from a summary.

1. **The insurer's own grievance cell.** Complaints are first lodged with the insurer, in writing, with the full set of documents, and the insurer is obliged to respond. The complaint reference from this stage is the foundation for every later escalation. A complaint not made in writing is not a complaint for these purposes.
2. **The regulator's channel.** IRDAI's grievance mechanism is the next route for consumers who do not receive a satisfactory resolution. The scope covers developments in insurance products, services and claims handling against the licensed entity.
3. **The ombudsman route.** For unresolved complaints against an insurer, an insurance ombudsman route exists and coexists with the consumer commission route. The ombudsman scheme has its own limits, including a cap on the value of the complaint as defined by the rules. The cap, the acknowledgement of receipt deadlines and the decision timeline are prescribed in the current scheme and are subject to change.
4. **The consumer commissions.** Under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, retail consumers with grievances, including mis-selling, can approach the district commission, then the state commission on appeal, then the national commission. The three-tier structure mirrors the value of the dispute, and the filings require the same evidence set.
5. **Criminal route where warranted.** Where the facts support offences such as cheating in connection with a fraudulent representation, the criminal law applies independently of the civil and consumer routes. Where the seller was unlicensed or the product was unfiled, SEBI or the regulator's enforcement route may also be relevant.

The ombudsman and the consumer commissions are alternative routes in many cases and must not be used in a way that duplicates proceedings. Which route suits a given case depends on the value, the facts, the evidence and the cost of pursuing it. Legal advice on the specific case is appropriate where the amount or the personal consequences are significant.

## What the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 changes

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules provide remedies to retail investors who were mis-sold a product or misled by a representation, and they structure the district, state and national commission route. The act brought in a regulator, the Central Consumer Protection Authority, with powers over unfair trade practices, and it specifically regulates misleading advertising, which is the closest legal analogue to mis-selling.

The practical consequence is twofold. First, a consumer who has been misled has a remedial structure that does not depend on the regulator's goodwill or on the insurer's internal processes; the commission route is judicial in nature. Second, the standard of a misleading representation is a question of fact based on the actual material and the reasonable view of it, which is why the evidence of what was communicated and in what form is decisive. A claim that the seller said something different is worth very little without a recording or a documented illustration.

## Insurance ombudsman details

The insurance ombudsman scheme is the sector-specific alternate dispute resolution route. Its enabling provisions set out the meaning of the complaint, the kinds of complaints that can be taken up, the timeframe for the insurer's response, the period allowed to the complainant to approach, the procedure, and the award and award-execution framework, including the value cap on complaints that it can entertain. The scheme is administered by the Council for Insurance Ombudsmen under the IRDAI's overarching regulatory framework.

The explicit point for a policyholder is that the ombudsman route is cheaper and faster than litigation but is bounded by the value cap and by the scope of complaints it covers. A high-value matter, or one involving a complex factual dispute, is more likely to belong in a consumer commission or in a civil court. The ombudsman's award is enforceable against the insurer up to the cap, and the decision and its reasons are public, which is a reason that this channel sometimes elicits a settlement that an internal grievance does not.

## Evidence: what to keep and in what order

Every route, regulator, ombudsman or commission, begins and ends with evidence. The set is short and should be kept complete from the first day.

- The proposal form as signed, including any rider.
- The benefit illustration shown or quoted before the sale, if it exists.
- The policy document as received, with the cover note.
- All premium receipts, bank statements and direct-debit mandates.
- All written correspondence with the insurer and the intermediary.
- Any recording of the sales conversation, including messages, meeting links and emails.
- The complaint made to the insurer, in writing, and its acknowledgement and reference number.
- A timeline of the sale and the subsequent events.

Where the policy document differs in any material respect from what was said, and the complaint is made before the free-look window has closed, the policyholder's position is overwhelmingly strong. Outside the window the position is fact-dependent, and the absence of a recording of the sale conversation is usually fatal to a claim that the document says something different to what was said. This is why the evidential habit matters more than any individual red flag.

## Explain simply

Insurance mis-selling is mostly a mismatch between the product that was described and the one that arrives, and the mismatch is biggest where a savings product is sold as if it were an investment. The protection that exists is not a guarantee but a sequence of escape hatches. The free-look period is the first and cheapest, and it has an expiry. After that the remedy runs through the insurer's own grievance desk, then the regulator's channel, and finally the ombudsman or the three-tier consumer commission route, each of which depends on having used the previous one and having kept the evidence. The numbers that matter, the free-look length, the grievance response deadline and the ombudsman value cap, are set in regulation and change; take them from the current official document rather than from anyone's summary, including this one.

## Worked example

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast or an observed case. The numbers are invented to show how charges compound in the early years of a savings-oriented policy.

Suppose a policyholder pays an annual premium of Rs 1,00,000 into a savings product for a term of 20 years. In the first policy year the insurer deducts a premium allocation charge of 6% of the premium, a policy administration charge of Rs 5,000 and a mortality charge of Rs 8,000. The amount actually invested in the fund at the end of the first year is therefore approximately Rs 81,000: Rs 1,00,000 minus Rs 6,000 minus Rs 5,000 minus Rs 8,000, before any fund management charge. Total deductions of Rs 19,000 consume 19% of the first year's premium, which is why the accumulated value at the end of year one is roughly 81% of a year's contribution.

If a buyer believed that the full Rs 1,00,000 was invested and quoted a return of 8% per annum, the actual investment base in the first year was 81% of the assumed base, so the early-year return on premium is far below the quoted figure. Over 20 years the charges reduce but do not disappear, and the shortfall between the premium stream and the accumulated value at the term is, in this illustration, well over a year's premium. The illustration shows why a savings policy presented on a gross return figure is not a comparable product to a low-cost investment product, and why the benefit illustration rather than the verbal quote is the document that carries information.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes structure, process and evidence. It does not provide a step-by-step method for carrying out a mis-sale, and it does not diagnose or advise on any specific policy. No specific free-look period, grievance deadline, value cap or charge structure is asserted as current because these are set in regulation and change; where a figure matters, it should be taken from the current regulation and the insurer's policy wording.

Three gaps are worth naming. First, the article does not estimate how often consumer commissions actually order refunds in mis-selling cases, because outcome data on that point is not part of the sources used. Second, the criminal route is described at the level of offences and institutional routes, not at the level of a complaint template, because a template would need to be a legal document and this is not a legal service. Third, the article does not compare products, because a comparison requires product-level data that is specific to each offer and changes over time.

You place an order on a Friday afternoon and the shares appear on Monday. Nothing went wrong; the settlement cycle counts business days, and the weekend is not one of them. That single sentence resolves most of the confusion that surrounds T+1. The change to a shorter cycle did not alter what ownership is, when a contract binds or how risk is transferred. It altered the length of the interval in which an investor holds a claim rather than an asset, and in doing so it moved funding pressure and cut-off dates closer to the trade.

This is the third article in the Equity Mastery series. The first described how a share travels from an executed order to a demat credit; the second covered order types and execution. This one takes the settlement cycle itself as its subject: what it measures, what the change actually changed, and how to plan cash flow around it. Dates of individual implementation phases are not carried by the source material used here and are flagged for verification rather than asserted.

## What the settlement cycle actually measures

A settlement cycle is a convention about when obligations created by trades must be discharged. The convention is expressed as the number of business days from the trade date, written T plus that number. Under a one-day convention the obligation falls due on the next business day, and under a two-day convention on the second.

Three things are important about what the number does and does not cover.

- **It governs settlement, not execution.** A trade becomes a binding contract when it is matched and executed. The cycle says nothing about that moment and does not make the contract revocable.
- **It runs on business days.** Weekends and exchange holidays do not count as settlement days, so the calendar interval between trade and credit can be considerably longer than the number suggests.
- **It applies to the net obligation.** Clearing aggregates and nets a member's trades in the same security for the same settlement date, and it is the net quantity that moves.

That last point explains an outcome that surprises first-time investors: a purchase and a sale of the same security on the same day net to nothing, so no delivery occurs and the position settles as a cash difference. The cycle only bites on trades that create a delivery obligation.

## What changed, and what did not

Indian equities moved from a two-business-day cycle to a one-business-day cycle, known as T+1, brought in on a phased basis that began with a limited set of securities and expanded to the wider market. The phase dates and the exact coverage at each stage need to be verified against exchange and regulator announcements, because commentary has not always distinguished the announcement of a phase from its commencement.

What genuinely changed is the length of the interval and everything that depends on it.

- **Client funds and securities are held for less time.** The shortened cycle reduced the window in which an intermediary held a client's money and shares in the course of settling, which is the point of the change from a client-asset perspective.
- **Funding must be in place sooner.** A delivery obligation now falls due one business day after the trade, so the cash or the securities supporting it must be available on a shorter notice period.
- **Broker working capital needs fell.** A shorter cycle reduces the amount of capital an intermediary must carry between execution and settlement, which is an efficiency gain that accrues first to the intermediary.
- **Corporate-action cut-offs moved closer to the trade.** A record date determines entitlement, and a shorter cycle compresses the interval between the last date on which a trade can create an entitlement and the record date itself.
- **The interval in which a position can be sold before the original trade has settled narrowed.** Practices that depended on a longer window between execution and delivery became materially less viable.

What did not change is just as important. Ownership is still the demat credit. Novation still interposes the clearing corporation between the two sides, so a specific seller's failure still does not cost the buyer the shares. Liquidity, free float and the investable supply of a security are untouched by the length of the cycle, because they depend on who holds the shares and how many are available to trade, not on how quickly a trade settles. And a shorter cycle does not reduce the risk that a position loses value; it reduces the time for which an unsettled claim exists.

## Explain simply

When you buy a share, the trade is final the moment it executes, but the share only appears in your demat account on the settlement date. Indian equities currently settle one business day after the trade, which is what T+1 means, where the day counted is a business day and not a weekend or an exchange holiday. So a Friday purchase arrives on Monday, and a purchase just before a run of holidays arrives after them. Shortening the cycle from two days to one made the gap smaller: money and shares change hands sooner, brokers hold client assets for less time, and you have to be ready to fund a trade sooner. It did not change what ownership is or make shares safer.

## When the money and the shares move

The sequence on the settlement date is worth stating plainly, because it is what the shortened cycle brought forward.

On the exchange side, sellers deliver the net quantity of each security into the clearing corporation, and the clearing corporation pays out the corresponding quantities to the buyers' participants. On the funds side, buyers pay and sellers receive. The securities move by book entry between depository accounts. Pay-in precedes pay-out, so no buyer is credited with a security the clearing corporation has not received.

For an investor, the practical timeline is this. The broker debits the ledger, and in most arrangements collects the funds, around the trade. The demat credit, which is the transfer of ownership, arrives on the settlement date. A sale runs in the opposite direction: the securities are debited on the settlement date and the sale proceeds become available for withdrawal after that flow completes, subject to the broker's own release policy. The shortened cycle compressed the interval on both legs, so a sale's proceeds and a purchase's shares both arrive sooner than they would have under a two-day convention.

## Why business days are not calendar days

The cycle counts settlement days as defined by the exchange calendar, and the calendar is published in advance. Two features of it matter.

First, weekends are excluded, so a trade placed on the last business day of a week settles on the first business day of the next, which is a three-calendar-day gap for a one-business-day obligation. Second, exchange holidays are excluded, and holidays cluster: a run of two or three consecutive market holidays turns a one-business-day obligation into a calendar interval of several days. Neither is a delay in the process; both are the convention being applied honestly.

The consequence for planning is that the number of calendar days between trade and credit is a function of the calendar, and the settlement calendar published by the exchange is the document that answers it. An investor who needs funds on a particular date should count forward in business days from the trade and check the holiday list, rather than adding a fixed number of days to the trade date.

## Corporate actions, record dates and the ex-date

Corporate actions are where the shorter cycle produces the most visible change, because entitlement is determined by a record date rather than by a trade date.

For a bonus issue or a split, the additional or re-denominated shares are credited to the accounts on the register as of the record date. A split or bonus changes the number of shares and the per-share figures — including earnings per share — arithmetically, while leaving market capitalisation unchanged, because the same business is now divided into more units. The ex-date is the date on which the market price adjusts to reflect the action, so a fall in the quoted price on the ex-date of a bonus is arithmetic rather than loss.

Because settlement is now faster, the last date on which buying a share can create the entitlement is closer to the record date than it was under a two-day cycle. That does not change the rule — buying on or after the ex-date does not create the entitlement — but it changes the planning margin. An investor who intends to hold through a record date has less slack now, and an investor relying on a corporate action to arrive before a planned sale has less room for the credit to be delayed by a failure elsewhere in the chain.

## What the shortened cycle changed for cash flow

The clearest way to see the effect is to look at the funding pattern rather than the calendar.

Under a longer cycle, an investor had more days between committing to a purchase and having to have the money in place, and a broker had more days of float. The shortened cycle transfers that float away and replaces it with discipline. The consequences are mostly good and occasionally inconvenient.

- A profitable sale releases funds sooner, which slightly raises the effective return on the capital released, because idle cash earns less than invested cash. That difference is exactly the opportunity cost the calculator in this series measures.
- A purchase needs funding sooner, so a settlement account that was routinely in credit a day later may now need to be funded before the trade rather than after it.
- Sequences of transactions have to be planned more tightly. A sale that funds a subsequent purchase is now a shorter bridge than it was, so an investor who rolls positions should check that the proceeds of the first arrive before the obligation for the second falls due.
- Intraday positions are unaffected in substance, because they net to a cash difference and create no delivery obligation.

## Failures and buy-in

A shorter cycle reduces the time available to fix a problem, and it does not change who bears it. If a seller's account does not contain the securities promised, that is a short delivery. The clearing corporation does not pass the problem to the buyer: it arranges delivery of the securities from the market through a buy-in or auction process, and the cost and penalties fall on the defaulting member.

What the shortened cycle changed is the margin for correcting operational problems on the buyer's side as well: a demat credit rejected because an account is closed, frozen or subject to a name mismatch now surfaces closer to the trade. In a functioning broker relationship this is rare; in a neglected or dormant account it is the most common reason a settlement appears to fail.

## Worked example

Illustrative calculations only. The figures are chosen to expose the arithmetic and are not prices, charges or rates quoted by any exchange, broker or depository.

**Counting in business days.** Suppose 100 shares are bought at ₹250 each, a trade value of ₹25,000. A trade executed on a Thursday settles on the following Friday, one business day later, if that day is a settlement day. The same trade executed on a Friday settles on the following Monday, subject to that Monday not being an exchange holiday. If the intervening Monday and Tuesday are market holidays, the same one-business-day obligation falls due on the Wednesday, which is a five-calendar-day gap from the trade. The obligation did not get longer; the calendar did.

**The cost of a settlement day.** Suppose ₹1,00,000 is committed to a purchase and the settlement interval is one day longer than it would be under a shorter cycle. At an illustrative 6% a year, one day on ₹1,00,000 is about ₹16.44, obtained by multiplying ₹1,00,000 by 0.06 and dividing by 365. That is the scale of the float involved in a single retail trade, and it is why the aggregate effect of a one-day change matters to intermediaries who handle thousands of trades a day even though it is trivial for one investor.

**Recovery arithmetic after a loss.** A fall of 50% requires a gain of 100% on the remaining value to return to the starting point. This has nothing to do with settlement and everything to do with why the cycle should not be confused with safety: settling faster does not change the arithmetic of a loss.

## What this does not tell you

This article explains the settlement cycle, its timing and its practical cut-offs. It does not tell you which securities are worth owning, and it does not predict how a price will behave. It also does not cover primary-market application mechanics, which run on a different funding logic to secondary-market settlement.

Three gaps deserve emphasis. First, the phase dates and security coverage of the move to a shorter cycle are not asserted here, and anyone who needs them for a specific operational date should take them from the exchange and regulator announcements. Second, the description is of the standard equity path; derivative contracts and other instruments route through the same clearing architecture with their own margin and settlement rules. Third, a shorter cycle is an efficiency improvement rather than a risk reduction: it compresses the window in which an unsettled claim exists, and it leaves the concentrated credit risk that sits behind novation exactly where it was.

Cost is the one variable in investing that is known in advance, is almost entirely within the investor's control, and is nonetheless ignored in most performance conversations. A fund's expense ratio is quoted as a small percentage — often smaller than the day-to-day movement of the assets it describes — which makes it easy to treat as a rounding error. It is not a rounding error. It is a recurring proportional subtraction applied to the compounding base, and over decades it accumulates into an outcome measured in lakhs. This article derives the arithmetic, computes the drag over four horizons, and separates the two things a fee illustration usually conflates.

## Explain simply

A fund charges a percentage of the money it manages, every year. That charge comes out of the portfolio, so the portfolio compounds from a smaller base next year, and the year after that the smaller base still has to carry the fee. Because the fee is charged on the assets rather than on the profit, it also applies in years when the assets fall. A charge that looks tiny in any single year becomes large over thirty years for exactly the same reason a small positive return becomes large over thirty years — compounding does not care whether the number is a gain or a cost.

## Where a fee sits in the compounding equation

Let the gross return per year be g, the annual fee rate be f, and the starting value be V. Ignoring the fee, wealth compounds as usual:

$$ V_T^{\text{gross}} = V_0 \left( 1 + g \right)^{T} $$

If the fee is deducted from the return each year, so that the portfolio compounds at the gross rate less the fee, the net path is

$$ V_T^{\text{net}} \approx V_0 \left( 1 + g - f \right)^{T} $$

This is the form almost always used, and it is a first-order simplification. If instead the fee is charged on the value *after* the year's return, the exact factor is the product of the growth factor and one minus the fee:

$$ V_T^{\text{net}} = V_0 \left[ \left( 1 + g \right) \left( 1 - f \right) \right]^{T} = V_0 \left( 1 + g - f - gf \right)^{T} $$

The two conventions differ by the product of the gross return and the fee, which is second-order when both are small: at a ten per cent gross return and a 1.5 per cent fee the difference is 0.15 per cent a year. A total expense ratio is accrued against a scheme's net assets and reflected in the net asset value, which makes the daily equivalent of the second form the more faithful description. For the arithmetic below the first form is used, and the reader should know that it flatters the fee slightly.

The ratio of net to gross terminal wealth follows immediately, and it depends only on the fee, the gross return and the horizon:

$$ \frac{V_T^{\text{net}}}{V_T^{\text{gross}}} = \left( \frac{1 + g - f}{1 + g} \right)^{T} = \left( 1 - \frac{f}{1 + g} \right)^{T} $$

The term in brackets is the fraction of each year's growth that the fee does not consume; the exponent is how many years that fraction is applied.

## Worked example: 1.5 per cent a year for four decades

Every input here is hypothetical and chosen to make the arithmetic visible: a starting corpus of 10,00,000 rupees, a gross return of 10 per cent a year, and a fee of 1.5 per cent a year. These are not the terms of any scheme.

| Horizon | No fee | With 1.5% a year | Shortfall | Shortfall as share of the no-fee value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 years | 25,93,742 | 22,60,974 | 3,32,768 | 12.8% |
| 20 years | 67,27,500 | 51,12,010 | 16,15,490 | 24.0% |
| 30 years | 1,74,49,402 | 1,15,58,010 | 58,91,392 | 33.8% |
| 40 years | 4,52,59,256 | 2,61,32,640 | 1,91,26,616 | 42.3% |

The single line that carries the argument is the last column. One and a half per cent a year removes about thirteen per cent of terminal wealth over a decade and over forty per cent over four decades. Nothing about the fee changed between those rows; only the number of years it was charged grew. This is the arithmetic of the claim that a small annual cost compounds, and it is a subtraction applied to the compounding base rather than a one-off deduction.

## What was paid as cash, and what was forgone

There is a distinction inside the shortfall that most cost illustrations skip. The rupees actually handed over as fees accumulate as a geometric series, because each year's fee is a percentage of a base that has grown. Summing the payments gives

$$ \text{Fees paid} = f \, V_0 \, \frac{\left( 1 + r \right)^{T} - 1}{r}, \qquad r = g - f $$

On the same hypothetical inputs, over thirty years the fees actually paid amount to about 18,63,000 rupees, while the shortfall in terminal wealth is about 58,91,000 rupees. The gap between those two figures is not a trick: it is the return that the money paid in fees would itself have earned had it stayed invested. The cash cost is the smaller number and the opportunity cost is the larger one, and both are real. An illustration that quotes the larger figure as "fees paid" overstates what was handed over; an illustration that quotes only the smaller figure understates what the fee cost. The honest presentation reports both and says which is which.

## The drag grows, then flattens

The share of terminal wealth lost is a fraction raised to a power, so it behaves like a decay curve. For horizons short enough that the exponent is small it is approximately linear in the horizon, which is why the first decade's loss looks modest. As the horizon extends it approaches a ceiling:

$$ 1 - \left( 1 - \frac{f}{1 + g} \right)^{T} \longrightarrow 1 \text{ as } T \text{ grows} $$

The fraction lost can approach but never exceed the whole, because the fee is charged on a base that is itself shrinking relative to the no-fee path. What the ceiling does not capture is the rupee amount, which keeps growing: the ratio flattens while the absolute gap widens, because both paths are larger. Investors usually care about the rupee figure at the horizon they actually face, and it is the more useful of the two.

## Costs that are not the expense ratio

The expense ratio is the most visible recurring cost, not the only one. The total cost of ownership of a holding is the sum of several distinct charges, and a comparison that uses one of them is not a comparison of cost.

- **Transaction costs inside the portfolio.** Brokerage, taxes on trades in the underlying securities and the price impact of buying and selling are costs of running a portfolio, and they are borne by the scheme regardless of what is disclosed as a recurring charge.
- **Exit loads and entry charges.** One-off, but they matter whenever a switch is being considered, and a switch motivated by a small cost difference can be defeated by them.
- **Separately charged advisory fees.** Where advice is billed directly rather than embedded in the product, it is a real cost that sits outside the scheme figures.
- **Taxes on the realised gains.** Tax is not a fee but it reduces the net outcome in the same way, and it depends on holding period and on the investor's own position.

## Direct and regular plans: the mechanism, not a number

Mutual fund schemes in India are commonly offered in more than one plan, and the expense ratios of those plans differ because they carry different distribution-related charges. The levels are disclosed per scheme and per plan on a monthly basis, and the daily net asset values on which any cost comparison rests are published for every scheme. That is the mechanism: the difference between two plans of the same scheme is documented, and it is disclosed rather than inferred. The specific figures vary by scheme, by category and by period, and no figure is quoted here, because quoting one without the disclosure behind it would turn a documented fact into an assertion. Anyone auditing cost should read the current disclosure for the scheme and plan in question rather than a remembered number.

## Where the fee-drag argument misleads

The argument is arithmetically sound and rhetorically overused, in five recognisable ways.

- **Fee is compared against return, as though they were alternatives.** A one per cent fee on a ten per cent gross return is not "ten per cent of the return"; it is one per cent of the assets. The two quantities have different bases, and treating them as a ratio understates the cost over long horizons.
- **Cost is treated as the only variable.** Net outcome is gross return less cost. A lower-cost fund that earns less gross may deliver less net, so cost is one input to a decision and not a verdict on it.
- **Tax is ignored.** A cost-driven switch that realises taxable gains can destroy more value than the fee difference it captures, especially over short horizons.
- **The mirror case is forgotten.** A small and persistent *advantage* in gross return compounds exactly as a small cost does. The same arithmetic that makes a fee expensive makes a genuine edge valuable, and it is a failure of reasoning to invoke compounding only against costs.
- **Real versus nominal is blurred.** A fee is charged in nominal terms; over long horizons the rupee drag grows while the real burden depends on what inflation does at the same time. Comparing a nominal fee drag with an inflation-adjusted outcome requires deflating one of them.

## Auditing your own total cost

1. **Find the expense ratio for the exact plan** held, from the current scheme disclosure, not from memory or a comparison site.
2. **Add the one-off charges** — exit loads, switch costs, entry charges — when a transaction is being contemplated.
3. **Add advisory and platform charges** that are billed separately.
4. **Estimate the tax** that a change would realise, using the holding period and the actual rules in force.
5. **Compare net to net.** Two options are comparable only after all of the above are applied on both sides.
6. **Repeat the exercise periodically.** Costs change, and so does the plan that is cheapest for a given scheme.

## What this does not tell you

This arithmetic does not say that a low-cost product will deliver a better outcome, that a high-cost one cannot, or that a switch should be made. It does not forecast any return, and its inputs here are hypothetical rather than observed. It does not capture transaction costs inside a portfolio that are not disclosed as a recurring charge, and it does not model the fee's interaction with tax, which can dominate it. What it establishes is narrower and firmer: a recurring proportional cost is a compounding subtraction, its effect grows with the holding period, and the rupee gap between a fee-paying path and a no-fee path is larger than the cash actually paid, because the cash would have compounded.

## Open questions

- How should a total cost of ownership, including portfolio transaction costs, be disclosed so that schemes are genuinely comparable?
- For an investor moving between direct and regular plans, what is the break-even period once exit loads and any realised capital-gains tax are included?
- How much of a cost difference between plans reflects a genuine difference in service rather than a difference in distribution economics?

Fees are the rare variable in investing that can be known in advance and reduced without giving up anything except the service being paid for. The arithmetic of the previous sections is what makes that decision worth taking seriously, and the arithmetic of gross return is what stops it from being taken alone.

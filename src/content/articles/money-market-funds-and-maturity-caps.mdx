Among the debt categories, money-market schemes are the most directly tied to what the central bank does. They hold the shortest instruments in the market, and those instruments are repriced at the current policy rate within weeks. That makes the category simple in one respect — its return tracks the short end of the curve — and easy to overrate in another, because being short is not the same as being riskless. This article sets out what a money-market scheme is allowed to hold, what the maturity ceiling in the category definition actually does, how policy moves reach the NAV, and where the category differs from the ultra-short band that sits beside it.

## Explain simply

A money-market fund holds debt that is about to be repaid — government borrowing at the shortest tenor, short paper issued by banks and companies, and short-term lending between market participants. Because everything matures soon, the fund's holdings are constantly being replaced at today's rates, so its return moves with the policy rate rather than being fixed for years. The maturity ceiling in the rules is what keeps the portfolio this short. The fund still owns securities rather than holding cash in trust, so its unit value is marked to market and can move, and a default in one holding is still a loss. The category is lower in interest-rate risk than longer debt funds, not free of risk.

## The instruments in the band

Money-market schemes hold the short end of the debt market. The instruments in this segment fall into a few families:

- **Government paper at the shortest tenor**, which carries effectively no credit risk in rupee terms but still reprices when short rates move.
- **Short-term paper issued by banks and corporations**, which pays a spread over government paper for the credit risk of the issuer and is a principal source of the category's extra yield.
- **Secured short-term lending between market participants and repo transactions**, which provide both yield and the liquidity buffer a scheme needs to meet redemptions.

The eligibility of each instrument, the quality it must carry and the limits on how much of it a scheme may hold are set in the categorisation circular and in the money-market framework, both of which have been amended. This article therefore names the families and not the limits; anyone who needs the operative list and thresholds should read the current circular and the scheme information document.

## The maturity ceiling, and why it defines the category

The defining rule of a money-market scheme is a ceiling on the maturity of the instruments it may hold, together with a limit on the portfolio's overall maturity profile. The ceiling is what separates the category from the ones above it, and it is the reason the category behaves the way it does.

A short ceiling has three effects, and they are worth separating.

1. **Low interest-rate sensitivity.** Because the holdings mature soon, their prices are close to their redemption value, so a change in market yields moves the portfolio only a little. That is the main thing the ceiling buys.
2. **Rapid reinvestment.** Maturing instruments are replaced at current rates, so the scheme's income tracks short-term rates with a lag rather than staying fixed. A holder in a falling-rate environment sees income decline, not hold.
3. **Limited room to earn a term premium.** A scheme confined to the shortest paper cannot reach for the extra yield that comes with lending a little longer. That is a cost of the ceiling as much as a protection.

The ceiling itself is a number in the circular, and it is not restated here. What can be asserted structurally is that the money-market category is the most tightly capped of the short-term debt categories, and that the cap — not the scheme's name — is what decides how far its NAV can move.

## How policy reaches the NAV

The short end of the curve is where monetary policy acts first. The central bank sets its policy rate and manages liquidity in the banking system, and the yields on the instruments a money-market scheme holds respond to those settings within a short period. Two channels matter to a fund holder.

- **The income channel.** As holdings mature and are replaced, the yield the scheme earns on its portfolio moves toward the prevailing short-term rate. A cut in the policy rate lowers the income the scheme will earn on reinvestment; a rise lifts it. The adjustment is gradual, spread over the maturity of the existing holdings.
- **The valuation channel.** A change in short rates also revalues the existing holdings. When rates rise, the market value of holdings falls, so the NAV dips before the higher income has been earned. When rates fall, the NAV rises first and the lower income follows.

The two channels pull in opposite directions on different horizons. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why a money-market scheme is described as a low-duration exposure rather than a cash equivalent.

## Money-market against ultra-short: substance, not label

The two categories overlap enough that they are often treated as one. The distinctions that matter are structural rather than nominal.

| Feature | Money-market scheme | Ultra-short scheme |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maturity ceiling | Typically the tightest in the debt range | A longer band is permitted |
| Dominant exposure | The shortest rates | Short rates plus a little term premium |
| NAV sensitivity to a rate move | Smallest | Small, but larger than money-market |
| Credit room | Governed by the category's quality limits | Governed by its own limits, which may allow more spread |
| Natural horizon | Weeks to a few months | A few months |

The practical reading is that a money-market scheme is the shortest expression of a short-rate view, and an ultra-short scheme is a slightly longer one. Neither is intrinsically better; they answer different horizons. Because the bands are close, the decisive evidence is in the monthly portfolio — the actual weights, the average maturity and the quality of the holdings.

## The four risks, separated

- **Credit risk** is the risk that an issuer of short paper fails to pay. The category's quality limits reduce it, but they do not remove it, and a default is absorbed entirely by the NAV.
- **Interest-rate risk** is the risk that short rates rise and revalue the portfolio. It is the smallest of the four here because of the ceiling, but it is not zero.
- **Liquidity risk** is the risk of meeting redemptions by selling into a thin market at a poor price. It is managed through the scheme's cash and repo buffers, which is itself a drag on yield.
- **Mark-to-market behaviour** is how the first three reach the investor. The scheme's value is the market value of its holdings each day; there is no promise of a fixed rupee amount.

## Why the yield here is compensation

A money-market scheme can offer more than a deposit or a longer-dated alternative at different points in the cycle, and the difference is never free.

- A spread over government paper is **compensation for issuers' credit risk**, since short corporate and bank paper can default.
- Holding less cash and more paper earns a premium that is **given up in liquidity** — it is available until the moment a scheme needs cash quickly.
- A longer permitted holding within the ceiling is **compensation for a little more duration**.
- All of it is reduced by the **expense ratio**, which is charged against the portfolio and directly lowers the NAV growth the holder sees.

The right question about a higher-yielding money-market scheme is therefore what it gave up to earn the extra, and whether the answer is visible in its disclosure.

## Worked example: an illustrative rate move and credit event

The following is an illustrative calculation from assumed inputs, not a forecast and not any scheme's observed result. Suppose an investor holds ₹1,00,000 in a money-market scheme whose portfolio is assumed to have a modified duration of 0.3 years.

**A rate move.** If short-term yields rise by 0.4 of a percentage point, the approximate NAV change is the modified duration multiplied by the yield change, sign reversed:

$$ \Delta NAV \approx -D \times \Delta y $$

That is 0.3 multiplied by 0.4%, an assumed fall of about 0.12%, or roughly ₹120 on ₹1,00,000, before the higher reinvestment income that follows. Notice the direction of the mechanism over time: the diary of the NAV dips, then the income on reinvested holdings rises toward the new short rate.

**A credit event.** Assume separately that a holding representing 1.5% of the portfolio defaults, with a recovery of 30% assumed, so the loss given default is 70%. The loss is the holding's weight multiplied by the loss given default:

$$ \text{Loss} \approx w \times LGD $$

That is 0.015 multiplied by 0.70, or about 1.05% of NAV, roughly ₹1,050 on ₹1,00,000. On a portfolio earning a thin spread over the risk-free rate, that single event can exceed several months of income. It is the clearest argument for reading the quality of the holdings rather than accepting the category label as a safety rating.

## The four lanes, in this context

- **Contractual.** The coupon or discount at which each short instrument is issued, backed by a specific issuer, is the contractual part of the return.
- **Model-estimated.** The duration arithmetic above, and any projection of the scheme's income from the yields of its holdings, are model estimates from stated assumptions.
- **Historical realised.** What money-market schemes delivered over a past period of policy settings, computed from scheme-level NAV data. It describes that period and does not precede the next one.
- **Speculative.** The gain from falling short rates and the loss from rising ones. The ceiling limits the size of this lane relative to longer funds; it does not close it.

## What this does not tell you

- **It does not state the ceiling.** The maturity limit and instrument eligibility are set in circulars that have been amended; the operative numbers must be read there.
- **It does not forecast rates.** Nothing here predicts or recommends a view on the policy rate, and the category should not be used as a rate bet dressed up as cash management.
- **It does not handle tax.** Gains from debt-oriented schemes are taxed under rules that depend on holding period and change over time.
- **It does not separate two schemes.** Two funds in the band can hold different quality and carry different costs; the disclosures are where the comparison happens.
- **It does not replace the emergency layer.** Money needed without notice belongs in same-day access, not in a marked-to-market scheme.

## Open questions

- How much of the very short yield available to money-market schemes is genuine compensation for credit and liquidity, and how much reflects a temporary imbalance between the supply of short paper and the demand for it?
- When the short end of the curve is flat or inverted, does the maturity ceiling hold investors back from yield that is available a little further out without much extra risk?
- Does the closeness of the money-market and ultra-short categories help investors position money, or does it mainly create the appearance of a choice that the disclosures would collapse?
- As settlement and payment systems speed up, does a fund wrapper add anything to the very short-term money that a direct instrument does not?

Most investors choose a mutual fund by name and by the last few years of returns. Neither tells them what the fund is allowed to do. In India that question has a regulatory answer: every scheme sits inside a category defined by SEBI, with portfolio rules that constrain what the manager may hold. The category is the most reliable piece of information on the cover page, because it is the one the manager is obliged to respect.

This article opens the Mutual Fund Atlas series by setting out that grid. It is about decoding labels, not about choosing funds.

## The label problem the framework solved

Before the categorisation framework, two fund houses could sell schemes with near-identical names and very different portfolios. A "bluechip" fund might hold the largest companies; another "bluechip" fund might hold something else entirely. Comparison was impossible, and the investor had no way of knowing what mandate they were buying.

The framework replaced that with a closed grid. Instead of allowing an unrestricted supply of names, it defined a fixed set of categories, assigned each a portfolio rule, and restricted how many schemes a fund house may run in the same category. The consequence is that a category label now means approximately the same thing across fund houses, which is what makes category-level comparison meaningful. It is the closest thing Indian retail investors have to a standardised unit of comparison in funds.

The grid is not permanent: it has been amended since it was introduced, and the thresholds live in the circular rather than in the scheme documents.

## The five groups at a glance

| Group | What it contains | The organising idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equity | Schemes investing predominantly in shares | Which market-capitalisation segment, and which mandate |
| Debt | Schemes investing in bonds, money-market instruments and government securities | Duration band, and credit quality |
| Hybrid | Schemes holding equity and debt together | How much equity, and whether the split is fixed or dynamic |
| Solution-oriented | Retirement and children's schemes | A purpose, with a lock-in attached to it |
| Other | Index schemes, exchange-traded funds and funds of funds | Replication of an index, or investment in other schemes |

The groups are not arbitrary. Each answers the question an investor actually asks: what kind of asset is inside, and how is it managed? The category within the group answers the follow-up: which segment, at what risk.

## Equity categories and the holding rules

The equity group is separated mainly by market-capitalisation segment and by the kind of mandate the manager must follow. Market capitalisation is measured by the industry classification of listed companies, published as a list and updated periodically, which divides the market into three segments: the largest companies, the middle band, and the smaller ones. The segment is what the category rules refer to.

| Category | The mandate it imposes |
| --- | --- |
| Multi cap | Equity as the substantial majority of the portfolio, with a stated minimum allocation spread across the large, mid and small segments rather than concentrated in one |
| Large cap | A minimum of around four-fifths of assets in the largest segment |
| Large and mid cap | A minimum of about a third of assets in each of the large and mid segments |
| Mid cap | A minimum of roughly two-thirds of assets in the mid segment |
| Small cap | A minimum of roughly two-thirds of assets in the small segment |
| Dividend yield | A majority of assets in shares with a stated dividend yield |
| Value | A majority of assets selected on a value strategy |
| Contra | A majority of assets selected on a contrarian strategy |
| Focused | A majority in equity, but in a portfolio limited to a small number of stocks |
| Sectoral or thematic | A minimum of around four-fifths of assets in the named sector or theme |
| Equity-linked savings | A minimum of around four-fifths in equity, with a prescribed lock-in |

Two structural points are easy to miss. First, the minimum is a floor, not a target: a large-cap fund holding exactly the minimum and keeping the remainder in cash is compliant, and is deliberately different from an index fund. Second, those floors and caps are what limit **style drift** — a value fund that starts behaving like a momentum fund is not merely disappointing, it is non-compliant.

The thresholds above are described in round terms because the framework expresses them in percentages that have been amended and are best read in the circular itself.

## Debt categories: duration bands and credit quality

Debt categories are organised on two axes, and confusing them is the most common error in reading a debt fund's label.

**Duration** is the sensitivity of a bond portfolio to interest rates. The framework bands several categories by the maturity profile of the portfolio, from the shortest — overnight and liquid schemes, which hold instruments maturing within days or months — through short, medium and longer duration bands, up to long duration schemes for investors who want maximum interest-rate sensitivity. A dynamic bond scheme is the exception inside this axis: it is permitted to move across durations, which is a mandate for interest-rate views rather than a fixed band.

**Credit quality** is the risk that an issuer does not pay. Categories here include corporate bond schemes, which are required to hold predominantly higher-rated corporate debt; credit risk schemes, whose mandate is lower-rated corporate credit and therefore higher expected default risk; banking and public-sector schemes, concentrated in a defined set of issuers; gilt schemes, which hold government securities and carry no meaningful credit risk but full interest-rate risk; a constant-duration gilt variant with a target duration; and floating-rate schemes, which hold instruments whose coupons reset with a reference rate.

The distinction matters because it decides what kind of loss the fund can suffer. A gilt fund's risk is almost entirely interest-rate risk. A credit risk fund's risk is mostly default risk. The two can look similar in a rising-rate year and behave completely differently when a credit event occurs.

## Hybrid categories, and the arbitrage oddity

Hybrid schemes hold equity and debt together, and the categories are separated by how much equity they carry and how the split is managed.

- **Conservative hybrid:** predominantly debt, with a small equity component that provides some growth without dominating the risk.
- **Balanced hybrid:** a substantial allocation to each, without using arbitrage positions to satisfy the equity requirement.
- **Aggressive hybrid:** predominantly equity, with a debt component that dampens the ride.
- **Dynamic asset allocation or balanced advantage:** the split is moved across equity and debt according to a stated model or a manager's discretion, which makes the equity exposure itself a variable.
- **Multi asset allocation:** required to hold several distinct asset classes, each above a minimum, so that no single one dominates.
- **Arbitrage:** positions that capture the price relationship between the cash and derivatives markets, with the offsetting legs largely neutralising market direction.
- **Equity savings:** a blend of equity, debt and arbitrage exposure, sitting between the hybrid and the arbitrage categories.

The arbitrage category is the oddity worth understanding. Its portfolio is largely hedged, so its return comes from the spread between the cash and futures markets rather than from equity appreciation, and its risk profile is nearer a short-term debt fund than an equity fund — despite sitting in a group alongside equity-heavy categories. The label is accurate about holdings and potentially misleading about risk.

## Solution-oriented schemes and the residual group

Two categories are defined by purpose rather than by asset mix, and both carry a prescribed lock-in.

- **Retirement schemes** are designed around retirement saving and impose a lock-in that reflects that purpose.
- **Children's schemes** are designed around saving for a child's needs, with a lock-in tied to the beneficiary.

The lock-in is the product. It removes the investor's ability to abandon the plan early, which is a benefit for a long-horizon goal and a genuine cost when circumstances change. Investors who cannot tolerate the restriction should not buy the category, however attractive the holdings.

The residual "other" group contains what the grid deliberately leaves out: **index schemes** and **exchange-traded funds**, which replicate a stated index rather than being managed to a segment, and **funds of funds**, which hold units of other schemes rather than securities directly. These are exempt from the one-scheme-per-category duplication limit, which is why a single fund house can offer many index funds and exchange-traded funds tracking different indices.

## What a category name does and does not promise

A category name promises **a portfolio rule**. It promises that the fund will be invested in a defined way, and therefore that two funds with the same label are comparable in kind.

It does not promise:

- **A return.** The rule constrains holdings, not outcomes. Two large-cap funds can hold different stocks, in different weights, at different costs.
- **A risk level.** Concentration, cash levels and, for debt, credit quality can differ materially inside one category.
- **Cost.** The expense ratio varies between schemes and between the direct and regular plans of the same scheme, and it is charged against the assets every year.
- **Tax efficiency.** The portfolio turnover ratio, which measures how actively the portfolio is traded, affects the transaction costs borne by unit holders and the timing of gains.
- **A safe exit.** The exit load and the holding period that triggers it are set out in the scheme documents, not in the category.

That list is also the answer to "is my fund doing what it says?" The category tells you whether it is compliant. The expense ratio, turnover and portfolio tell you whether the compliance is being delivered efficiently.

## How to verify a scheme's category yourself

Everything needed is disclosed, and the check takes minutes.

1. **The scheme information document and the key information memorandum** state the category, the investment objective, the asset allocation pattern the fund will follow, and the benchmark. This is where the mandate is written down.
2. **The monthly portfolio disclosure** shows the actual holdings, the total expense ratio and the portfolio turnover ratio. Compare the holdings against the asset allocation pattern; where the fund is near a regulatory floor, the composition of the remainder tells you what the manager is doing with the freedom the rule leaves.
3. **The AMFI scheme data** provides industry-level category information and the daily NAV file provides scheme-level NAVs and scheme codes, which is what makes it possible to compute category statistics, rolling returns and drawdowns from a reproducible source rather than from a chart in a presentation.
4. **The scheme document** for exit load and minimum investment terms.

## Explain simply

Indian mutual funds are sorted into fixed regulatory categories, so a "large-cap fund" or a "liquid fund" means roughly the same thing whichever fund house sells it. The category sets what the fund is allowed to hold — a large-cap fund has to keep most of its money in the biggest companies, a mid-cap fund in medium ones, a liquid fund in very short-term debt. That makes comparison possible, but it does not tell you what the fund will earn, what it charges, or how risky it is inside its category. To find those out, read the monthly portfolio and the expense ratio, not just the name.

## Worked example: reading one scheme's label

An illustrative walkthrough, using invented figures to show the method rather than any real scheme.

Suppose a scheme is named an equity opportunities fund. The name is marketing; the category is regulatory. The scheme information document places it in a category that requires a minimum share of assets in equity, states its asset allocation pattern and names its benchmark. The monthly portfolio discloses net assets of ₹1,000 crore; if the category floor were a minimum of four-fifths in equity, the illustrative arithmetic is that at least ₹800 crore must sit in equity and at most ₹200 crore may sit in cash, debt or other permitted instruments. That ceiling is a piece of information the category supply has just given you for free.

Now the questions the category does not answer, and where you find the answers:

- What is the total expense ratio, and is it the direct or the regular plan? The monthly disclosure states it; the difference compounds against the corpus every year.
- How much is the portfolio turning over? A high turnover ratio raises transaction costs and shifts the timing of taxable gains.
- Does the portfolio sit at the floor, with a large cash or non-equity position, or well inside the mandate? That is a view on the market, and it should be a deliberate one.
- Is the benchmark sensible for the category, and does the performance presentation compare like with like? A category is not a benchmark; the benchmark is the hurdle, and it is disclosed.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the taxonomy and how to verify a label. It does not select a fund, quantify the dispersion within a category — that requires computing the distribution from scheme-level NAV data — or resolve which category suits a goal, which is a question of allocation and horizon rather than of labels.

Two omissions are deliberate rather than oversights: the tax treatment of gains from different scheme types, which is governed by the Income-tax Act and treated separately on this site, and the mechanics of expense ratios, direct and regular plans and benchmarks, which later articles in this series cover. Nothing here is advice, and no category or return is recommended or promised.

A tenant signs a lease, pays rent every quarter, and eventually the money appears in a unit holder bank account. That journey looks automatic from the outside, and the whole appeal of a REIT rests on it looking automatic. It is not automatic. Between the rent and the distribution sit property operating costs, debt, maintenance spending, reserves and a manager, and every one of them has a legitimate claim on the cash before a unit holder does.

This article follows the money from the tenant to the account, and then opens the distribution itself. A payout from a trust is not one thing: it is usually a package of interest, dividend and returned capital, and treating the total as a single income number is the most common error in reading a REIT.

## Explain simply

Think of a REIT as a large landlord with borrowed money. The tenants pay rent. Out of that rent the landlord pays for security, cleaning, repairs, electricity, property taxes and insurance, then pays the interest on its loans, then spends on keeping the buildings in good order, then puts something aside for the unpredictable. Whatever is left over is the cash the landlord can actually hand out. SEBI requires it to hand out at least ninety per cent of that remainder. A unit holder receives a share in proportion to the units held, and that share arrives as a package whose parts are labelled differently, partly for legal reasons and partly because the parts are taxed differently.

## Where the money starts: rent from tenants

The top line of a REIT is contractual. Commercial leases, particularly for offices, are typically signed for multi-year terms with defined escalations, which makes the near-term rental income unusually predictable compared with most businesses. That predictability is the product. But predictability is not permanence, and three variables move the top line over time: occupancy, the rent at which vacated space is re-let, and the escalations written into existing leases.

Two features distinguish the rental stream of a trust from that of a private landlord. The first is scale, which allows professional leasing and tenant management and gives access to institutional tenants who would not rent from an individual owner. The second is concentration. A trust with a dozen buildings and a handful of large tenants has a rental stream that depends on a surprisingly small number of decisions, and a single large default can matter more than any cost the manager can control.

## The costs that stand between rent and cash

Every rupee of rent is claimed several times before a unit holder sees any of it. At the level of the property, the trust bears operating expenses: staff and security, cleaning, utilities, repairs, property taxes and insurance. What remains is usually described as net operating income. Below that line sit the claims of the capital structure: interest and principal on debt, and the capital expenditure needed to keep buildings let, which is not optional spending and is distinct from expanding the portfolio.

Only after those claims does the trust arrive at net distributable cash flow, commonly abbreviated NDCF. NDCF is the cash available to unit holders after operating expenses, interest and debt service, maintenance capital expenditure and any mandatory reserves. A manager that funds expansion is spending above the maintenance line, and that spending competes with distributions, which is one reason a trust may raise fresh capital rather than fund growth from rent.

## Net distributable cash flow is not profit

The single most useful distinction in REIT analysis is the one between distributable cash and accounting profit. Reported profit is struck after depreciation and after fair-value changes to the property portfolio. Neither of those necessarily moves cash. A large fair-value gain can flatter profit while leaving distributable cash untouched; a fair-value write-down can turn reported profit negative while the buildings continue to collect rent and hand over cash.

The regulation reflects this by making NDCF the distribution base and requiring the trust to disclose it at least once every six months. When you read a trust report, the profit figure and the NDCF figure answer different questions. Profit is the accountant assessment of value created; NDCF is the cash that could be paid out. Distribute based on profit and a trust would be forced to pay out unrealised revaluation gains; distribute based on cash and the payment tracks what the buildings actually produced.

## The mandatory distribution rule

The regulatory requirement is that at least 90 per cent of net distributable cash flows must be distributed to unit holders. That number does the heaviest lifting in the entire structure, because it is what converts a property portfolio into an income instrument. It is also frequently misread. The obligation is a share of available cash, not a fixed rupee amount per unit. If NDCF falls, the distribution falls with it; the trust is not promising a constant payment.

Two consequences follow. First, the distribution is more reliable in form than in size: you can expect a payment, but not necessarily the same payment. Second, very little cash is retained. The trust keeps at most a tenth of NDCF plus whatever it raises separately, so expansion is typically funded by issuing new units or by borrowing rather than by accumulated earnings. The distribution rule and the growth constraint are the same rule seen from two directions.

## Three components inside one distribution

The cash is one number in your account, but it is usually assembled from three kinds of payment, and the composition matters.

- **Interest** arises where the trust or its entities have advanced debt funding, so part of the payout is interest on money lent within the structure.
- **Dividend** is paid out of distributable profits of the entities that hold the assets, and is the component most closely tied to the operating performance of the property.
- **Return of capital** is a repayment of invested capital rather than income earned on it. It is your own money being handed back, which is why a high share of return of capital in a distribution is a signal to look closer rather than to celebrate.

The distinction is not cosmetic. The composition changes how the payment is taxed in the hands of a unit holder, and it changes what the payment tells you about the health of the underlying business. A distribution made largely of return of capital is not the same economic event as a distribution made largely of dividend, even when the rupee amount and the date are identical. The calculator linked to this article exists precisely because the split has to be entered explicitly rather than assumed.

## Distribution per unit and the traps in reading it

The figure to track is distribution per unit, usually abbreviated DPU, which is the cash distributed per unit for a defined period, normally disclosed quarterly and aggregated annually. DPU is meaningful only alongside three other facts: the issue price at which the units were first sold, the prevailing market price, and the split between interest, dividend and return of capital.

The trap is the sequence of arithmetic. A headline figure computed as distribution divided by the current price will rise mechanically whenever the unit price falls, which is the opposite of good news, and will fall whenever the price rises, which is not bad news. DPU itself, read over several years next to the number of units outstanding, avoids both distortions. A DPU that holds or grows against a stable unit count reflects a portfolio that is working; a DPU that declines per unit, or grows only because the trust has issued more units, is telling you something about asset stress or dilution.

## Worked example: from rent to a distribution

The following is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast and not a description of any listed trust. Every figure is chosen for arithmetic clarity.

| Line | Illustrative annual figure |
| --- | --- |
| Rent collected from tenants | ₹120 crore |
| Property operating expenses | −₹18 crore |
| Net operating income | ₹102 crore |
| Interest and debt service | −₹22 crore |
| Maintenance capital expenditure | −₹6 crore |
| Mandatory reserves | −₹4 crore |
| Net distributable cash flow | ₹70 crore |

The trust must distribute at least 90 per cent of ₹70 crore, which is ₹63 crore. If 63 crore units are outstanding, that is a DPU of ₹1.00 for the year. Suppose the split is interest of ₹0.25, dividend of ₹0.45 and return of capital of ₹0.30, summing to the same ₹1.00. The total cash is unchanged by the mix, but the mix decides both the tax treatment and how much of the payout was earned rather than merely returned.

Now stress the top line. Suppose rents fall by 10 per cent to ₹108 crore while the trust cannot reduce its interest bill, its maintenance spending or its reserves in the same proportion, because debt service is contractual and buildings must still be maintained.

| Line | Stressed annual figure |
| --- | --- |
| Rent collected from tenants | ₹108 crore |
| Property operating expenses | −₹18 crore |
| Net operating income | ₹90 crore |
| Interest and debt service | −₹22 crore |
| Maintenance capital expenditure | −₹6 crore |
| Mandatory reserves | −₹4 crore |
| Net distributable cash flow | ₹58 crore |

The mandatory distribution becomes 90 per cent of ₹58 crore, or ₹52.2 crore, and with the unit count unchanged the DPU falls to about ₹0.83. A ten per cent decline in rent has produced roughly a seventeen per cent decline in the amount paid per unit, because the cost base below the rent line is largely fixed. That amplification is the operating leverage of a trust, and it runs in both directions.

## What this does not tell you

This article explains where a distribution comes from; it does not tell you whether any particular distribution is attractive. It does not assess the credit quality of the tenants, the maturity profile of the debt or the age of the buildings, all of which shape the durability of the rental stream. It does not quantify the tax owed on any of the three components, because that depends on rules in force and on the holder circumstances, and it does not translate a distribution into a yield, which requires a price this article does not supply.

It also says nothing about valuation. A distribution can be healthy while the unit price is falling, and a distribution can be under pressure while the price is rising, because the market prices the future cash flow rather than the last payment. Keeping the cash question and the price question separate is what makes a trust analysis readable.

## Open questions

- When a trust funds part of a distribution from borrowings, asset disposals or reserves rather than recurring rent, how quickly does the published cash-flow disclosure make that visible to a careful reader?
- Over a full property cycle, how much of a typical trust distribution is genuinely inflation-linked through lease escalations, and how much simply reflects the re-letting of space at market rents?
- For small and medium REITs holding only a handful of assets, how much extra return is required to compensate for concentration that a larger trust can diversify away?
- As distributions are increasingly split between their components, will unit holders read the composition with the same attention the market pays to the total?

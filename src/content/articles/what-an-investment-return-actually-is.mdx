Every investment discussion — in a bank branch, on a fund factsheet, in a WhatsApp forward — reduces to one number: "the return". Nine percent. Fifteen percent. "It doubled in two years." The number feels like a fact. It is almost always a blend of at least four different quantities, each with its own risk profile, and the blending is where the trouble starts.

This first article in the Science of Returns series establishes the vocabulary the rest of the site will use. Nothing here is about picking an investment. It is about what you are reading when someone shows you a number.

## Return is a change in wealth, over a stated period

Formally, an investment's return over a period is the change in its total value — income received plus change in price, reinvested — divided by what you put in. Written as an equation, starting from a value $V_0$, ending at $V_T$, with income $D$ paid during the period:

$$ R = \frac{(V_T + D) - V_0}{V_0} $$

There are three moving parts, and almost every argument about a reported return is an argument about one of them. The **starting value** must include everything you actually committed — the purchase, the brokerage, the stamp duty. The **ending value** must include everything still yours, including a position in a second account you had forgotten about. The **income** must include every distribution, whether it was paid out or reinvested.

If you buy units at ₹100, receive ₹4 in distributions, and the units end at ₹106, your total return is **10%** for that period. Skip the distribution — it "went to your bank account" — and the price change alone says 6%. Both numbers get printed; only the first is true to what happened to your wealth.

Two immediate consequences:

- **A return without a period is not a return.** "10%" means nothing until it is 10% per year, or 10% in three months. Annualisation is not decoration; it changes a mediocre number into an excellent one and vice versa.
- **A return without income is a price-change story.** This is why dividend-paying and accumulating investments of identical economic substance can show different published "returns". The gap between a generous dividend payer and a low-yield compounder is often purely accounting.

Annualising is a second place where the honest figure is optional. To turn a multi-year total return into a per-year rate you compound; you do not divide:

$$ R_{\text{annual}} = (1 + R)^{1/T} - 1 $$

A 21% total gain over two years is $(1.21)^{0.5} - 1 = 10.0\%$ a year, not 10.5%. A 44% gain over three years is $(1.44)^{1/3} - 1 = 12.9\%$ a year, not 14.7%. Simple division always flatters, and the longer the period, the more it flatters.

## Four quantities, one word

Here is the failure mode this site is built around. The word "return" is used for at least four distinct things:

**1. Historical realised return.** What actually happened to a defined population over a defined window — an index, a fund, a category. Its credibility depends on what it includes (survivorship drags it upward) and where the window begins and ends (which is chosen, consciously or not, by whoever shows you the chart).

**2. Contractual yield.** What a binding agreement pays — deposit interest, bond coupons, promised distributions. This is the strongest form of claim, because the law stands behind it, but it is only as good as the credit of the counterparty. A 14% coupon from a risky borrower is a 14% promise *priced for a reason*: the market's estimate of default and delay.

**3. Model-estimated expected return.** What arithmetic produces from assumptions: growth rates, margins, rates of reinvestment. These are projections, not promises, and their accuracy is limited by their assumptions — which are inspectable and should always be printed next to the number.

**4. Speculative upside.** The possible-but-unsupported: what a token, an early-stage company or a lottery-ticket strategy *might* deliver. Honest researchers label this lane clearly. The entire "100x in 3 years" genre lives here and pretends not to.

Most bad investment decisions are a category error between these lanes. A model estimate is read as a contractual promise; a historical window is read as a forecast; a speculative hope is priced as if it were the first two. Keep the lanes separate and most pitches collapse on their own.

The lanes are not separated by how confident the speaker sounds. They are separated by what would have to be true for the number to hold, and by the test that would falsify it:

| Lane | What it claims | The test that matters | Typical failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Historical realised | This is what happened to a defined population over a defined window | The window, survivorship and net-of-cost basis are stated | Read as a forecast |
| Contractual | A binding agreement pays this | The credit of the counterparty and the enforceability of the contract | Default and delay treated as impossible |
| Model-estimated | These assumptions produce this outcome | The assumptions are printed beside the number | Assumptions hidden |
| Speculative | This is possible | A base rate for how often it has occurred | Priced as though it were contractual |

A return figure is incomplete in a second way too: it says nothing about the risk taken to produce it. Portfolio theory makes that concrete — return and variance are chosen together, because a higher expected return is compensation for bearing something. The reward-to-variability ratio that Sharpe introduced in 1966 was built to put return on a per-unit-of-risk footing so that portfolios could be compared rather than merely ranked. And the cross-sectional evidence of Fama and French showed that market beta alone does not explain equity returns — which is another way of saying that one percentage is a summary of several exposures at once. Compare two investments on return alone and the comparison has quietly discarded the variable that sets the price.

## Arithmetic averages lie; compounding doesn't

Return averages have a subtle trap that deserves the arithmetic, because it costs people real money. Suppose a fund returns +50% one year and −50% the next. The *arithmetic* average of the two annual returns is 0%. But your wealth: ₹100 → ₹150 → ₹75. You lost 25%.

The average that describes wealth is the **geometric mean**, the compound rate that takes the starting value to the ending value:

$$ \text{CAGR} = \left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)^{1/T} - 1 $$

For the fund above: $(75/100)^{1/2} - 1 = -13.4\%$ per year. The gap between the arithmetic 0% and the compound −13.4% is called *volatility drag*, and it grows with the square of volatility. A useful approximation for annualised figures:

$$ g \approx a - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} $$

where $a$ is the arithmetic average, $\sigma$ the volatility and $g$ the growth rate of your actual wealth. This is why a mathematically "average" market can underperform its own average for decades — and why two investments with the same stated average return are not equivalent if one takes a smoother path.

> **The practical test.** When anyone quotes an average return, ask: *of what — the yearly numbers, or the wealth that resulted?* If they don't know the difference, you now know more about the arithmetic than they do.

## CAGR and XIRR are different questions

Two standard measures are constantly confused:

**CAGR** answers: *what constant annual rate takes my starting value to my ending value?* It requires only two points. It is the right tool for comparing endpoints with no cash flows in between.

**XIRR** answers: *given my actual dated cash flows — SIP instalments in, withdrawals out — what annual rate did my money earn?* It solves an equation across the whole timeline. This is the only honest way to measure a portfolio you have actually been investing in, because your contributions bought different prices along the way.

The difference is material precisely when it matters most. A monthly SIP into a falling-then-recovering market can show a CAGR that looks modest while the XIRR — computed on actual money at actual dates — is excellent, because later instalments bought deeply discounted units. An investor who judges themselves by CAGR in that situation misreads their own results. The calculators linked at the end of this article do both, and the worked example below shows the mechanics once.

## Worked example: the same portfolio, two honest measures

An investor puts ₹10,000 at the start of each year for four years into an index fund that returns −10%, +5%, +25%, +15% in those years, and values the holding at the end.

- **CAGR of the fund itself** (₹100 → after four years of those returns): $0.9 \times 1.05 \times 1.25 \times 1.15 = 1.358$, so $\text{CAGR} = 1.358^{0.25} - 1 = 7.95\%$ per year. That describes the market, not the investor.
- **XIRR of the investor** depends on how ₹40,000 was deployed: the first ₹10,000 rode the −10% year, the last rode only +15%. Depending on weighting, the investor's rupee-weighted outcome can sit above or below the fund's time-weighted CAGR — which is exactly why funds report *time-weighted* returns (a CAGR-like number stripped of your cash-flow timing) and you should report *money-weighted* ones (XIRR) to yourself.

Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions, and the industry rarely says which one it is showing.

### A second example: the same asset, two windows

An illustrative price path — a 20% fall, then a 30% rise, then a 10% rise (a construction to make the arithmetic clear, not the record of any actual index):

- Over all three years the cumulative factor is $0.80 \times 1.30 \times 1.10 = 1.144$, a CAGR of $(1.144)^{1/3} - 1 \approx 4.6\%$ a year.
- Over the last two years only, the factor is $1.30 \times 1.10 = 1.43$, a CAGR of $(1.43)^{0.5} - 1 \approx 19.6\%$ a year.

The same asset, ending at the same price, is a mediocre performer or an excellent one depending on where the window opens. Nobody has to lie for this to happen; they only have to choose a start date.

## From gross to real: the three deductions

The number a product advertises is gross, nominal and pre-tax. Three deductions stand between it and your life:

1. **Costs.** Expense ratios, brokerage, platform fees, stamp duty, exit loads, advisory fees. Costs are certain, returns are not; a 1.5% annual cost on a 7% gross return consumes over a fifth of the eventual wealth gain, and the effect compounds.
2. **Tax.** Taxed at your marginal rate or at special capital-gains rates, with surcharge and cess stacked on top. The tax reference on this site carries the dated rules; the post-tax calculator applies them.
3. **Inflation.** The purchasing power of the rupee. A 7% nominal return with 5% inflation is not 2% real — it is $(1.07/1.05) - 1 \approx 1.9\%$ real, and tax makes it worse.

> **The floor problem.** For a resident investor in the 30% slab with surcharge and cess, a fully-taxed nominal 7% with 5% inflation can produce a *negative* real, post-tax return. This is not hypothetical pessimism; it is the arithmetic that makes asset allocation a first-order question rather than a detail. The next article in this series works through inflation mechanics; the tax series does the same for the tax side.

## Where each measure misleads

Each measure above is correct, and each is misused in a predictable way. The failure is rarely fabrication; it is a measure answering a question nobody asked.

| Measure | Question it answers | Where it misleads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arithmetic mean | The average of the yearly numbers | Overstates compounding whenever returns vary |
| Geometric mean / CAGR | The constant rate from start to end | Silent on path, cash flows and window choice |
| XIRR (money-weighted) | The rate on your actual dated cash flows | Confounded by your own deposit timing |
| Time-weighted | The growth of the asset between flows | Ignores the timing that produced your outcome |
| Nominal, gross | The change in value before costs, tax and inflation | Overstates what you keep |

## What a return number cannot tell you

Even a correctly labelled return leaves questions open, and it is worth being explicit about them:

- It is not a forecast. It is a fact about a window that has closed.
- It does not describe risk. Past volatility is a floor on future volatility, not a ceiling.
- It does not identify the source of the gain. Leverage, concentration, illiquidity and credit exposure all raise expected return by raising risk.
- It says nothing about position size. A 100% return on ₹1,000 and a 6% return on ₹10,00,000 are not comparable decisions, and neither is comparable to another investor's until costs, tax and currency are matched.

## Reading the next return claim you meet

A checklist that fits on an index card — apply it to any pitch, factsheet or forward:

1. **Which quantity is this?** Historical, contractual, model or speculation. If the answer is "all of the above", stop.
2. **Over what period, and why that period?** Windows that end at peaks flatter; windows chosen after the fact are marketing.
3. **Gross or net of what?** Costs, taxes and inflation — ask for each.
4. **What happens in the bad path?** Anyone can quote the average; ask how far below the trend the asset travelled, and what that did to investors who bought at the top.
5. **Who is the counterparty?** For contractual yields this is the whole question: a promise is only as strong as the party promising.
6. **What is the base rate?** Not the best case, not a screenshot — the distribution: how did most participants in this strategy actually do?

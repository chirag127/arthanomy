Every investment fraud in this library shares at least one feature, and this is it. Somewhere in the pitch, a market-linked outcome is presented as a certainty. The rate varies, the vehicle varies, the story varies — a plantation, a trading programme, a co-operative, a "vendor partnership", a bond-like instrument with a familiar name — but the sentence that does the selling is always about a return that will happen rather than a return that might.

The reason this is the universal red flag is not moral. It is arithmetic. A guarantee is a liability, and a liability must be met from somewhere: from earnings, from reserves, from a guarantor's assets, or from the next contributor. Only the last of those is unlimited, which is why schemes that promise returns are drawn towards it. This article explains why regulated market products cannot promise a return, which guarantees are real and how far they extend, and how to grade the return language in any pitch in about a minute.

## Explain simply

When someone tells you a return is guaranteed, they are telling you they will pay it whatever happens. That is a promise, and promises are paid out of something. A bank can promise deposit interest because it earns a spread and holds capital, and the law makes it keep both — within limits. A mutual fund cannot promise a return at all, because its returns come from markets that nobody controls, so it tells you what it is trying to do and shows you what has happened before. Anyone else promising a fixed return above what a deposit pays is either doing something the rules do not allow, or paying you with money that came from the last person who believed them. The words to watch are "guaranteed", "assured", "fixed", "safe", "we have never missed" and "indicative but consistent" — and the question to ask is always: who is legally obliged to pay this, and with what money?

## A guarantee is a liability, not a slogan

Start with the accounting. A promise to pay a fixed return creates an obligation that grows with the amount promised and with time. It sits somewhere: on a balance sheet, in a contract, in a guarantor's informal assurance, or in nobody's accounts at all. Where the obligation sits determines whether the guarantee means anything.

Four sources can meet such an obligation. **Earnings** from a real business — the only durable source. **Reserves** accumulated in good years — finite, and drawn down silently. **A guarantor's assets** — real only if the guarantor has them and the promise is enforceable against them. **New contributions** — unlimited until they stop, which is the definition of a Ponzi scheme: returns paid to earlier investors out of money contributed by later ones, sustained only by continuous new inflows.

A pitch tells you which source it depends on by how it answers one question: what earns the money that pays me? If the answer is left vague, the scheme is relying on the fourth.

## Why market-linked products cannot promise a return

Market-linked products have outcomes that depend on prices nobody controls. That is not a marketing position; it is why the regulatory framework for such products rests on disclosure, categorisation and risk labelling rather than on guaranteeing outcomes. A regulated pooled vehicle states an objective, an asset allocation and its past results. It cannot state a future return as a fact, because it has no mechanism to produce one.

This yields the operative test for any pitch. A product whose returns depend on markets cannot lawfully be sold with a promise of a return. So when a market-linked-looking product carries a fixed promise above a comparable deposit rate, one of three things is true: the product has been mislabelled and is not what it claims; the promise is being made by an entity that is not authorised to make it; or the return is being paid from new contributions rather than from the market.

The bound matters too. The reference point is not zero. A deposit yields interest contractually, and a savings product can promise a modest contractual return. The red flag is a promise that is *higher than what a genuine guarantee pays*, because higher than the guarantee means the extra has to come from risk that has been hidden rather than priced.

## The guarantees that are real, and how far they reach

Three categories of genuine guarantee are worth knowing, along with their edges.

**Bank deposits.** Interest is a contractual obligation of the bank, not a market outcome. Because banks fail, deposit insurance exists under the DICGC Act, and its protection applies per depositor per bank up to a stated limit. Two consequences follow. First, the guarantee is real but bounded, and above the limit you are an unsecured creditor of the bank. Second, the institution making the promise is supervised, which is what makes the promise more than a sentence.

**Sovereign obligations.** Obligations of the government carry the government's credit rather than a private promoter's. They are the closest thing to a guarantee available, and they still do not offer whatever rate you like: a sovereign pays what it pays.

**Contractual guarantee components in savings and insurance products.** Certain long-dated contracts define a guaranteed benefit in their own wording. That guarantee is a promise by the issuer, enforceable to the extent of the contract and the issuer's ability to pay. Its value depends on what the contract says, not on what the seller said.

Notice what these have in common: a named promisor, a document, capital and a supervisor. A "guarantee" without all four is a description of intent.

## The phrases that dodge the rule

When the hard word is unavailable, pitches use soft ones. Each has a specific function.

- **"Assured", "certain", "fixed".** Synonym substitution. The sales effect of "guaranteed" without the contractual meaning that word carries.
- **"Indicative", "expected", "targeted".** Pushes the claim into the model lane while the sales pitch treats it as contractual — the most common form of the category error.
- **"We have never missed a payout."** A statement about the past presented as a property of the future. Given that early payouts are exactly what a scheme must produce to recruit, punctuality is evidence of cash flow, not of earnings.
- **"Capital protected with upside."** Usually means the downside has been moved rather than removed: into illiquidity, into a lock-in, into a promoter's discretion, or into an asset whose valuation nobody checks.
- **"A share of profit, not a return."** A device to avoid the return promise while still being sold on a number. Whatever it is called, ask who computes the profit and who audits the computation.
- **"Guaranteed by the promoter personally."** Shifts the obligation to an individual whose assets have not been disclosed and cannot be reached quickly.
- **"SEBI registered", "RBI approved", "government backed".** Registration statements applied to the wrong entity or the wrong activity. The check is not whether some related entity is registered, but whether the entity making the promise is authorised to do what it is doing.
- **"Backed by escrow, insurance or an underwriter."** A serious claim with a document behind it, or a phrase with nothing behind it. Ask for the document and the counterparty's name.

A pitch that uses several of these is not being vague by accident. Vagueness is what lets the seller mean a guarantee while telling a court it meant an estimate.

## Grading any pitch on its return language

Four lanes exist for any statement about a return, and every legitimate product belongs to exactly one of them.

1. **Contractual.** A binding obligation to pay. Deposits, sovereign obligations, contractually defined benefits. Audit the promisor, not the number, and check the limit of any insurance or guarantee.
2. **Model-estimated.** An arithmetic projection from stated assumptions. Legitimate when the assumptions are printed beside the number and labelled as estimates, and worthless when the assumptions are hidden.
3. **Historical realised.** What actually happened over a stated period to a stated population. Useful evidence, never a promise, and sensitive to the choice of window and to survivorship.
4. **Speculative.** A possible outcome with no supporting track record and no obligation. Legitimate only when labelled as such.

The grading routine is therefore: identify the lane the pitch is *speaking* in, then the lane its wording *claims*. A statement that belongs in lane two or three but is presented in lane one is the red flag, and no further analysis is needed. This is the discipline the rest of this library applies to every return figure, and it is the fastest test available.

## Worked example: what a fixed promise implies

Two illustrative calculations, not a description of any real scheme.

First, the liability. Suppose a scheme collects ₹1 crore and promises 24% a year. The obligation is ₹24 lakh a year, before salaries, commissions to agents who brought the money, rent, and any cost of actually investing it:

$$ 0.24 \times 1,00,00,000 = 24,00,000 \text{ per year} $$

For those payments to come from earnings, the pool must earn more than 24% per year, net of everything, every year. Nothing regulated pays that as a certainty — which is precisely why the promise cannot come from a genuine guarantee.

Second, the promise's own arithmetic. A 24% return compounding for a decade turns ₹1 into about ₹8.59:

$$ 1.24^{10} \approx 8.59 $$

The number is not the point; the direction is. Compound promises grow faster than any sustainable source of payment, so a scheme that honours the promise for a few years and then cannot is not unlucky — it is following the arithmetic. If the money is not compounding at that rate inside a real business, the difference is arriving from the next set of contributors.

## The operating features that come with the promise

Return language is the first signal, and behaviour is the second. The features below appear together so often that seeing three of them is itself disqualifying.

- **Referral commissions.** Investors are paid for bringing investors, which makes growth a requirement rather than a consequence. Money-multiplier structures — pay to enter, be paid from later entrants — are the pure form, and several have been prosecuted in India as unauthorised collective investment schemes and money circulation schemes.
- **Smooth payouts on a fixed rhythm.** A monthly or quarterly payout that never varies is a cash-flow design, not an earnings result. Genuine businesses have variable months.
- **Social proof as the argument.** Screenshots, group chats, testimonials and a friend who was paid. All of them are consistent with a scheme paying early contributors precisely so that they will recruit.
- **Exit friction.** Withdrawals require a form, a penalty, a personal request, a delay, or a "notice period" that lengthens when many people ask. A scheme that must pay out of new inflows needs to slow outflows.
- **No audited accounts, or an auditor who is not independent.** Where the same person produces and explains the numbers, the numbers are not evidence.
- **A private contract instead of a regulated instrument.** Booking forms and service agreements give a contributor a contractual claim and nothing else. Where the structure pools money from many investors for a common purpose managed by a promoter, the arrangement has the shape of a collective investment scheme, and unregistered schemes of that kind are unlawful.

## Where to report

The first question is what was actually sold, because that determines which regulator has jurisdiction, and the pitch rarely makes it obvious. A pooled or market-linked product dressed as an investment belongs to the securities regulator, which publishes alerts on guaranteed-return schemes, unregistered advisers and unauthorised collective investment schemes: a complaint about a registered intermediary goes first to the entity and then to SEBI's SCORES grievance portal, while an unauthorised entity is reported through the route published alongside those alerts. A promise that took the shape of a deposit belongs instead to the banking regulator, because a securities registration says nothing about whether an entity may accept deposits; where the entity is a bank or an NBFC, the route is the RBI's centralised complaint portal at cms.rbi.org.in under the Reserve Bank – Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026, in force from 1 July 2026 and replacing the 2021 scheme (matters received before that date, and appeals and awards under the 2021 scheme, continue under it), with the complaint made first to the entity before the limitation period under the Limitation Act, 1963 expires. Deposit interest is contractual, but its insurance under the DICGC Act protects only up to a stated limit, above which the depositor is an unsecured creditor. In every case the conduct is also potentially criminal: cheating and criminal breach of trust fall under the criminal law in force since July 2024, and states maintain Economic Offences Wings for these cases. Where a misleading representation caused the loss, a consumer commission route exists in parallel.

Whatever the route, the evidence that matters is the pitch itself: screenshots of the offer, the document you signed, the receipts, the payout statements, and the identity of the account that received your money.

## What this does not tell you

This article does not tell you that every high return is fraudulent. High returns exist, and they are earned by taking risk — concentration, illiquidity, leverage, credit exposure or skill. What distinguishes them from a fraudulent promise is that the risk is disclosed, priced and borne by the investor rather than asserted away. It also does not resolve the hardest practical case, which is a genuine business that makes a confident projection and then fails; a failed forecast is not a fraud, though the language used to sell it may still have been misleading.

Nor does it tell you what happens to your money after a collapse. The realistic position is that the obligation was never funded, so there is little to recover, and the processes that follow rank claims and distribute whatever is realised rather than making investors whole. And it does not replace the contract: the document you sign, and the identity of the entity that signs it, are what a court will read.

## Open questions

Three questions remain open. Why do soft disclaimers — "assured", "indicative", "historically consistent" — survive enforcement better than blatant promises, and should the standard be what a listener reasonably understands rather than what the words technically mean? How much of the persistence of guaranteed-return schemes is explained by the absence of any comparable guaranteed product for savers who genuinely want certainty, and is that gap a policy problem rather than only a policing one? And could independent verification of payouts, rather than self-reported returns, be made a condition of advertising any pooled scheme?

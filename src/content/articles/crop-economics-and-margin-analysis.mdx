Most arguments about farming are arguments about a single subtraction that nobody has written down. A crop uses seed, fertiliser, water, labour, machinery and a season of the land's time, and it produces a quantity of something that sells for a price. Whether the activity paid is the difference between the two, expressed per acre and per season, and nothing in the experience of growing a good crop answers that question. A visibly heavy harvest can lose money, and a modest one can pay, because the outcome depends on the price and the cost as much as on the yield.

## Explain simply

Take the amount harvested, multiply by the price actually received, and subtract what it cost to produce. That is the margin. Every argument about whether a crop pays is an argument about those three numbers. The traps are that the yield used is often the best figure from a research plot rather than what the field actually produced, that the price used is often the market print rather than what reached the grower's account after deductions, and that the cost left out usually consists of the family's own labour and the value of the land itself.

## The identity, and why the denominator matters

The arithmetic is trivial; the discipline is in the definitions. Per acre and per season:

$$ \text{Margin} = (Y \times P) - C $$

where $Y$ is the quantity harvested per acre, $P$ is the net price realised per unit after all deductions, and $C$ is the cost of raising the crop. Once the margin is known, the more useful question is what it is a return on. The capital employed in a crop is not the paid-out cost alone; it is the working capital spent on inputs plus the value of the land that was occupied for the season, plus any machinery and irrigation assets dedicated to it. A margin of a few thousand rupees per acre looks healthy against a paid-out cost of tens of thousands and trivial against land that is worth lakhs per acre. Only the second comparison answers whether the activity is a good use of the money.

## Yield: a distribution, not a number

Yield is the least controllable of the three inputs and the one most often reported as a single flattering figure. Research institutions publish crop-yield ranges from trial stations, and those figures are valuable for establishing what a crop can do under favourable management. They are also measured under controlled conditions with timely irrigation, adequate nutrition and careful protection, which is precisely the set of conditions that a rain-fed field cannot guarantee. The correct use of such a range is as an upper bound and as a comparison across crops, not as the number that goes into the budget.

A budget therefore needs a distribution rather than a point. The realistic version asks what the crop yields in a good season, in a median season and in a poor one, and what the margin becomes in each, and it asks how often the poor season occurs. Yield variability is not a nuisance parameter to be averaged away; it is the reason crop revenue is volatile even when prices are stable.

## Cost of cultivation: paid-out cost, then the imputed lines

Cost is where most optimistic budgets are built, because the omitted items have no invoice. It helps to build the cost side in three layers.

**Paid-out cost.** Everything that requires a cash payment during the season: seed, fertiliser, plant protection, hired labour, hired machinery, fuel, irrigation charges, and interest on any crop loan.

**Imputed family labour.** Where the operator and the family provide the labour, no payment is made, but the labour has an alternative use. Cost-of-cultivation studies impute a value to it for exactly this reason. A budget that treats family labour as free is comparing a household's wages with another farm's costs, which is not a comparison.

**Imputed land rent and interest on owned capital.** Owned land has a rental value even where leasing is restricted, because the owner could in principle have leased it somewhere the law permits, or could have sold it and earned a return elsewhere. Owned machinery, a borewell and a pump have a user cost. Where tenancy law restricts leasing, the imputed rent is harder to observe, which is a measurement problem rather than an argument for treating it as zero.

Adding the last two layers is what separates a cash margin, which is a real and useful figure for a household deciding what to plant, from an economic margin, which is the figure that answers whether the crop pays relative to the alternatives.

## The gap between the printed price and the realised price

The price in the budget should be the net price realised, not the price quoted in the market, and the difference is larger than most budget templates allow. Between the field and the payment there is typically a chain of deductions and losses: commission or user charges at the market, transport, loading and unloading, grading and rejection of sub-standard lots, drying and shrinkage, and storage losses where produce is held. Where the sale is made at harvest under pressure to repay input credit, the realised price also reflects that timing rather than the season's best price.

Two consequences follow. First, a budget built on the quoted price overstates the margin by the whole of the gap. Second, the grower controls part of it. Grading, cleaning, drying to the specified moisture content and choosing a selling channel all affect the realised price, and instruments such as the electronic national market and warehouse receipts exist to widen the choice of where and when to sell. A warehouse receipt is a document of deposit that can support sale, pledge or financing of stored produce, which matters when the decision is between selling at harvest and holding for a better price.

## Break-even: two numbers that end the argument

Rearranging the identity gives the two figures that make a budget checkable against a single sale slip:

$$ \text{Break-even price} = \frac{C}{Y} \qquad \text{Break-even yield} = \frac{C}{P} $$

The break-even price is the lowest net price at which the crop covers its stated cost, and the break-even yield is the smallest harvest that does so at the stated price. Both are more useful than the margin itself, because both are observable in real time. If the price being offered in the market is below break-even, the season is loss-making regardless of how good the crop looked, and the decision becomes one about whether to hold, to sell through a different channel, or to accept the loss.

## Worked example: one acre, three views of the same crop

Every figure below is an illustrative calculation, chosen to show the arithmetic. Nothing here is a published cost, yield or price for any crop.

Take one acre with a paid-out cost of ₹30,000 for the season, a harvested quantity of 20 units, and a net realised price of ₹2,000 per unit.

- Gross value: 20 units at ₹2,000 each, or ₹40,000.
- Margin over paid-out cost: ₹40,000 less ₹30,000, or ₹10,000, which is 33 per cent of the paid-out cost.
- Break-even price at that yield: ₹30,000 divided by 20, or ₹1,500 per unit.
- Break-even yield at that price: ₹30,000 divided by ₹2,000, or 15 units.

Now add the imputed lines. Suppose the land used is worth an illustrative ₹8,00,000 and its imputed rent is taken at 2 per cent a year, which is ₹16,000, and the value of family labour for the season is taken at an illustrative ₹8,000. Full economic cost becomes ₹54,000 and the margin becomes ₹40,000 less ₹54,000, or a loss of ₹14,000. The same crop, the same harvest and the same price: a cash surplus of ₹10,000 and an economic loss of ₹14,000. The difference is not a trick of accounting. It is the difference between the cash the household has at the end of the season and the value of what it consumed to get there.

The denominator completes the picture. Capital employed is the ₹8,00,000 of land plus the ₹30,000 of working capital, or ₹8,30,000. The cash margin of ₹10,000 is a return of 1.20 per cent for the season. If prices and costs are broadly stable and the general price level is taken at an illustrative 5 per cent a year, that nominal return is a real return of $(1.0120 / 1.05) - 1 = -0.0362$, or minus 3.62 per cent. A price fall of 10 per cent, holding everything else constant, would take the gross value to ₹36,000 and the cash margin to ₹6,000, and the land-plus-capital comparison becomes plainly negative.

## Reading a margin in the right lane

Crop margins appear in several very different forms, and they should not be blended.

- **Historical realised.** Cost-of-cultivation studies report what margins actually were. These are the only observed figures in the set, and they apply to the period and sample from which they were drawn.
- **Contracted.** Where the crop is grown under a written agreement at an agreed price, part of the revenue is contractual. The contract fixes the price, not the yield.
- **Model-estimated.** A per-acre budget built from assumed yield, price and cost is a model, and its output is an estimate with assumptions attached.
- **Speculative.** A margin that depends on next season's price, a future policy decision or a hoped-for yield recovery is speculation. It may be right, but it is not a forecast with a standard error.

## Why the same crop pays in one district and not in another

Four differences explain most of the variation between two fields in the same season.

**Yield potential.** Soil, water, drainage and the length of the growing window set what the parcel can produce, and research ranges apply unevenly across them.

**Distance and access.** A field near a market with competing buyers faces a smaller deduction from its gross value than one that must absorb transport and a single buyer.

**Tenure.** An owner-operator bears the land's opportunity cost; a tenant also bears the rent. The same yield and price can leave one solvent and the other not.

**Scale and mechanisation.** Fixed costs per acre fall with area, which is why small holdings can show a positive cash margin and a negative economic margin at once.

## Risk: yield and price move together against the grower

The two components of revenue are not independent. A favourable season for a crop in a region raises yields across many fields at once, and the resulting supply increase can push prices down in the same season. In revenue terms the movements partially offset, which reduces the volatility of gross value relative to what the two variables would produce separately. The standard variance expression makes the point:

$$ \text{Var}\left(\frac{\Delta R}{R}\right) \approx \sigma_{Y}^{2} + \sigma_{P}^{2} + 2\rho\,\sigma_{Y}\sigma_{P} $$

where $\rho$ is the correlation between proportional changes in yield and price. When that correlation is negative, the two terms offset and revenue is more stable than the individual variations suggest.

Two practical responses follow. Diversification across crops with different seasonality and different demand reduces the chance that a single price falls against a single output. And crop insurance converts a yield loss into a claim, which changes the shape of the distribution rather than its median. The central scheme for notified crops in notified areas works on a shared-premium basis with the balance subsidised, and claims for yield loss are settled on the basis of crop-cutting experiments rather than the grower's own estimate. Premium rates, coverage and enrolment conditions are notified and revised from time to time, so the terms in force for a specific crop and season must be confirmed rather than assumed.

## How costs move, and why the real margin is the one that matters

Input and labour costs do not move together, and they do not move with the price of the crop. Fertiliser and fuel prices follow their own policy and global markets, hired labour follows rural wages, and machinery costs follow the cost of capital. Over a period, nominal margins can rise while real margins fall. That is why the consumer and wholesale price series published by the national statistics office matter to a farm budget: a nominal margin has to be divided by them before it can be compared with the previous decade's. Without that step, a margin that looks better than five years ago may simply be a smaller real sum.

## Open questions

- How much of the variation in realised price across districts in one season is explained by transport and market access rather than by measurable quality differences?

## The per-acre budget, built in this order

1. **Choose the unit.** One acre, one season, one crop. Mixing crops and seasons produces a number that answers nothing.
2. **Set quantity.** A realistic yield for a median season, with a good-season and a poor-season variant alongside it.
3. **Set the net price.** The expected price after commission, transport, grading and shrinkage, not the quoted market price.
4. **Build paid-out cost.** Every cash payment of the season, including interest on crop credit.
5. **Add the imputed lines.** Family labour, land rent and the user cost of owned machinery and irrigation.
6. **Compute the margin, then the break-even price and break-even yield.**
7. **Divide by capital employed.** Land value plus working capital, not paid-out cost.
8. **Deflate.** Convert the nominal margin into a real one using the published price series before comparing it with any other period.

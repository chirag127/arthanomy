A portfolio is not a single thing that can be handed over. It is a set of records held by several intermediaries — a depository for demat holdings, a registrar or asset management company for mutual fund folios, a bank for deposits, an insurer for policies — each keeping its own copy of who owns what. When the record-keeper's customer dies, every one of those copies must be changed by a defined procedure. Nomination is the standing instruction that makes the change possible. Transmission is the procedure that carries it out. Neither is investment analysis, and both are considerably easier to arrange while the person who must sign the form is still able to do so.

## Why nomination exists

An intermediary can only act on the record in front of it. If the registered holder dies without leaving it an instruction, the intermediary has no basis to move the asset to whoever turns up with a claim; it needs an authority it can point to. Nomination creates that authority in advance and in the form the intermediary can process.

Under SEBI's framework the facility is mandated across demat accounts and mutual fund folios, with comparable requirements existing for bank deposits and insurance. Its purpose is procedural rather than proprietary: it tells the depository, the registrar or the bank whom to deal with, so that a routine change of record does not have to wait on a slower legal route.

That framing matters, because it explains what nomination is good at and what it is not. It is excellent at giving an intermediary a defensible instruction. It is not a substitute for deciding how an estate should be divided.

## Nomination is not a will

A will states how an estate is to be distributed. A nomination tells an intermediary whom to act for. The two overlap in effect and are not the same instrument, and the careful position is that a nomination does not override succession law where a will or a legal-heir challenge exists.

The unresolved part is what the nominee actually receives. In ordinary practice the intermediary will deal with the registered nominee, because that is the instruction it holds. Whether the nominee then owns the asset absolutely, or holds it subject to the rights of the legal heirs, has been argued in different contexts and has not produced a single consistent answer across asset types. This article takes the intermediary-facing position — the one that determines what actually happens to the record — and flags the ownership question as one to verify with a professional rather than assume.

The practical consequence is that nomination and a will are complements, not alternatives. A nomination shortens the route. A will, and the succession documents that accompany it, decide the destination where the estate is contested.

## Where nominations need to be registered

Nomination is an account-level setting. There is no single register that covers everything, which is why the exercise is an inventory first and a form-filling exercise second.

| Holding | Where the nomination is recorded | Who acts on it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demat holdings | With the depository, through the depository participant | Depository participant, on behalf of the depository |
| Mutual fund units held in a folio | With the asset management company or its registrar, against the folio number | AMC or registrar |
| Bank deposits | With the bank holding the deposit | The bank |
| Insurance policies | With the insurer that issued the policy | The insurer |

The depository side is documented by the depositories themselves, which publish the mechanics of holding, the charge schedules and the transmission-on-death procedures that a survivor uses. The fund side is documented in the investor-awareness material that AMFI and the industry publish, where the folio is the unit of record and the nomination attaches to the folio rather than to the investor.

Other account types — pension accounts, small savings, employer arrangements — maintain their own versions of the same idea, with their own forms and their own definitions of who may receive the balance. The principle is general; the paperwork is not.

## How to register a nomination

The process is deliberately unglamorous, and it is worth doing once, properly, rather than repeatedly in a hurry.

1. **Build the inventory.** List every account that holds an investment, with its identifier: the demat account number and depository participant, the folio number and fund house, the deposit number payable at which branch, the policy number. Identifiers are what intermediaries search on, so an inventory without them is only a reminder.
2. **Obtain each intermediary's current nomination form or online flow.** Forms are intermediary-specific. Use the one the intermediary publishes or the flow inside its own authenticated application, not a form forwarded by an unknown party.
3. **Decide the allocation before filling in the form.** If more than one nominee is permitted, the shares must be stated and must add up to the whole. A form that allocates part of the holding leaves the remainder in the ambiguous condition the exercise was meant to remove.
4. **Record the nominee's details accurately.** Name in the form the intermediary can match against identity documents, date of birth where required, relationship, and contact details. Mismatched names are a common reason for a nomination to be refused at the worst possible moment.
5. **Submit, and keep the acknowledgement.** A nomination that was sent but not recorded does not exist as far as the intermediary is concerned.
6. **Confirm it appears on the record.** Check the account statement, the folio statement or the depository's holding statement for the nomination entry. Confirmation on the record is the only proof that counts.

The number of nominees permitted and the rules on allocating shares between them are set by the relevant regulator and depository and have been revised over time. Verify the current position with the specific intermediary rather than relying on a form that was correct some years ago.

## What transmission is

Transmission is what happens after death: the process by which a holding registered in the name of the deceased is moved into the name of the nominee, the survivor or the legal heir. It is a change of record, not a transfer for value, which is why it follows a documentary route rather than a market transaction.

Transmission is initiated by the claimant, not the intermediary. Nothing moves until someone tells the record-keeper that the holder has died and produces the evidence that the intermediary requires. That is the structural weakness of the whole system: an intermediary with a stale mobile number and no nomination has no way to know, and no way to act until it is told.

## The documents transmission usually needs

The list varies by intermediary, by asset type and by whether the claim rests on a nomination, a will or a succession route. The items below are the ones that recur; treat them as a starting point to be confirmed with each record-keeper.

- **Proof of death.** The death certificate, in the form the intermediary specifies, with attested copies where required.
- **A transmission request.** The intermediary's own transmission form, signed by the claimant.
- **The claimant's identity and address proof**, and the claimant's own know-your-customer details, because the record is being changed to a new person.
- **Evidence of the basis of the claim.** The registered nomination where one exists; otherwise the succession documents the intermediary prescribes.
- **Bank details** of the account into which the transferred holding or proceeds should be paid, in the name of the claimant.
- **The certificates, statement or holding proof** that identify the account being transmitted.

Where the claim is disputed, transmission stops being an administrative process and becomes a legal one. No intermediary will move a record between competing claimants on its own authority.

## Transmission with and without a nomination

| | With a valid nomination on record | Without a valid nomination |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who the intermediary deals with | The registered nominee | The person who establishes title under the documents the intermediary prescribes |
| Base evidence required | Proof of death, claimant identity, transmission request | The same, plus succession evidence |
| Likely route | Administrative, handled against the registered instruction | Succession route: a succession certificate, probate or another court-backed authority, or a simplified route where the intermediary permits one |
| Typical friction | Low, provided the nominee details match the records | Higher and less predictable, because the route depends on the documents available |
| Disputed estate | Can still be challenged by legal heirs | Frequently ends in the legal route |

Simplified routes exist. Some intermediaries allow a claim on an affidavit and indemnity, without a court order, and some of those routes carry value limits that are prescribed or set by the intermediary. Because those limits are thresholds that change, this article does not state any: confirm the current rule with the intermediary. The design intent is that small holdings should not require a court process, and the practical lesson is that a nomination keeps even a large holding on the administrative route.

## Joint holdings and the survivor route

Where an account is held jointly, the mode of holding decides the survivor's position, and it is a separate mechanism from nomination. Holdings are commonly recorded with a survivorship instruction so that the surviving holder continues as holder; the intermediary then acts on that instruction rather than on a nomination.

The trap is assuming that the recorded mode is the one intended. Joint holding, either-or-survivor holding and nomination are different arrangements with different consequences, and a depository participant or registrar will act on what the account record says, not on what the family believed. Reading the holding statement and confirming the mode of holding is part of the inventory exercise above.

## Keeping the picture current

Nomination is not a one-time form. It is a set of instructions that decay as circumstances change, and it is worth revisiting on a fixed rhythm.

- **Refresh after every major life event:** marriage, the birth of a child, a death in the family, divorce or separation, and any change in who is financially dependent.
- **Check for lapsed nominations.** A nominee who has died leaves the account in the same position as one that was never nominated.
- **Keep contact details current.** The intermediary's registered mobile number and email address are how a transmission conversation starts; an obsolete number is a practical barrier, and it is also an account-security weakness in its own right.
- **Keep the inventory and the nominations consistent.** If a folio names one person and a demat account names another, the estate bears the cost of the inconsistency.
- **Do it at the annual review.** Folding nomination into the yearly portfolio review is cheaper than running a separate exercise, and it puts the check where the records are already being opened.

## Explain simply

Nomination is a form that tells your broker, depository, fund house, bank or insurer who is allowed to receive your investment if you die. It is not the same as a will. It does not decide how your estate is divided, and it can be challenged. Transmission is what happens next: the person who survives you sends the death certificate and their own identity papers to each record-keeper and asks for the holding to be moved into their name. Doing this now costs an afternoon, across accounts you can list while you are alive. Doing it later means working from whatever records survive you, with the account details you can reconstruct and the documents each intermediary decides it needs.

## Worked example

The following is an illustrative calculation, not observed data and not a prediction. It shows how a nominee allocation is meant to read, and what changes when the allocation is missing.

Suppose a demat account and a mutual fund folio together hold an illustrative value of ₹10,00,000, and the holder registers three nominees with shares of 50 per cent, 30 per cent and 20 per cent. On transmission, the allocation is arithmetic:

- First nominee: 0.50 × ₹10,00,000 = ₹5,00,000
- Second nominee: 0.30 × ₹10,00,000 = ₹3,00,000
- Third nominee: 0.20 × ₹10,00,000 = ₹2,00,000

The three shares sum to ₹10,00,000, that is, to the whole holding. A form that names the same three people but allocates only 90 per cent of the holding leaves the remaining 10 per cent — ₹1,00,000 on these illustrative figures — to be resolved by the intermediary's default rule or by a succession route, which is exactly the outcome the nomination was meant to prevent.

Now change one assumption: the nomination was never registered. The same ₹10,00,000 does not vanish, but the route to it changes. Instead of an administrative transmission against a registered instruction, the survivor must establish title using whatever documents the intermediary prescribes, which on an intestate and undocumented account may mean a court-backed authority. The arithmetic of the holding is unchanged; what changes is the number of months, the professional fees and the number of signatories involved. Those costs are not in the table above because they depend entirely on documents that this illustration does not assume.

## What this does not tell you

- **It is not legal advice.** Whether a nomination defeats a will, who is beneficially entitled as against a nominee, and what happens in a contested estate are legal questions with outcomes that vary by asset type and by the facts. A professional should be consulted before relying on any of it.
- **It states no thresholds.** The permitted number of nominees, share-allocation rules and any value limits on simplified transmission routes are regulatory or intermediary settings that change. They are deliberately not quoted here.
- **It does not cover assets held abroad**, or assets held through structures such as a company, a trust or a partnership, each of which has its own succession route.
- **It does not cover the tax treatment of the recipient.** Receiving an asset on transmission and later disposing of it are separate questions with their own rules.
- **It does not measure the cost of delay.** The time an asset spends outside the survivor's control, and the market exposure it carries during that period, depend on the case and are not estimated here.

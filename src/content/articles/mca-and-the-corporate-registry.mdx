Every credit decision, investment and contract begins with a question of identity: is the company on the other side real, who controls it, and what has it already pledged? India answers that question through a statutory register of companies maintained by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. The register is not a rating, an audit or a guarantee. It is a record of what companies have filed, and of what the law requires them to file. This article, part of the Indian Financial System series, explains what the registry holds, how an investor or lender can read it, and the precise difference between what a filing proves and what it merely reports.

## Explain simply

The registry is a public filing cabinet for companies. When a company is incorporated, borrows, changes its directors, pledges assets as security, or closes its accounts, the law requires it to file something with the ministry, and that filing becomes part of the public record. You can look up a company and see its incorporation details, its directors, its annual accounts and the charges registered against its assets. The catch is that the registry records what was filed, not whether it is true. A company that files accounts is not necessarily a company that is doing well, and a registry search that comes back clean is not a credit opinion. It is evidence that a filing was made, which is useful, and it is not the same as proof that the filing is accurate.

## The registry and what it holds

The Ministry of Corporate Affairs administers the registry through its MCA21 platform. The word to hold on to is *master data*: the registry is the reference point for the identity and status of companies and for the statutory filings they make. It is not a database of opinion, and it is not a database of transactions. It is a database of filings.

Four kinds of information matter to a reader:

- **Incorporation and registration details.** Whether the entity exists, when it was created, and what its registered status is.
- **Director and key personnel details.** Who is accountable, and whether the same individuals appear across several entities.
- **Annual accounts and returns.** What the company has reported about its finances, subject to the limits of any filing.
- **Charge registrations.** What assets have been pledged as security, to whom, and when.

The registry's authority comes from statute: companies are required to file, and the ministry maintains the record. What the record does not do is verify the substance of a filing. It holds the document; it does not certify the document's truth.

## What an investor or lender can inspect

Each item in the registry answers a different question, and the questions are often confused.

Incorporation details answer *does this entity exist and how long has it existed*. A young company with a short trading history is a different risk from a long-established one, and the registry is where you establish the difference.

Director details answer *who is accountable*. A reader can see the individuals associated with a company and can look for the same names across several companies. Concentration of directorships is a fact to note, not a conclusion in itself; it becomes a conclusion only when combined with what those other companies are and how they have performed.

Charge registrations answer *what has already been pledged*. This is the item most relevant to a lender, and it is where the registry does its most concrete work.

Annual accounts answer *what has the company reported about its finances*. The answer is bounded by what a filing can contain. The accounts are the company's own reported numbers, and the registry's role is to hold them, not to audit them.

## What the registry proves, and what it only reports

This is the distinction the whole article turns on. The registry **proves** that a filing was made and what the filing says. It **reports** the company's own account of itself. It does not prove that the company is solvent, that its revenue is real, that its directors are suitable, or that its assets are unencumbered.

A registry search is therefore best understood as a first filter. It can rule things out: a company that does not exist, a charge that was never registered, a directorship that was never disclosed. It cannot rule things in. A company can be fully compliant with its filing obligations and still be a poor credit risk, because compliance measures process, not performance.

The practical consequence is that a clean registry search should never be presented, or read, as a verification. It is a source of specific, checkable facts. Whether those facts are reassuring is a separate judgement that the registry does not make.

## Charges, security and the order of creditors

Charges matter because they decide who is paid first. When a company borrows against its assets, the security it grants is recorded as a charge, and that charge is registered. A registered charge is a public signal that a prior claim exists over the asset pool.

For a new lender, the question is simple: how much of the company's assets are already spoken for? A registry search that reveals existing charges tells the lender something concrete about the position it would be joining. The exact ranking of creditors and the amount actually recovered are determined later, and if the company fails they are determined in the insolvency process rather than by the registry itself.

This is why a charge search belongs at the start of a lending decision rather than at the end. It is cheap, it is public, and it can change the structure of the transaction before any money moves.

## Where the registry ends and insolvency begins

The registry records the company as a going concern. When a company cannot pay its debts as they fall due, a different framework takes over. Under the insolvency code, financial creditors can trigger a resolution process, the management is displaced by a resolution professional, a moratorium on debt recovery applies, and the process ends in a resolution plan or liquidation. The tribunal overseeing these proceedings is where recovery is determined.

The two records sit side by side. The registry holds the company's statutory history; the insolvency process determines what its creditors actually receive. A reader who has only looked at the registry has seen the company's past. A reader who has looked at the insolvency records has seen its failure. Neither substitutes for the other, and a company with a long, clean registry can still end up in a resolution process.

## Worked example: a lender's registry check

The figures below are an illustrative calculation on hypothetical numbers, chosen to show the method rather than to describe any real company.

Suppose a lender is asked to advance ₹50,00,000 to a company against its assets. A registry search shows charges already registered in favour of other lenders totalling ₹30,00,000, and the lender's own assessment values the company's encumbered assets at ₹60,00,000. The residual claim available to everyone behind the existing charges is:

$$\text{residual} = \text{asset value} - \text{existing charges} = 60,00,000 - 30,00,000 = 30,00,000$$

That residual is only 60 per cent of the proposed ₹50,00,000 advance, so ₹20,00,000 of the loan would sit behind claims that are already secured. If the assets were later worth less than the existing charges, the new lender could recover nothing at all.

This is an illustrative calculation, not a valuation of any company. Its purpose is to show why a registry search is a first step in sizing a secured position rather than a substitute for one: the search tells you what is already pledged, and the lender's own work must still establish what the assets are worth.

## Using the registry in due diligence

A sensible sequence runs in this order. First, confirm the company's existence, status and incorporation history. Second, identify the directors and look for the same individuals across other entities. Third, search the charges and establish what has already been pledged. Fourth, read the annual accounts as filed, treating them as one input rather than a verdict. Fifth, if the company is listed or has issued securities, read the market-level disclosures that sit alongside the registry record, because a listed company publishes information that a private company does not.

At each step the discipline is the same: the registry supplies facts, and the reader supplies the judgement. The registry can tell you that a charge exists. It cannot tell you whether the charge is a good idea.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the structure and use of the public corporate registry. It does not verify any individual company, and it does not quote filing fees, form numbers, thresholds or company counts, because those are specific and change. It does not assess whether a company is a good borrower or investment, which depends on cash flows, collateral values and market conditions that the registry does not contain.

It also does not cover the market-facing disclosures of listed companies, which are a separate layer of information, or the detailed mechanics of the insolvency process, which this series treats elsewhere. The registry is the statutory backbone; it is not the whole body.

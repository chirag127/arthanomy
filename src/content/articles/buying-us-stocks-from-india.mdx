There is nothing complicated about buying a single US share. The interface looks like any brokerage, the order form looks like any order form, and the confirmation arrives in seconds. What is complicated is everything that sits behind it: a market that opens and closes on a different calendar and during different hours, a settlement system with its own rhythm, a depository chain in a different jurisdiction, an account that has to be funded through a cross-border remittance, dividends that arrive with a tax withheld at source, and an Indian tax return that insists on knowing about every one of these assets every year. The investor who operates this route competently is not the one with the best stock ideas but the one who runs the process reliably.

This is the fourth article in the Global Investing series. It describes the day-to-day experience rather than the strategy: the mechanics of the trade, the currency, the dividends, the corporate actions, and the reconciliation that has to be done at the end of the year. No rate, charge, date or investable figure is asserted as current, because each comes from the individual broker, market or regulator's published rules and changes on its own schedule.

## The market access set-up

Access to US markets from India runs through a broker, directly or through a domestic platform. The broker holds the account, routes orders, and handles the US side's clearing and settlement infrastructure, and the money is handled by the authorised dealer bank before it arrives at the broker.

What matters operationally is to understand which party is accountable for which step. The bank is accountable for the foreign-exchange reporting of the remittance and any collection at source. The broker is accountable for order handling, custody, statements and the foreign tax paperwork relevant to the investor's residency. The Indian depository and its domestic record-keeping system do not hold the position: the US chain does. That difference is the crux of most confusion when something goes wrong, because the first place an investor checks is often the wrong institution.

The domestic depository system in India, operated by the National Securities Depository Limited and the Central Depository Services (India) Limited, is the model most Indian investors know: assets held in electronic form, identified by an international securities identification number, charges published, pledge and transmission rules prescribed. Foreign holdings are not held there. They sit in the foreign depository chain accessible through the broker, which is why ISIN-level familiarity from domestic investing does not map cleanly across.

## Order handling and trade execution

US equities trade in standard types of order familiar to an investor used to any other market: market orders, limit orders, stop-loss orders and stop-limit orders, among others. The practical differences are temporal rather than technical.

The US trading session is offset from Indian time, so that active participation in the market means working in the Indian evening and late night for the most part. An order placed while the US market is closed queues for the open. Market orders executed at the open or in the first few minutes after it risk receiving a price that is worse than the quoted close, because the market is repricing overnight information quickly. Limit orders give control over the price but can miss the trade if the order is left too tight against a fast-moving stock. These are not exotic observations; they are the normal behaviour of any limit-order economy when accessed across time zones.

Fractional-share trading, offered by some platforms, allows the investor to buy a currency amount rather than a whole share. The mechanics appear identical at purchase, but the holding is held and serviced by the broker rather than by the underlying foreign depository system in the standard way. Dividends on fractional positions, corporate actions on those positions, and disposal of small residual balances are handled by the broker's own rules, which affect both tax timing and record-keeping and should be read when choosing the platform.

## Settlement and the cash timeline

The US market operates on its own settlement schedule, which determines when cash becomes available for reuse and when securities become drawable. The specifics are published by the US market's clearing and settlement system and change only occasionally, but they are not the same as the Indian settlement cycle, and planning two markets together requires knowing the gap between them. Cash sent abroad to fund a trade is out of the Indian account before the trade settles abroad; proceeds from a sale abroad are out of the foreign account before they can be repatriated and credited in India.

The cash exists simultaneously in two places in a different state: reserved abroad pending settlement or reinvestment, and credited in India only after the full process of repatriation and foreign-exchange reporting is complete. Most operational frustration with a direct US account comes from the two end-to-end chains refusing to line up on the day they were expected to. The fix is not a better platform; it is a calendar of settlement dates kept for the account, updated from the broker's confirmations rather than from the trade date alone.

## Currency conversion as cost and as return

Every Indian investor in US equities owns two assets: the underlying stock and the currency. The conversion happens at least three times over the life of a position: when capital is sent out to fund it, when dividends are converted, and when proceeds come back. Each conversion carries a bid-ask spread, a margin set by the bank or platform, and sometimes a wire or telegraphic-transfer fee. These spreads are a real cost, differ across providers and are not always shown as a line item on the trade confirmation.

Currency is also a return, in the same way that the US market is an equity return, and the two compound together rather than add. A 10 percent gain in the dollar price combined with a 5 percent fall in the dollar against the rupee is not a 15 percent rupee gain; the composition is multiplicative. The currency-impact calculation is therefore necessary to answer even the basic question of how the investment has performed in the investor's own currency, and to separate what was investment skill or exposure from what was exchange-rate movement.

The practical habit is to record the rupee-converted cost of every purchase, every dividend received and every sale, with the conversion rate the transaction actually occurred at, rather than with a representative or average rate. Using an average rate hides both the cost and the source of the difference, and makes the position difficult to value or to reconcile.

## Dividends and withholding

US companies pay dividends in US dollars. Before the dividend reaches the Indian investor, a withholding tax is applied in the United States as a withholding on dividends paid to a non-resident, at the rate applicable to the investor's treaty status. The rate is not fixed for all non-US holders; it depends on the investor's classification and on the applicable tax treaty between the United States and India, and these matters are settled through the tax-declaration form required from non-US investors before the dividend is attributed. The form affects both the withholding and the declaration, and it needs to be submitted, renewed and kept current in accordance with the issuing-country's rules.

The residual Indian tax is then computed on the dividend under the investor's total income. The foreign tax withheld against the dividend is generally available as a credit against the Indian liability attributed to that income, subject to the time and evidence rules and to the limitation that ties the credit to the Indian tax on that income. Excess withholding that is not creditable in the year is a loss unless the credit window for a correction is available, which places a premium on getting the withholding declaration right at the outset rather than repairing it later.

Dividend reinvestment, if offered by the broker in the form of additional fractional shares, can quietly accumulate and create both tax-timing and reconciliation problems, particularly where reinvestment is partial or in fractions and the acquiring transaction is not logged in the Indian record as a new purchase. Cash dividends in Indian rupees, received by conversion, keep the position simpler because they do not denominate as a new foreign transaction and are more straightforward to reconcile.

## Corporate actions

A long-term holder of US equities will eventually encounter a corporate action: a dividend that changes amount or type, a stock split, a reverse split, a merger or an acquisition, a delisting, a spin-off, a rights issue, or a change in the share register following a reorganisation. Each of these is handled under US market rules, which differ from the Indian rules in both timing and documentation, and the delivery of value to the investor is done by the broker on the foreign chain rather than by the Indian holding system.

The operational follow-ups are predictable. A stock split changes the number of shares and the per-share cost basis, without changing the total cost, but it has to be recorded exactly as against the average-cost calculation. A merger or cash acquisition ends the position and leaves cash in the foreign account that has to be repatriated or reinvested, and it converts a long-term holding into a single taxable event in both the US and Indian reporting. A spin-off creates a new holding whose cost basis has to be allocated from the original, and an allocation that is not reasoned through at the time tends to remain wrong. A rights issue requires the investor to decide whether to subscribe, accept or sell within a short window set by the US market, which may or may not be announced in time for an investor outside the jurisdiction.

Most losses and most errors in direct US share portfolios are not from bad stock calls but from corporate actions that were not diarised, followed up or recorded in the Indian return. The remedy is administrative and should be administrative before it becomes advisory: a calendar, an inbox rule for broker emails, and a routine of recording every event on the day it arrives.

## Reporting: the Indian layer on top

The foreign holding does not escape the Indian tax and disclosure framework.

- **Foreign assets must be declared.** A resident holding foreign assets must disclose them in the schedule in the annual income-tax return in which foreign assets are reported, with details of the holding, the income and the nature of the interest, for any year in which the assets were held. This schedule is not optional, and a return that omits it while claiming a credit or reporting dividend income is facially incomplete.
- **Income is taxable in India.** Dividends and capital gains on foreign securities are reported and taxed under the relevant heads and rates for the year. Long-term and short-term classifications, and any holding-period thresholds, apply on the basis set out in the Income-tax Act; the exact treatment should be taken from the current official text rather than from a secondary summary.
- **Foreign tax credit.** Tax withheld in the United States against dividends or other income may be credited against the Indian liability attributable to that income under the rules in the Income-tax Act read with the applicable treaty, and the credit is limited in the usual way to the tax attributable to that income. The credit is claimed with the foreign tax payment evidence, such as the withholding statement from the broker, through the prescribed procedural route and in the year to which it attaches.
- **Tax residency evidence.** Treaty benefits usually require evidence of the investor's tax residency, raised in advance rather than after the income arrives, and any required declarations about beneficial ownership or permanent establishment where prescribed.

Two common failure patterns deserve naming. The first is filing the Indian return as though the foreign holding were a domestic one, which does not capture the currency conversion correctly and which usually fails the disclosure step. The second is claiming the foreign tax credit without attaching the evidence or in a different year, both of which result in the credit being deferred or denied.

The Indian side does not hold or value the security; it requires the investor to do so. Keeping the broker's year-end statement as the anchor document and reconciling it to the amounts actually received in rupees is the simplest way to make the two reports agree.

## Comparing the direct route to a domestic international fund

The comparison of magnitudes between a direct US portfolio and a domestic international fund requires current figures and is not made here. The comparison of types is more robust and is set out below:

| Dimension | Direct US brokerage | Domestic international fund |
|---|---|---|
| Asset held | Individual US securities | Fund units in India |
| Custody | Foreign chain through broker | Fund and its custodian |
| Currency control | Full, per investor | None: fund-managed |
| Income type | Direct foreign dividends and capital gains | Fund-determined distribution |
| Indian tax reporting | Direct foreign-asset disclosure required | Domestic holding, no foreign-asset declaration for the investor |
| Foreign withholding | Direct, with credit position | Inside the fund, passed through distribution rules |
| Choice of exposure | Broad: individual securities and sectors | Limited to the fund's mandate |
| Operational load | High and continuous | Low and annual |
| Customisation | High | None beyond fund choice |

The direct route is right for an investor who wants specific exposures, runs the process reliably, and holds a sum for which the conversion costs are immaterial. The domestic fund is right for an investor who wants the exposure, does not want to operate a foreign account, and is content with fund-level expenses in exchange for the removal of the compliance layer. Neither choice is an error; they allocate time and complexity differently across the lifetime of the holding.

## A routine that survives, for the direct route

The investors who run direct US portfolios without mishap are usually not excited about it. They run a routine.

- **A single funding calendar.** One or two remittances a year rather than many small ones, where the purpose and the limit structure permits, so that the conversion and the paperwork occur once.
- **A single account file.** Remittance advices, collection records, order confirmations, settlement confirmations, quarterly statements, foreign tax vouchers and dividend receipts in one place, named by tax year.
- **A corporate-action inbox rule.** Broker emails auto-filed on arrival and reviewed weekly, rather than whenever the investor next opens the app.
- **A reconciliation at year end.** Broker year-end statement against rupee records, with currency conversion rates and conversion cost, run before the return is filed rather than during it.
- **A disclosure checklist.** Run as a short checklist against the annual return, including the foreign-asset declaration and the foreign-tax credit claim, prepared from the account file rather than from memory.

The routine is deliberately unglamorous. It is also the difference between an exposure that compounds and an exposure that quietly accumulates unresolved records.

## Explain simply

Operating a direct US equity account from India is not an investment skill so much as an operations skill. The trade is quick, but its consequences spread across the US market's calendar, the foreign custody chain, the investor's currency position, the US tax system via the withholding at source, and the Indian tax and disclosure framework. The unhurried part is the investing, and it is also the cheap part to get wrong in the sense that a wrong stock is easy to notice and a wrong record is not. The practical discipline is identical to every other investor-with-paper, but the number of calendars in play is higher: a funding date, a settlement date, a corporate-action date, a tax-filing date, and the period during which the foreign withholding return or correction window is open. Miss any one and the position may be fine and the record will not be.

## Worked example

An illustrative calculation, the figures invented to show the currency contribution. The stock price and the exchange rate are both invented and are not representations of any security or market level.

A purchase is made of 100 units of a fictitious US security at 50 US dollars per unit, for a total of 5,000 US dollars, funded by a remittance at 83 rupees per US dollar. The rupee cost is therefore 4,15,000 rupees.

One year later the share price is 55 US dollars per unit, a 10 percent gain in the foreign currency, and the dollar is at 86 rupees, a rupee depreciation of 3.6 percent. The proceeds are 5,500 US dollars, converted at 86 rupees, or 4,73,000 rupees. The rupee return is 4,73,000 divided by 4,15,000 minus 1, which is approximately 14.0 percent. The gain in the foreign stock contributed 10 percentage points and the currency contributed roughly 3.6 plus the interaction, which is approximately the difference between the rupee return and the dollar return. In this illustration the currency covered a third of the gain and added roughly 50 basis points through the interaction term.

If the dollar had instead appreciated to 80 rupees over the year, the same 10 percent stock gain would produce 4,40,000 rupees from 5,500 dollars, a rupee return of 6.0 percent, below even a low domestic alternative. The arithmetic is the reason the currency contribution is measured rather than guessed, and the reason a US position should be weighed as a bet on both the stock market and the exchange rate. In neither case is the stock's own return at fault; the composition is identical in construction and opposite in experience.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes mechanism, not advice. It does not recommend any security, platform, market or allocation and it does not evaluate any broker. It contains no broker selection criteria because the correct broker for an individual depends on needs, sizes, preferences and regulatory coverage that a general article cannot weigh.

Three gaps are named explicitly. First, the service-level, fee and custody arrangements of any platform are extrinsic to this text and change; no figure or comparison is asserted, because both would be wrong about some providers and useful only about the others. Second, the article does not cover repatriation planning, the treatment of a foreign position on death or succession, or the interaction of Indian and US inheritance law, each of which requires specialist advice and is outside this brief. Third, the US withholding and income-tax treatment is described at the mechanism level; the individual's own computation, including the correct classification, the claim for credit, and the handling of any excess credit, requires the current official text and the person's own tax adviser.

An emergency fund is the cheapest form of financial resilience available to a household, and the least glamorous. Its purpose is narrow and specific: to make sure that an unexpected expense or a loss of income is met from cash rather than by selling a long-horizon investment at whatever price happens to be on offer that week. Everything else — the number of months, the instruments, the interaction with credit — follows from that single purpose.

This is the fifth entry in the Operating a Portfolio series. It sits after the cash-flow plan, because sizing the reserve requires knowing the household's required outflows, and it feeds back into the policy statement, because the reserve is a constraint that the rest of the portfolio must respect.

## Explain simply

An emergency fund is cash set aside so that an unexpected expense or a lost income does not force a sale of investments at a bad moment. It is sized on the payments that must keep being made, not on salary or total spending, and it should cover enough months to absorb a shock without borrowing. It must sit in instruments whose value is certain on the day it is needed, which rules out shares and locked-in products. A credit line can help, but it cannot be the fund, because credit can be withdrawn exactly when trouble arrives.

## What the reserve is actually for

The reserve buys three things. It buys **time**, so that a temporary loss of income does not become a permanent one, because decisions made from a position of liquidity are better than decisions made under pressure. It buys **optionality**, because a household with cash can decline a bad offer, whether that is a low price for an asset or a poor employment term. And it buys **protection of the rest of the plan**, by removing the main mechanism through which a well-designed portfolio gets dismantled — a forced sale at the bottom.

That third function is the one that makes the reserve a portfolio decision rather than a budgeting detail. Without a reserve, an equity portfolio is not really a long-horizon portfolio; it is a portfolio that will be liquidated on the first significant shock. The reserve is what lets the long-horizon assets stay long-horizon.

## The correlation problem

The reason the reserve cannot be held in the very assets it protects is that shocks tend to arrive together. A downturn that costs jobs is often the same downturn that marks down equity, and the sectors most exposed to the slowdown are frequently the ones whose prices have fallen most. The household that must sell growth assets to pay for a job loss sells them into the weakness, realising the loss and permanently forgoing the recovery.

This is not pessimism, it is a description of why the reserve is a different instrument from the portfolio. Reserve assets are chosen for the certainty of their value on a known date, not for their expected return. The value of that certainty is easiest to see in the moment it is used, and hardest to remember when markets are calm.

## Sizing: what to count as required outflow

The reserve is sized against required outflows, not against income or total spending. Required outflows are the payments that must be met to keep the household solvent and functioning if income stopped:

- Loan instalments, including home, vehicle and personal loans, and any minimum credit-card payments.
- Insurance premiums, especially health and term cover, whose lapse would remove protection in the middle of a crisis.
- Rent or maintenance and property charges.
- Utilities, communications and transport needed for work.
- Baseline food, medical and education commitments.
- Regular support the household provides to dependants.

Discretionary spending is excluded on the reasoning that it can be cut under pressure. The exclusion is not a claim that the household will enjoy cutting it, only that the reserve is protecting against insolvency rather than against discomfort. Getting this number right is the hard part, and the cash-flow planning article in this series is the companion that produces it.

## Sizing: how many months

The number of months the reserve should cover is a household-specific judgement, and the mistake is to adopt a rule of thumb without stating why. Conventional guidance spans a few months for a very secure household to more than a year for a fragile one; the useful work is in deciding where the household sits and recording the reason. Five factors do most of the moving:

| Factor | Moves the reserve down | Moves the reserve up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income stability | Two secure salaries | Single income, freelance, business |
| Dependants | None | Children, elderly parents |
| Fixed obligations | Low, flexible | Large instalments, school fees |
| Other liquidity | Access to family, other reserves | None |
| Health exposure | Strong cover and buffer | High out-of-pocket exposure |

The size should be stated in the policy as a target, with a band, so that it can be checked monthly rather than renegotiated at the moment of need. That is the natural place to record the household's answer to the question, and the reason the reserve appears as a line in the policy statement.

## Where to hold it

The test for a reserve instrument is not its return; it is whether the money will be intact and reachable on the day it is needed. On that test:

- **Bank savings balances** qualify on availability but typically earn the least.
- **Liquid and overnight fund categories** offer same-day or next-day access and are designed for exactly this use; their value can move slightly, and exit rules and load structures should be read.
- **Short-tenor bank deposits** qualify but must be structured for access. A ladder of deposits maturing in successive months keeps money reachable without breaking a single long deposit, and staggered maturities mean only one tranche is exposed to a penalty if it must be broken.
- **Cash at home** qualifies for a small operational amount, but carries a different risk.

What does not qualify is worth stating plainly, because the temptation is real. Equity funds, sector or thematic funds, lock-in instruments, property, and any holding whose value depends on a market price or a buyer are unavailable on a known date. Securities and fund units are held and identified at the depository and scheme level, which is precisely why they cannot be treated as instant liquidity: transfer and redemption take time and settle at a price the household does not control.

The yield on reserve instruments is a matter of published bank deposit rate statistics, and it changes as deposit rates adjust. Those rates move with policy and liquidity conditions and do not move in the same direction for borrowers and savers, which is why the reserve should be expected to earn a modest, changing amount rather than a fixed one. The reserve is not an investment, and its return should not be the criterion used to choose between qualifying instruments.

## Credit lines: a top-up, not the reserve

A credit line, overdraft or credit-card limit can usefully supplement a reserve, but it cannot replace it, for three reasons that are structural rather than moral.

First, credit can be withdrawn or repriced exactly when the household most needs it, because a stress severe enough to threaten the household's income often coincides with lenders tightening. A facility that exists in calm markets is not guaranteed to exist in the month it is needed. Second, the cost of borrowing exceeds the return on reserves — published statistics show a spread between term-deposit and lending rates — so covering a shock with credit makes the household pay that spread at the worst time. Third, using credit converts a temporary shock into a scheduled obligation, which is the opposite of what the reserve is for.

The sensible arrangement is explicit and written into the policy: the reserve is the primary instrument, and a credit facility is a stated secondary layer with a defined purpose and a repayment plan. That way the household knows, in advance, which one it intends to use and what happens next.

## Building and rebuilding the reserve

With the target set, building it is a process with three levers:

1. **Automate a transfer on the day income arrives.** A standing instruction from the main account to the reserve account, before discretionary spending can absorb the money.
2. **Direct windfalls to it until the target is met.** Bonuses, refunds and gifts close the gap faster than monthly discipline alone.
3. **Hold it in a separate, labelled account** so that its balance is visible and not confused with everyday funds.

A starter reserve matters more than a perfect one. The usual sequence is a minimal buffer first, then expensive debt, then the full reserve; the minimal buffer exists so that an ordinary mishap does not become new high-cost borrowing. Rebuilding after use is part of the plan and should be written as such, because a reserve that is spent and never refilled has merely delayed the problem.

## Worked example: sizing and splitting the reserve

The following is an illustrative calculation, not observed data or a recommendation. Suppose a household's required outflows are ₹75,000 a month and the policy target is six months. The reserve target is:

$$ 75{,}000 \times 6 = 4{,}50{,}000 $$

so an illustrative ₹4,50,000. A workable split across qualifying instruments might be ₹50,000 in a savings balance for immediate needs, ₹1,50,000 in a liquid fund, and ₹2,50,000 in a ladder of short deposits maturing in successive months. If the reserve earns an illustrative 6% and the same money invested over the long run would have earned an illustrative 10%, the annual opportunity cost is:

$$ 4{,}50{,}000 \times (10\% - 6\%) = 18{,}000 $$

The Opportunity Cost calculator shows the same gap as a compounding figure over a full holding period. Two observations follow. The gap is the premium paid for not being a forced seller, and it is small relative to the damage a forced sale at the bottom of a drawdown can do. And the figure depends entirely on the assumed rates, which is why the reserve should be sized on required outflows and access, not on the gap.

The SWP calculator is the other side of the same planning question: how long a corpus lasts at a given withdrawal rate, which is what turns an under-sized reserve into a visible timeline rather than a vague discomfort.

## What this does not tell you

This article supplies a framework, not a number. The required outflow, the month count and the split between instruments are household judgements, and none of them can be verified in advance. Nor does the reserve substitute for insurance: health and term cover address the losses that are too large to be reserved against, and a household with a generous reserve and no cover has solved the wrong problem.

The reserve also cannot be sized once and forgotten. Income fragility changes with employment, dependants arrive and leave, obligations are repaid, and deposit rates move. The size that was right last year may be wrong this year, which is why the number belongs in the policy with a review date rather than in a note-to-self. Finally, more is not automatically better: an over-sized reserve is money that will not fund the goals it was meant for, and the discipline of sizing it accurately is what keeps the rest of the plan funded.

## Open questions

- How should the reserve scale with income volatility rather than with a fixed number of months, and can volatility be estimated without pretending to precision?
- Does an undrawn credit facility measurably reduce the reserve a household needs, given that credit can be withdrawn in the stress that triggers the need?
- What proportion of the reserve is better held in outright cash versus a liquid instrument, once settlement time and transaction rules are accounted for?
- At what point does an emergency reserve become distinct from a near-term goal bucket, and should the two be merged or kept separate in the policy?

The reserve is the part of the portfolio that exists to protect the rest of it. Sized against required outflows, held only in instruments that are trustworthy on the day they are needed, and reviewed on a schedule, it converts a household from one that must react to a shock into one that can choose how to respond.

A portfolio review is often mistaken for a market view. It is the opposite. A market view asks what will happen next and positions for it; a review asks whether the plan still describes the life it was written for, whether the portfolio still resembles the plan, what it cost to run, and what paperwork has quietly lapsed. The output is a short list of decisions and a note of the reasons. Done properly, it is one of the few activities in investing that is almost entirely within the investor's control.

## What an annual review is for, and what to gather

The review has three jobs. It tests the plan against reality — goals, horizons, income and obligations. It tests the portfolio against the plan — allocation, drift, costs and tax. And it tests the records against the portfolio — nomination, contact details, holdings and statements. Everything else is commentary.

Gather the evidence before starting, because the review is only as good as the numbers it works from.

- The investment policy document, or a note of the objectives and target allocation if no document exists.
- Statements for every account, as at the same date where possible. Comparing a demat statement from one month with a folio statement from another produces drift that is not real.
- The transaction history for the year: contributions, withdrawals, switches, dividends and any corporate actions.
- The cost record: expense ratios, platform and brokerage charges, and any fee change the intermediaries announced during the year.
- The tax record: realised gains and losses, dividend or interest income, and any advance-tax payments made.
- The paperwork: nominations on record, registered contact details, and the list of account identifiers.

## Goals and horizons

The first pass is about the destination, not the portfolio. For each goal, note the target amount, the date it is needed, and the money already dedicated to it. Then compare the required contribution with the contribution actually being made.

The value of this step is that it catches the mismatch that no amount of portfolio tinkering can fix. If a goal has moved closer in time, the horizon shortens and the allocation that was appropriate has to change with it. If income has risen or fallen, the contribution that is sustainable has changed. If a goal has been abandoned or a new obligation has appeared, the policy document itself may need amending.

It is also the step that prevents the review from becoming a return chase. A goal review asks whether the plan is still funded; a return review asks whether last year was pleasant. Only the first is a decision input.

## Allocation drift

Allocation drift is the gap between the portfolio's actual weights and its target weights, and it appears whenever the components grow at different rates. A target of 60 per cent equity and 40 per cent debt can become 68 and 32 after a strong equity run without a single transaction being made. The portfolio's risk has changed even though nothing was decided.

The review step is mechanical:

1. Value each sleeve at a single date.
2. Compute its actual weight as a share of the total.
3. Compare with the target weight and with the band the policy document permits.
4. Decide whether the deviation requires action, and if so, which action.

Two corrections are available, and they are not equivalent. Selling the overweighted sleeve and buying the underweighted one restores the target immediately but realises gains and incurs costs. Directing fresh contributions to the underweighted sleeve restores the target more slowly but without a sale. The right choice depends on the size of the drift, the size of the incoming flows, and the tax position — which is why tax is the next step, not the first.

## The cost audit

Costs are one of the few variables an investor controls completely, and they are the reason a review is worth doing even in a year when nothing else changed.

Start with what the industry publishes. Scheme data, expense ratios and category definitions are available in the machine-readable material that AMFI publishes, which allows a holding to be compared with the category it actually belongs to rather than with the label it is sold under. A fund that has drifted from its stated category is a cost problem and a risk problem at once.

Then look at what the household actually paid. The line items to check are:

- The expense ratio of each fund, and whether it changed during the year.
- Direct and regular plan status, and whether the plan on the statement matches what the household believes it is holding.
- Brokerage, platform, depository and account-maintenance charges, including any charge introduced during the year.
- Exit loads triggered by any switch made in the year, including switches made for tax or rebalancing purposes.
- Advisory or distribution fees, where any are paid separately from the scheme.

The arithmetic of cost is cumulative rather than annual, which is what makes it worth an annual glance. A charge that looks immaterial as a percentage of a single year compounds across the holding period. A cost audit is therefore less about finding a scandal than about noticing a drift in the fee structure that nobody decided to accept.

## Tax position

Tax enters the review twice: as a cost of any correcting action, and as a set of decisions with a calendar attached to them.

The review should establish what has already been realised in the year — short-term and long-term gains and losses, interest and dividend income — and what remains available before the year closes. Where the set-off and carry-forward rules allow losses to offset gains, the timing of a realisation can matter; that is the domain of tax-loss harvesting, which is a deliberate realisation of losses against gains, subject to the conditions and the carry-forward rules that apply.

The review is also where an investor checks the year's paperwork position: the tax deducted at source on interest or dividends, any advance-tax instalments due, and the documents the household will need when it comes to filing. It is not the place to state rates, exemptions or thresholds; those are set by the tax law for the relevant year and must be read from the official sources and the current rules, not from a checklist.

The one question the review should answer is whether the portfolio's structure — which assets sit in which account type, and which are held in taxable form — still makes sense given the income the household now earns. That is a structural question, and it changes less often than the tax law does.

## Liquidity, insurance and paperwork currency

This step is the one most often skipped, and it is the one whose omission shows up as a forced sale in a bad month.

- **Liquidity coverage.** Count only genuinely same-day assets — deposits, liquid funds and similar — against the months of required outflows they can cover. Equity, property and alternative assets are excluded by design, because their worst moments coincide with the moments when cash is needed. If the coverage has fallen, the reserve needs topping up before anything else in the review is actioned.
- **Emergency reserve.** Check that the reserve itself has not been quietly spent on a goal, and that it is held somewhere it can actually be reached without a market sale.
- **Insurance adequacy.** Term cover is a function of obligations and dependants, not of portfolio value; review it whenever liabilities or family circumstances change. Health cover is reviewed the same way, and the sum insured is compared with the cost of the care the household might need.
- **Paperwork currency.** Nomination on record for every account, registered mobile numbers and email addresses that are current, joint-holding instructions that say what was intended, and a holdings inventory that lists every account with its identifier. A lapse in this column costs nothing to fix now and can cost months later.
- **Withdrawals.** For a household drawing from the portfolio, check that the withdrawal rate and the sequencing of sales still match the plan, and that the drawdown is not concentrated in the sleeve that has fallen.

## Reading the system context without forecasting

The review is a reasonable moment to look at the state of the financial system, provided the material is read for the right purpose. The RBI's Financial Stability Report publishes semi-annual assessments of system-level stress indicators, bank asset-quality trends and macro-financial risks. Its value to a household is contextual: it describes the conditions in which counterparties operate.

What the material should not become is a forecast. A system-level report does not tell an investor which way markets will move, and it is not calibrated to any household's allocation. Its legitimate uses are narrower and more concrete: checking whether the assumptions about counterparty concentration remain sensible, whether the household's cash and deposit holdings are spread across institutions in a way that reflects the deposit-insurance position, and whether anything in the system picture argues for revisiting the policy document's risk statements rather than the portfolio's weights.

## The decision log, and what to leave alone

The review ends with a written record. The log is short, and it is the most valuable page produced all year.

- **What was decided**, in one line each, with the action and the date.
- **On what evidence**, whether that was a drift figure, a cost change, a tax position or a life event.
- **What would change the decision**, so that the next review can test it rather than re-argue it.
- **What was considered and rejected**, so that a rejected idea does not return as a fresh one.

The boundary is equally important. A review does not act on a year of returns, a headline, a fund's recent performance or a prediction. It acts on the triggers the policy document named: a breach of a rebalancing band, a change in goals or horizons, a change in costs, a change in tax position, or a change in the household's circumstances. Everything else is noise, and the discipline of the exercise is to write it down as reviewed and left alone.

## Explain simply

Once a year, set aside a few hours to check seven things in a fixed order: whether your goals and dates still make sense, how far the portfolio has drifted from its target mix, what it cost you to run, where you stand on tax, whether you have enough accessible cash and enough insurance, whether your nominations and contact details are up to date, and what the financial system looks like at a general level. Then write down what you decided and why, and stop. The point is not to predict the market. It is to make sure the plan, the portfolio and the paperwork still match each other — and to change only the things the plan says are worth changing.

## Worked example

The following is an illustrative calculation, not observed data and not a forecast.

Suppose a portfolio has an illustrative value of ₹40,00,000, with a target allocation of 60 per cent equity and 40 per cent debt. Over the year, equity has run up and debt has not, so the sleeves now stand at ₹27,20,000 of equity and ₹12,80,000 of debt. The actual equity weight is therefore:

$$ \frac{27,20,000}{40,00,000} = 0.68 $$

That is 68 per cent, against a 60 per cent target — a drift of eight percentage points. To restore the target by trading, the equity sleeve must fall to 0.60 × ₹40,00,000 = ₹24,00,000, so ₹3,20,000 of equity is switched into debt. Debt then rises from ₹12,80,000 to ₹16,00,000, which is 40 per cent of the total.

Now change the assumption about fresh flows. If the household invests ₹80,000 a month and directs all of it to debt for four months, ₹3,20,000 reaches the underweighted sleeve without a single sale, and the portfolio returns to target as the contributions arrive. The arithmetic of the destination is identical; the difference is that the second route realises no gains and triggers no transaction costs, at the price of taking four months to arrive. Which route is preferable depends on the size of the drift, the tax position and the cost per transaction — the three inputs the review examines before it acts.

## What this does not tell you

- **It is not advice.** A checklist cannot assess whether a particular allocation, fund or decision suits a particular household. That judgement depends on objectives, tax position and risk capacity.
- **It states no numbers.** Expense ratios, tax rates, exemptions and regulatory limits are deliberately absent, because they change and because they are set by the official sources rather than by a review process.
- **It does not forecast.** Nothing here produces an expected return, a market view or a timing signal, and the system-level material is not calibrated to any household.
- **It does not cover the implementation.** Placing the orders, correcting an erroneous debit, or handling a failed transaction are operational tasks with their own procedures.
- **It assumes the records exist.** The review is cheap when statements have been filed and an inventory maintained; where they have not, the first review is mostly an exercise in reconstruction.

Gold is bought largely on trust. A buyer cannot, at the counter, determine the fineness of a metal article with any confidence — the difference between one grade and another is invisible to the eye, and the tools needed to measure it are not on the shop floor. The entire apparatus of hallmarking exists because of that gap: it substitutes an accredited test for the buyer's judgement, and it converts a claim made by an interested seller into a statement made against a published Indian Standard.

That is a real improvement over pure trust. It is also narrower than many buyers assume, and the Indian regime is now specific enough to say exactly how narrow: it names the two metals covered, the grades that may be marked, the three components of the mark, and the remedy a consumer has when the description does not match the metal.

## Explain simply

A hallmark is a small set of marks struck into a metal article to say that it has been tested and that its purity matches a declared grade. Think of it as a purity certificate melted into the item: it tells you what the metal is, not what the item is worth. It does not tell you the weight, the quality of the workmanship, whether the price is fair, or what a jeweller will pay if you sell it back. The Bureau of Indian Standards is the body that runs this scheme in India, and since July 2021 each hallmarked piece also carries a unique identification number so that a single article can be traced back to its certification record.

## The problem hallmarking solves

Purity is a *credence* attribute: you cannot verify it by looking, and often cannot verify it even after use. In a market with a credence attribute, the seller knows more than the buyer, and the buyer's only defences are reputation and certification.

Reputation alone has limits. It is slow to build, easy to trade on and expensive to challenge. Certification changes the structure of the transaction by introducing a third party whose interest is in the accuracy of the test rather than in the sale — and by giving the buyer something to point to if the article does not match its description. In India the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) operates that role; a Reserve Bank of India committee on gold identified BIS as the agency to run the scheme in 2000. The mark is the visible tip of an arrangement involving a standard that defines purity grades, testing centres that measure items against it, assaying methods that fix how precise the measurement is, and a marking step that records the result on the item itself.

## What the hallmark contains

Under the present regime a hallmark on an article comprises three marks:

- **The BIS logo.** A symbol indicating that the article was marked under the national purity-assurance scheme rather than by a private arrangement.
- **Purity in caratage and fineness.** The grade to which the article was certified, expressed both in the familiar Indian carat convention and in the corresponding fineness in parts per thousand.
- **The HUID.** A six-digit alphanumeric Hallmarking Unique ID. Since it was introduced on 1 July 2021, this number is unique to the individual article, which is what makes the mark traceable rather than a generic stamp. A buyer can look it up using the "Verify HUID" function in the BIS Care app.

Two implications follow. First, the hallmark is **specific**: it certifies a particular article to a particular grade, not the retailer, the collection or the price. Second, the hallmark is **dated in its own way**: it records a test performed at a point in time on a specific object. A later alteration — a repair with a different alloy, added parts, resizing with solder of unknown composition — is outside what the original mark covered, because the tested object and the object in your hand are no longer identical.

## The grades that may be marked

The markable grades are not open-ended. They are fixed in the Indian Standards, and BIS sets out two metals under hallmarking — gold and silver. The operative instruments are the Hallmarking Regulation 2018 and the Mandatory Hallmarking Order with its dated amendments.

For gold, IS 1417:2016 specifies six caratages with their corresponding fineness: 14K (585), 18K (750), 20K (833), 22K (916), 23K (958) and 24K (995). A "22K" ring is therefore shorthand for a precise fineness requirement, not a loose description. For silver, IS 2112:2014 specifies six grades in parts per thousand: 800, 835, 900, 925, 970 and 990. The same family of standards includes IS 15820 (the standard for Assaying and Hallmarking Centres), IS 1418, IS 2113 and IS 17278 (refined bars).

The practical point is that a buyer can check the claimed grade against a closed list. A mark that does not correspond to one of these grades is not a hallmark anyone should rely on.

## Who may mark, and who may sell

Hallmarking is not something a buyer or an ordinary trader can arrange. Only a BIS-registered jeweller may approach an Assaying and Hallmarking Centre (AHC) to have articles hallmarked, and AHCs are themselves recognised by BIS against IS 15820. BIS carries out surveillance audits of recognised centres and draws market samples that are tested in BIS referral assay laboratories, which is how the accuracy of the testing layer is itself policed.

Registration as a jeweller is deliberately low-friction. A jeweller applies online through the BIS portal, and the registration is granted without a fee or document upload and is valid for life. A registered jeweller is expected to display the BIS logo stating that hallmarked jewellery is available for sale, to keep the registration certificate available, to maintain at least a ten-times magnifying glass, and to display the relationship between fineness and caratage. Those are counter-level signals a buyer can look for, independent of any single article.

## What a hallmark does not certify

The list of things a hallmark does *not* tell you is longer than most buyers expect.

- **Weight.** Purity and mass are different properties. An article can be perfectly pure and unexpectedly light.
- **Price or value.** The hallmark says nothing about design, workmanship or rarity, which is where much of a jewellery price resides.
- **Making charges.** The fabrication fee is a separate component of the price and is typically not recovered on resale.
- **Resale terms.** What a jeweller will pay is a commercial decision, generally the metal content at the prevailing rate less a deduction for purity loss.
- **Future condition.** Repairs and alterations after testing are not covered.
- **Origin or ethics.** The mark concerns fineness, not where the metal came from or how it was produced.

Read that list as a map of the questions a hallmark leaves open. Each one is a place where a buyer must still do work.

## Testing and compensation when the mark is wrong

The regime is not only a standard; it also gives a consumer a route when the article does not match the mark. A consumer may take an article to any BIS-recognised Assaying and Hallmarking Centre and ask for it to be tested, for a fee and with priority over routine work, and will receive an assay report stating the measured fineness.

Where a violation is established — an article carrying a hallmark but not conforming to the declared grade — the Hallmarking Regulation 2018 provides for compensation of **twice the difference** calculated on the shortage of purity for the weight sold, plus the testing charges. That is a defined arithmetical remedy rather than an invitation to negotiate, and it is the reason the record matters: the invoice establishes the weight and grade sold, and the assay establishes what the metal actually was.

Complaints about the scheme, a jeweller or an assay centre can be made through the BIS Care app, the online consumer complaint portal, the dedicated complaints email address, or the public grievance officer. None of that replaces the police where an offence is involved, but it is the administrative layer specific to purity.

## What to check at the counter

A short, practical sequence:

1. **Look for the three marks**, in the right place on the article, and check that they are legible.
2. **Identify the grade** on the mark and confirm that it is one of the markable grades, and that the price quoted is for that grade.
3. **Verify the HUID** through the BIS Care app, and match it to the article in hand.
4. **Get the weight in writing** on the invoice, with the grade alongside it.
5. **Separate the price**, in writing, into metal value, making charges and tax.
6. **Ask about resale terms** before buying, specifically the deduction for purity loss.
7. **Keep everything.** A hallmark is evidence only if you can show what was sold to you.

This list is deliberately about documentation as much as about the mark. The mark answers one question well; the paperwork answers the rest poorly but at least recoverably.

## Removing purity risk altogether

A buyer whose real objective is exposure to the gold price rather than possession of metal has alternatives that eliminate purity risk by construction. Gold exchange-traded funds hold gold or gold-related instruments in a regulated fund structure, so the purity question is handled inside the product and the holder owns units rather than an object. Sovereign gold bonds are government securities denominated in grams of gold, on the sovereign's credit, with interest and redemption terms set in the issue documents; they carry no storage and no purity risk, though they are securities with their own conditions.

Each substitutes a different set of risks — market, expense and, for a security, credit and liquidity — for the purity risk it removes. Purity assurance and price exposure are separable problems, and blending them is what makes the counter conversation so confusing.

## Worked example: what a purity shortfall is worth

This is an illustrative calculation, not observed data, and the figures are hypothetical numbers chosen only to make the arithmetic visible.

Suppose an article was sold as 22K at a stated weight, and the metal value at the declared grade would be ₹100,000 at the prevailing rate. Assume, purely for illustration, that an assay shows the actual purity falls short of the declared grade by an amount worth ₹2,000 of that metal value. Under the compensation rule described above, the refundable amount is twice the difference, plus testing charges:

$$\text{compensation} = 2 \times 2,000 = 4,000 \quad \text{plus testing charges}$$

So the statutory remedy on a ₹2,000 shortfall would be about ₹4,000 before testing charges are added. That figure is modest next to the other round-trip costs — a making charge and a purchase tax on the same item typically amount to far more — which is exactly the point. Purity shortfalls are worth guarding against, and they are usually not the largest number in the transaction.

The example also shows why the hallmark's *specificity* matters. A generic claim of purity, unsupported by a test on the item, leaves the whole difference to chance; a mark tied to a certification record reduces it to a dispute that can be evidenced and, where the rule applies, arithmetically settled.

## What this does not tell you

This article does not list every category of article currently subject to mandatory hallmarking, reproduce the notification-by-notification exemptions, state the testing tolerances, or give the enforcement penalties. Those are operative rules that change; they must be read from the standards and the notifications in force. It also does not compare the cost of physical ownership against funds or bonds in rupees, evaluate any retailer, or predict the resale value of any article. And it does not claim that a hallmark eliminates the risk of a bad purchase — it converts a pure question of trust into a question of documentation, which is an improvement, not a guarantee.

The Reserve Bank of India is not a securities regulator and its published interest in crypto assets has never been primarily about whether a token is a good or bad investment. Its interest is in monetary sovereignty, in the integrity of the payments system, and in where risk accumulates when private instruments settle outside supervised institutions. Reading the official position correctly requires holding that distinction in view: the central bank's cautions are statements about the architecture of money and settlement, and they are frequently quoted as though they were opinions about prices.

This is the twelfth article in the Digital Assets Laboratory. The first article in the series set out the legal position as it stands. This one takes the central bank's stance as the object of study — what has been said, what has been done, how to date each act, and what the record does and does not imply for someone who holds or trades tokens. It is written as a record to be checked, not as a narrative to be trusted.

## What the central bank has actually said

The published position is consistent across several years and several channels, and it can be summarised without exaggeration.

- **The rupee is the only legal tender.** No token carries a sovereign guarantee, and no merchant is obliged to accept one in discharge of a debt.
- **Private crypto assets are not money.** The central bank's objection is that a private issuer's liability, priced by a market and settled on an unsupervised network, cannot perform the functions of a currency without importing the risks those functions were designed to avoid.
- **The risks named are specific.** Loss of monetary control if private instruments circulate widely, consumer harm from volatility and from the absence of recourse, the use of the channel for money laundering and terror financing, and concentration of risk in a settlement layer that is not supervised.
- **The technology itself is treated separately from the issuance model.** The same institution has studied central-bank digital currency and tokenised deposits on their own terms, because a direct liability of the central bank is a different instrument from a private token.

That last point is the one most often missed. The official concern is not that distributed ledgers do not work; it is that a private unit of account issued outside the monetary system creates problems that the ledger's technical properties do not address.

## The measures taken over time

The regulatory history is a sequence of distinct acts, and conflating them is the source of most confusion. Described at the level of what happened, the sequence runs as follows. Each dated reference carries a flag because the packet of sources for this article does not carry the dates, and commentary in circulation routinely mixes announcement dates with effective dates.

1. **Directions to regulated entities.** The central bank directed entities it supervises not to deal in virtual currencies or provide services facilitating their trade. The direction operated on regulated institutions rather than on individuals, and the date requires verification against the central bank's own archive.
2. **Judicial review.** The direction was challenged and the challenge succeeded, so the prohibition on regulated entities dealing in virtual currencies did not survive in that form. The judgment date and its precise scope require verification.
3. **The statutory tax category.** The Finance Act, 2022 created the category of the virtual digital asset and set out how income from transferring one is computed. This is a tax provision, and its date of creation is stated in the packet's own description of the category.
4. **Anti-money-laundering obligations.** The reporting, record-keeping and customer-identification obligations that apply to reporting entities were extended to intermediaries dealing in virtual digital assets, so that exchanges and similar businesses now operate inside a reporting framework rather than outside it. The date of extension requires verification.
5. **Continued public caution.** The cautions have not been withdrawn. The central bank has continued to state its concerns publicly, and to distinguish them from its own work on central-bank digital currency.

Two things follow from setting it out this way. First, no single act is "the regulation"; each act has its own instrument, its own addressee and its own effective date. Second, the sequence is not a monotonic tightening or loosening. The directions as originally issued were set aside, while obligations under other frameworks were extended in the same period, and a tax category was created on top. A reader who expects a single direction of travel will misread it.

## Announcement dates are not commencement dates

This is the discipline that separates accurate tracking from noise, and it applies to every unusual asset class, not only this one.

An announcement establishes intent. A speech, a press release or a paragraph in a policy document tells you what an institution intends and why. A gazette notification publishes the instrument. A commencement clause, or a separate notification for a provision already enacted, establishes when the rule actually binds. The three can be months or years apart, and in the interval the position on the ground is unchanged.

For a participant, only the third date has operational consequences: it determines when a reporting obligation begins, when a deduction at source starts to apply, and which financial year a transaction falls into. The Gazette of India is the primary evidence for it, which is why a tax or regulatory article that cites only commentary is unreliable even when the commentary is well informed.

## Explain simply

The central bank has for years warned publicly that private crypto assets are risky, are not money, and sit outside the supervised payments system — but a warning is not a ban, and the directions it issued to banks were later set aside by a court. Separately, Parliament created a tax category for these assets, and anti-money-laundering rules were extended to the businesses that deal in them. So the position is not "legal" or "illegal" in a single word: holding and transferring are not prohibited, the assets are simply not a regulated product, and they are taxed and increasingly are subject to reporting duties. When a new rule is announced, the question to ask is not what was said, but when it came into force, and the gazette is where that is recorded.

## Why the objection is about payments, not about returns

It helps to see the central bank's arguments in their own terms.

A monetary authority sets the unit of account, influences the quantity of money and supervises the settlement system through which obligations are discharged. Private instruments that circulate as a means of payment compete with that function outside the perimeter built to manage it. If a private unit becomes widely used, the authority loses control over the transmission of policy and the ability to act as a backstop in a stress event. The concern is systemic rather than moralistic.

The consumer-protection argument runs alongside it. A token has no mandated disclosure document, no capital requirement behind its issuer, no segregated custody rule and no compensation scheme. A run or a failure therefore has no resolution mechanism, which is the same concern that motivates deposit insurance and resolution frameworks for banks.

The illicit-finance argument is the third leg, and it is the one that has produced concrete operational obligations. Because a transfer settles without an intermediary institution, the surveillance that supervised institutions provide has to be recreated at the boundary where tokens meet the regulated system — which is precisely the point of extending reporting-entity obligations to virtual-asset intermediaries.

## What the stance has not been

Three readings of the official position are common and all three are unsupported.

**It has not been a general prohibition.** Nothing in the published record stops a resident from holding or transferring a token. The directions that did restrict activity were addressed to regulated entities and were set aside on review.

**It has not been an endorsement.** Absence of prohibition is not approval. There is no registration regime for tokens, no mandated disclosure, no suitability requirement and no product-level protection. The gap between tolerance and approval is where nearly all the practical risk sits.

**It has not been a position on valuation.** The central bank has never said a token is overvalued or undervalued, and it does not operate a price regime. Anyone quoting a central-bank statement as a view on whether to hold a token is converting a monetary-policy argument into an investment opinion it was never intended to be.

## A tax category is not a regulatory home

The creation of the statutory category is the most misunderstood act in the sequence, because it is often read as recognition.

A tax category answers one question: how is income from a transaction computed and collected. It prescribes the rate, what may be deducted, what may be set off, and the collection mechanism. It is deliberately blunt, and the packet's own description of the category records that income from transferring a virtual digital asset is taxed at a flat rate with no deduction for expenses other than cost of acquisition and no set-off of losses against other income.

What a tax category does not do is establish who may issue such an instrument, what must be disclosed to a buyer, where customer assets must be held, how a failure is resolved, or who has standing to complain. Those are the questions a regulatory regime answers, and none of them is answered by a rate. This is why a portfolio can be fully taxable and entirely unprotected at the same time, and the two facts are not in tension.

A practical consequence follows. Because the regime is blunt, it is also indifferent to outcome: a loss on one transfer does not reduce tax on any other income, so the tax code influences the distribution of an outcome rather than the decision that produced it. That is a design feature, and it should be understood rather than worked around. The pending renumbering of these provisions under the Income-tax Act, 2025 does not change the mechanism, and the correspondence between the old and new section numbers is a matter awaiting verification.

## What this means for a participant

Four operational implications follow from the record, and they are stated as consequences of the position rather than as recommendations.

- **Tax compliance is real and personal.** The obligations attach to the individual's filings regardless of where the token is custodied or which venue executed the transfer.
- **Reporting duties reach the intermediary, not the network.** Because a reporting-entity framework now covers the businesses that deal in these assets, expect customer identification and transaction records at the point where tokens meet the rupee system.
- **Nothing in the framework substitutes for due diligence.** With no product regulator, the buyer is the entire diligence function, which is the central theme of the rest of this series.
- **The classification question is still open.** Whether these assets become securities, commodities or a category of their own would change the applicable protections more than any tax amendment would.

## How to track the record accurately

A short method, because the volume of commentary exceeds the volume of law.

1. Read the primary document rather than a summary of it, and check who it binds: an institution, an intermediary, or an individual.
2. Find the commencement date in the gazette or in the instrument's own clause, and record it against the calendar rather than to the day of the announcement.
3. Keep a dated file of what you read, because positions reverse: the directions to regulated entities were set aside, and a reader relying on memory alone would have the position wrong.
4. Separate tax instruments from regulatory instruments in your own notes, because they answer different questions and move on different timetables.
5. Treat any statement of the form "the regulator has now approved this" as requiring a citation to the instrument that does the approving.

## Worked example

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast and not tax advice. It uses round figures chosen to expose the mechanism.

Suppose in one year a holder makes two transfers. The first produces a gain of ₹1,00,000; the second produces a loss of ₹1,00,000. Economically the year is flat. Under a flat-rate regime with no set-off of losses against other income, the tax is computed on the profitable transfer alone — at a flat rate with no deduction for expenses other than cost of acquisition — and the loss on the second transfer produces no reduction anywhere. Using a 30% flat rate purely to make the arithmetic visible, the tax on the gain would be ₹30,000 before surcharge and cess, while the loss is real in cash but inert in tax. The net position is a cash outflow of ₹30,000 in a year that went nowhere.

The point of the example is not the rupee amount. It is that when a regime disallows set-off, the sequence of outcomes within a year matters as much as the annual total, and a holder who does not track transfers as they occur will discover this at filing time.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the official position and the sequence of instruments; it does not predict what the position will be, and it takes no view on whether any token is worth holding. It also does not reproduce the text of any direction or notification, because the packet of sources does not carry them and paraphrasing a legal instrument from memory is how errors enter the record.

Three gaps deserve emphasis. First, the dates are flagged rather than asserted, and any reader who needs them for a filing obligation should take them from the primary document and not from this summary. Second, the interaction between the tax category, the reporting-entity framework and the securities perimeter has not been tested by a large body of decisions, so the boundaries are outlines rather than settled lines. Third, the central bank's stance on the payments system and the legislature's stance on tax are not necessarily converging; they can diverge for years without either being wrong on its own terms.

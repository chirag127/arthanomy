A brokerage account is usually presented as a single product: access to the exchange, a screen, and a rate. It is more usefully understood as a bundle of four things — an execution service, a settlement rail, a custody record and a line of credit — each priced separately and each governed by different terms. The rate in the advertisement is only the first line of the cost stack, and it is often not the largest. Investors who choose on that rate alone meet the rest of the stack later, at the moment they need something the basic account does not include: a pledge against a position, an off-market transfer, a delivery instruction on short notice, or a corporate-action credit that has to be reconciled by hand.

This article describes what a broker actually provides, what each account behind a single login holds, how the cost stack is assembled, why flat-fee and percentage models diverge across trading patterns, and what a comparison should include beyond price. It quotes no rate and names no broker. Every number it would need lives in a schedule or a notification that changes, and the honest treatment is to describe the mechanism and say where the figure must be read.

## Explain simply

A broker is the exchange member through whose books orders are placed and settled, and it is usually also the depository participant that maintains the electronic record of what you own. What you pay is several things, not one. There is the broker's own fee. There are charges the exchange and the depository levy. There are statutory levies the government imposes on a transaction. There is tax on the service. Some of these are proportional to how much you trade, some are fixed per order or per instruction, and some are charged once a year. That mix, rather than any single rate, decides what a year of trading costs, which is why the same broker can be cheap for one investor and expensive for another.

## The broker, the accounts, and who holds what

Three relationships sit behind a single login, and separating them prevents most confusion about charges.

- **The trading relationship.** A trading member of an exchange places orders and is responsible for settling the trades made through it. Membership, including the distinction between trading and clearing membership, is a matter of the exchange's rules and of the market-conduct framework the regulator supervises.
- **The custody relationship.** The shares are not held by the broker in the ordinary sense of the word. They sit with a depository, and the broker typically acts as a depository participant, an intermediary registered for that purpose. The depository's own schedule governs what the depository-side services cost.
- **The funding relationship.** Any cash balance the investor keeps with the broker is a claim on the broker rather than a bank deposit, and the rules on how client funds must be kept form part of market conduct. Any position the broker funds is credit extended at a price.

The document that ties a particular trade to all three is the contract note, issued for each trade within the market-conduct framework, and it is the record to check when a charge is disputed. A standing authorisation that lets the broker debit the demat account to settle a sale is a separate authority from the trade itself, and the scope of any such authority is a term worth reading rather than assuming.

## The cost stack, head by head

The cost of a trade is not one line. The heads below appear, in some combination, on a broker's charge schedule and on the contract note. What this article does not do is state an amount: rates and bases are set by each broker's schedule or by government notification, they differ between brokers, and they are revised. Take every figure from the current schedule and, for the statutory heads, from the current notification.

| Head | Levied by | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brokerage | broker | per order, per unit of turnover, or a flat periodic plan, per the schedule |
| Exchange transaction charge | exchange | proportional to traded value |
| Regulatory turnover fee | regulator | proportional to traded value |
| Securities transaction tax | government | on specified transactions, per the applicable notification |
| Stamp duty | government | on the purchase side, per the applicable notification |
| Tax on services | government | on brokerage and related service charges |
| Depository participant charges | depository participant | annual maintenance plus per-instruction charges |
| Margin-funding interest | broker | per day on the funded amount, per the broker's schedule |
| Optional services | broker | call and trade, auto square-off, duplicate statements, account closure, per the schedule |

Two properties of this stack deserve emphasis. First, the fixed elements do not scale with order size while the proportional elements do, so cost per rupee traded depends on the size of the orders and not only on the rate. Second, the levies and the tax are not negotiable and not broker-specific, so a comparison between two brokers turns on the negotiable lines plus the schedule of fixed charges, not on the headline rate.

## Margins, pledges and the funding leg

For a delivery purchase, the funding need is the purchase value. For derivative positions and short-dated trades, the exchange's margin requirements apply, and the exchange publishes the contract specifications, lot sizes and margin and expiry calendars from which those requirements follow. Where the investor does not fund a position in full, the broker funds the difference, and the price of that funding is a per-day interest charge that never appears as brokerage.

A related mechanic is the pledge. Shares held in the demat account can be pledged in favour of the broker or a clearing corporation to collateralise a position, under the depository's pledge and margin-pledge rules. The practical consequences are administrative rather than analytical: a pledged holding cannot be sold or transferred while the pledge stands, a release has to be invoked for the investor to regain free use of the shares, and both the pledge and the release are depository instructions with their own charges. An investor comparing brokers on price should establish whether pledging is supported at all, what it costs and how a release is handled, because those terms can matter more than a small difference in the brokerage line.

## Flat fee versus percentage: where each wins

Two pricing shapes dominate retail brokerage: a fixed amount per executed order, and a percentage of the turnover of the order. Neither is cheaper in general, because each is cheaper across a region of order size.

- **Fixed per order.** The charge is the same whether the order is small or large, so the cost per rupee traded falls as order size rises. It favours investors who trade infrequently, in larger lots.
- **Percentage of turnover.** The charge scales with the order, so the cost per rupee traded is constant regardless of size. It suits small and frequent orders, where a fixed charge would be proportionally enormous.

The two converge at the order size where the fixed charge equals the percentage charge. That crossover is the single most useful figure in a broker comparison, and it is arithmetic an investor can do with two numbers taken from the two schedules. Below it, the percentage model costs less; above it, the fixed model costs less. For an investor who trades in a narrow band of order sizes, the crossover answers the question directly. For one whose order sizes vary widely, the total annual cost under each model does.

## What a price comparison leaves out

Cost is the measurable part of a broker relationship and rarely the part that causes trouble. The terms below are not on the charge schedule in any comparable way, and they are where accounts differ most.

- **Whether the services you need exist.** Pledge support, off-market transfers and the ability to move securities to another depository participant are not universally offered, and a cheap broker that cannot perform them is not cheap for an investor who needs them.
- **How corporate actions are handled.** Bonus issues, splits, dividends and buybacks arrive as credits against the demat holding and have to reconcile against the record.
- **What the terms permit.** The scope of any debit authority, the circumstances in which a position can be squared off without instruction, and the treatment of the client's cash balance are all contractual matters.
- **How a dispute escalates.** The route runs to the broker first and then through the market's grievance machinery, and the escalation path matters when a charge or a settlement is contested.
- **Where the client's funds sit.** How client funds and securities must be kept and reported is a question of market conduct, and the answer is a fact about the broker rather than about its price list.

None of these reduce to a rate. All of them can be read before an account is opened, which is cheaper than discovering them afterwards.

## Worked example: the crossover between a flat fee and a percentage

This is an illustrative calculation of a mechanism, not a quotation from any broker, and it assumes no particular rate. Let the flat fee per executed order be F and the percentage brokerage on turnover be p, so that an order of turnover V costs F under the first model and p times V under the second. The two are equal when F equals p times V, that is at the crossover turnover

$$ V^{*} = \frac{F}{p} $$

The arithmetic is worth internalising in its proportional form rather than its absolute one. Doubling the flat fee doubles the crossover turnover; halving the percentage rate doubles it as well; and moving both by the same factor leaves the crossover unchanged. For a trading pattern of n orders of average turnover V, annual brokerage under the fixed model is the product of n and F, and under the percentage model it is the product of n, p and V. The annual comparison therefore depends on how many orders are placed and how large they are, not on either alone. Substituting the two current schedule figures for F and p is the whole of the calculation, and the answer changes whenever either schedule changes.

## What this does not tell you

- **It states no rates.** Brokerage, exchange charges, levies, taxes, depository charges and funding interest are set by broker schedules or by government notification. Every figure must be read from the current source, and any figure quoted without a date should be treated as stale and requiring verification.
- **It does not rank brokers or models.** Nothing here indicates which arrangement suits any particular investor; that depends on order sizes, trading frequency and the services used.
- **It does not cover settlement mechanics in detail.** The cycle in which trades settle, and the obligations it creates, is a separate subject.
- **It does not cover tax treatment.** How a gain is characterised and taxed, and which costs are deductible, is a distinct question with its own current rules.

## Open questions

- How comparable are brokers' charge schedules in practice, given that the fixed heads are bundled and named differently from one broker to another?
- Whether the cost of the funding leg, which does not appear in a brokerage comparison at all, is disclosed in a form most retail investors can estimate before trading.
- How much of the divergence between cheap and expensive brokers reflects the presence or absence of services such as pledging, rather than differences in execution quality.
- Whether charge disclosure across brokers can be standardised enough that an annual cost comparison becomes a routine calculation rather than a manual one.

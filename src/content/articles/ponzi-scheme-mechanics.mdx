A Ponzi scheme is often described as an investment that collapsed. That framing hides the important part. The collapse is not a risk the scheme ran into; it is the scheme working exactly as designed until the design fails. The structure pays earlier participants with money taken from later ones, which means the scheme is never an investment at all — it is a queue, and queues built on recruitment have a known ending.

## Explain simply

A Ponzi scheme takes money in and pays some of it back as "returns". The money it pays back does not come from any profit; it comes from the next set of people who pay in. Early participants get paid and tell others, which brings in more money and keeps the pretence alive — until fewer people join, or too many ask for their money back, and there is nothing left to pay with. Nobody needs to prove intent for the arithmetic to bite: if the source of the payouts is new subscriptions, the arrangement ends when subscriptions stop, and they always stop.

## What makes a structure a Ponzi scheme

The definition rests on one question, and only one: where does the money paid to participants come from?

If it comes from profits earned in a real activity — interest paid by borrowers, dividends from a business, rent from a property, gains realised on traded securities — the arrangement may be risky, badly run, or good, but it is not a Ponzi scheme. If it comes from the subscriptions of participants who joined later, it is a Ponzi scheme even when it is dressed in the language of trading programmes, plantations, timeshares, e-commerce, artificial intelligence or gold.

| Feature | Genuine activity | Ponzi structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source of payouts | Profit or income from the activity | Subscriptions of later participants |
| Return pattern | Varies with markets and results | Fixed, smooth, time-bound |
| Cash needed | Capital to conduct the activity | Constant or growing new inflow |
| Incentive paid to participants | Sometimes, for distribution of a real product | Often, for bringing in new money |
| Ending | Depends on the activity | Certain, when inflow stops |

The distinction is not about legality of the underlying idea. It is about the cash-flow logic. A generous promise backed by a real activity can fail; a generous promise backed by nothing but newcomers cannot succeed.

## Why the arithmetic makes perpetuity impossible

A scheme that promises a return must find the money to pay it. If the promised return is $r$ for a period and the outstanding "capital" is $A$, the payout due at the end of that period is at least the product of the two, before any withdrawals of principal:

$$ \text{payout due} \approx r \times A $$

Growth in the pool makes this worse, not better, because the obligation scales with the pool. If participants reinvest their returns instead of withdrawing them, the liability compounds — a claimed 2% a month compounds to roughly 26.8% a year, so the inflow required by the scheme must itself grow by about that much every year merely to stand still.

The recruitment side has the same problem in a harsher form. A scheme that depends on each participant bringing in more participants is a tree, and a tree of even modest branching exhausts any finite population quickly. If each participant must bring in two, the number of participants needed at successive levels is 2, 4, 8, 16 and onward — and the cumulative number of people required by a given depth grows enormously:

| Depth | Participants at that level | Cumulative participants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 32 | 62 |
| 10 | 1,024 | 2,046 |
| 15 | 32,768 | 65,534 |
| 20 | 1,048,576 | 2,097,150 |

This is not a scenario about greed running out. It is the same structural arithmetic that makes a money-multiplier or "matrix" scheme a chain letter: the last level of a chain must be larger than all the levels before it, and a chain that must keep growing cannot grow forever in a country, a city or a WhatsApp group.

## Worked example: the monthly bill for a promise

This is an illustrative construction, not data from any scheme. Suppose a promoter has collected ₹10 crore and has promised participants 2% a month, payable in cash.

- The monthly payout due on that pool is ₹20 lakh — ₹2.4 crore over a year — before a single participant asks for their principal back.
- If the pool had been allowed to compound at the promised rate instead, the liability would grow by about 26.8% in the year, so the level of new money required would have to grow at least as fast.
- Any amount the promoter takes for personal use, marketing or "office expenses" increases the required inflow further, because it is money that cannot go to the promised payouts.

The scheme therefore needs a rising tide of new subscriptions from the day it opens. The promised return is not a percentage of a profit; it is a claim on future participants.

## Smoothness is itself evidence

A claimed double-digit annual return reported with almost no month-to-month variation is describing something that markets do not produce. Returns from equities, debt, property and commodities fluctuate; their standard deviation — the typical size of the deviation from the average — is positive and often large. A series with a claimed high return and a standard deviation close to zero is not a low-risk series; it is an accounting convention. The dispersion that real investing generates has to go somewhere, and in a Ponzi scheme it is hidden rather than removed.

That is the practical inversion worth internalising. Risk disclosure is not the absence of a warning sign; **unusually smooth returns are a warning sign in themselves**. If a scheme promises a high return and never has a losing month, the smoothness is not evidence of skill, it is evidence that the numbers are being chosen rather than earned.

## The structure test

Any opportunity can be run through a short set of questions, most of which have one-word answers.

1. **Who is the counterparty, and is it registered?** A pooled investment scheme requires registration with SEBI; an unregistered collective investment scheme is unlawful. A name, a website and a certificate of incorporation are not registration.
2. **Where do the payouts come from?** If the answer cannot be stated in one concrete sentence about a real activity with real cash flows, the answer is new subscriptions.
3. **Is the payment for recruiting?** Compensation that flows mainly from enrolling new participants rather than from selling to people outside the scheme is the signature of a money-circulation structure.
4. **Is the return fixed and unconditional?** Guaranteed, time-bound, uniform returns are not what investing looks like; they are what a liability looks like.
5. **Can you withdraw without friction?** Delays, penalties, rollover pressure and "reinvestment bonuses" all buy the scheme time.
6. **Are the accounts auditable?** If nobody can see where the money sits and what it earned, the answer is that it did not earn anything.
7. **What happens to the money if you stop recruiting?** If the answer is that payouts stop, the structure has already told you what it is.

## The legal position in India

Two bodies of rules matter most for retail investors, and they overlap.

Collective investment schemes must be registered with SEBI before they can pool money from the public, and enforcement orders repeatedly address arrangements structured nominally around land, plantations, teak, timeshares and e-commerce that promised returns while operating as unregistered schemes. Investors can check registration status with SEBI directly, and unregistered schemes are subject to adjudication and winding-up processes. Alongside this, deposit-taking and money-circulation arrangements are restricted by their own statutes, and the state police economic offences units typically register cases under those provisions, as well as under the general criminal law.

Since 1 July 2024 the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 has replaced the Indian Penal Code, and it carries the provisions on cheating, criminal breach of trust, forgery and organised crime that a large investment fraud will engage. Money laundering is separately addressed by the prevention of money-laundering framework, whose reporting and attachment mechanisms determine how proceeds are traced and frozen. The legal consequence of running a scheme is therefore not a single offence but a stack of them.

## Why leaving early is not a defence

There is a durable belief that participants who withdrew before the collapse "got out in time". The belief misunderstands where their money came from. A participant who was paid out was paid with another participant's subscription, so the gain was never earned; it was transferred. In recovery, the focus is on tracing and attaching assets and on adjudicating claims, and courts have dealt with the practical consequences of ranking investors among other creditors. In insolvency, secured creditors sit ahead of unsecured ones, and an unsecured claim on a failed entity is frequently worth a fraction of its face value. Recovery is the rule that fails most often in fraud, which is why prevention is the only decision that reliably helps.

## Reporting routes in India

If a scheme looks like a Ponzi structure, the appropriate responses are contained and documented rather than improvised.

- **Stop paying in.** Every additional rupee is a new subscription, and it is the money used to pay someone else's "return".
- **Preserve evidence.** Statements, receipts, chat messages, bank references, screenshots of claimed returns and the identity details of the promoter and the receiving accounts.
- **Route it to SEBI** through its investor-alert and complaint channels, particularly where the concern is an unregistered collective investment scheme or misleading claims about returns. SEBI maintains published alerts on unauthorised entities and guaranteed-return schemes.
- **Route it to the police** where cheating or unauthorised deposit-taking is involved, typically through the state economic offences unit that handles such cases.
- **Do not attempt private recovery** from other participants, and do not accept an offer of "settlement" in exchange for silence. Neither helps the investor and both can complicate the investigation.

## What this does not tell you

This article sets out mechanics, warning signs and reporting routes. It is not a determination about any particular firm, scheme or individual, and nothing here should be read as an allegation against any named business. It also cannot tell you whether a specific product is fraudulent from the outside — some failures look like Ponzi schemes after the fact and are simply failed businesses. What it can do is narrow the question to the one that matters: if the payouts depend on new money, the ending is already decided, and the only remaining variable is whether you are on the losing side of it. The opportunity cost of leaving early is a rounding error next to the cost of staying.

## Open questions

- How much of the money paid into unauthorised schemes in India is in practice recovered through attachment and insolvency, and what does the recovery rate imply for deterrence?
- How do victims actually choose between SEBI, the police and consumer forums, and does the fragmentation of routes delay action?
- Can disclosure standards for pooled products be designed so that the source of payouts must always be stated in a form a retail investor can read?

The durable lesson is structural rather than moral. A Ponzi scheme does not fail because it is badly managed; it fails because the only thing that can keep it alive — an ever-growing supply of new money — is the one thing no scheme can guarantee.

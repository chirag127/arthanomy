Life insurance comes in two shapes. In one, the premium buys a contingent payment and nothing else: if the insured dies within the term, the insurer pays the sum assured; if the insured survives, the policy simply ends. In the other, the same premium buys that payment plus a savings contract that matures, accumulates a fund, or returns the premiums paid. The second shape produces a number that can be displayed at the point of sale. The first shape does the protection work, and it is the foundation on which the rest of a household's plan should sit.

This article opens the Insurance and Pensions Decoded series by taking pure risk cover on its own terms: what it promises, why it is priced as it is, how to size it, and what the variants cost.

## Protection and investment are different products

Saving accumulates money for a future you intend to reach. Risk transfer moves a loss you may never suffer onto a pool of people facing the same chance, so that the unlucky few are made whole by the premiums of the many. Their failure modes are opposite. A savings product fails by delivering too little relative to its cost, which is a performance question. A protection product fails by delivering nothing when it was needed, which is a contract question — and it fails catastrophically, because the need arrives at the moment the household is least able to absorb it.

Because the two are sold inside one premium, they are rarely evaluated separately. The discipline this series applies is to pull them apart: price the cover as cover, price the savings element as an investment, and then ask whether the combination beats buying the pieces separately.

The economics of protection are ordinary once unbundled: the insurer's cost of cover is its expected death claims, adjusted for mortality assumptions, plus expenses and a margin. That is the mortality charge — the price of the risk. Everything else in the premium is the cost of a savings promise or of distribution.

## What the contract actually promises

Strip away the language and a term policy is a one-directional, conditional promise: pay the sum assured to the nominee if the insured dies within the term, and pay nothing if the insured survives it. Three features follow.

- **There is no accumulated fund.** A pure term contract has no meaningful surrender value, because there is nothing accumulated to surrender. Surrender rules matter enormously for policies that do build value; for pure cover there is usually no asset to recover.
- **The promise is conditional.** The policy document specifies the exclusions, the treatment of death by suicide during the first policy year, the grace period for a late premium, the conditions for reviving a lapsed policy, and the evidence the nominee must furnish. Those clauses, not the marketing, define what the family is buying.
- **The premium is committed for the term.** The insurer prices the contract on level funding over the whole term, so stopping early removes the cover at exactly the point where age has made it harder and dearer to replace. Nomination also matters: without it, the proceeds can fall into the insured's estate and pass through a slower process.

## Why pure cover is cheap: the arithmetic of pooling

The premium can be small relative to the sum assured because most policyholders in any year survive, so the pool's claims are spread across everyone.

A simplified illustration of the principle, not a quotation or a forecast. Suppose the one-year probability of death for an applicant were 0.1 per cent and a one-year contract paid ₹1 crore on death, with no expenses or margin. The expected claim cost is 0.001 multiplied by ₹1,00,00,000, or ₹10,000 — the pure price of the risk, on top of which expenses, margin, taxes and distribution are added.

Three things follow. First, the price is dominated by mortality, so anything that raises expected claims — age, tobacco, a pre-existing condition — raises the premium, and little else does. Second, the large multiple between premium and payout is not a return; it is a probability-weighted payment, and weighting it by the probability of death gives an expected value below the premium. That is the correct structure of insurance: the household buys variance reduction, not an expected gain. Third, the expected "return on premium" for a policyholder who survives is zero, and that is the design rather than a defect.

> Term insurance is the only purchase in personal finance where the best possible outcome is that it is never used.

## Sizing the cover: the needs method

The common shortcut — a multiple of income, such as twenty times — ignores almost everything that matters. It does not know whether the income is gross or post-tax, whether there is a mortgage, whether education is already funded, or whether the employer provides group cover.

Four things go in, and two come out.

1. **Income replacement.** The present value of the post-tax income the household would lose, held in today's rupees.
2. **Liabilities.** Outstanding loans, so the survivors are not forced to sell assets or liquidate a retirement corpus to keep the house.
3. **Future obligations.** Committed expenses with dates: education, care of a dependent, and any event the family genuinely intends to fund.
4. **Immediate costs.** Final expenses and a buffer for the disruption after a death, when income also stops.

Then subtract **existing cover** — employer group cover, earlier policies — and **assets already earmarked** for these purposes. The home the survivors will keep living in is not earmarked; neither is the retirement corpus the surviving spouse will need in old age. In symbols, with the income stream in today's rupees:

$$ \text{Cover} = \left( C \times \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r} \right) + L + F + E - X - A $$

Here $C$ is the annual after-tax income to be replaced, $r$ a real discount rate, $n$ the years of support needed, $L$ liabilities, $F$ future obligations, $E$ immediate costs, $X$ existing cover and $A$ earmarked assets.

The discount rate is where the honest disagreement sits. Working in today's rupees throughout — a real rate — keeps the calculation interpretable, and the rate should be conservative, because the money is paid out precisely when a household should not be taking market risk with funds it depends on. A range from several plausible assumptions beats one precise number that hides its assumption.

## Level or increasing cover: matching the shape of the need

Two curves have to be compared, and the usual mistake is to look at only one.

The **cover curve** is what the policy pays. A level term policy pays a flat sum assured; an increasing-cover option raises it at a stated rate, for a higher premium. Because a fixed sum assured loses purchasing power, its real value declines. An illustrative calculation, not a projection: ₹1 crore paid twenty years from now, discounted at 6 per cent inflation, is worth roughly ₹31 lakh in today's money.

The **need curve** is what the household would actually require, and for most families it declines. A mortgage amortises, children become independent, retirement assets accumulate. Against a shrinking need, a level sum assured is not obviously deficient.

The mismatch appears when the need is a fixed real obligation. Long-term care for a dependent does not shrink, and a level cover genuinely erodes against it. That is where an escalation provision earns its loading, and where the arithmetic of the two curves should be done rather than assumed. A decreasing cover tied to a mortgage tracks the liability directly: cheap and well matched, but it leaves nothing for anything else.

## The variants, and what they cost in structure

| Variant | What changes | Structural cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Level term | Flat sum assured throughout | The base case; cheapest for a given initial cover |
| Increasing term | Sum assured rises on a stated basis | Higher premium, funding the escalation |
| Return of premium | Premiums refunded on survival to maturity | Materially costlier for the same protection; the extra premium funds the refund |
| Decreasing or mortgage cover | Sum assured tracks a declining loan balance | Low premium, narrow purpose |
| Employer group cover | Cover provided through the employer's arrangement | Cheap, but typically ends with employment and is rarely portable |

Return of premium deserves the most care, because it converts a protection decision into a savings question under the guise of getting the money back. The refund is not a windfall; it is funded by the additional premium. The test is to compare the total cost of that policy against level term plus the same premium difference invested on a realistic, after-tax assumption over the same term.

The bundled shapes sit further along the same spectrum. An endowment plan pays the sum assured plus accrued bonuses on survival, so the premium contains a savings contract with guaranteed and non-guaranteed elements. A ULIP holds the policy's value in units of chosen funds, and IRDAI's linked-product regulations cap the premium-allocation, fund-management, policy-administration and discontinuance charges that may be deducted. Both are legitimate contracts; both should be judged by the price of the protection inside them and the cost of the savings inside them, separately.

## Underwriting: the price is personal

A term premium is not a market price; it is a price offered to one applicant. It is set from age at entry, term, sum assured, gender, tobacco and nicotine use, medical and family history, occupation and hazardous pursuits, and the underwriting class assigned after any medical tests.

Two forces explain why insurers ask so much. **Adverse selection** means people who know they are unwell are more likely to buy cover, so a pool priced uniformly would deteriorate. **Moral hazard** means insurance can change behaviour. Underwriting, exclusions and the disclosure obligation are the tools that hold both in check.

The practical consequence is that the application form is the contract's foundation. A material fact concealed at proposal — a diagnosis, a habit, an existing policy — gives the insurer grounds to dispute a claim years later, when the family is least able to argue. IRDAI's framework also provides a free-look period during which a new policy can be returned, which is the right moment to read the document against what was sold.

## Claims, and the insurer's ability to pay

On a death claim the nominee notifies the insurer, submits the prescribed documents, and the insurer assesses admissibility against the policy and the disclosures made at proposal. IRDAI's protection-of-policyholders framework prescribes the timelines and the grievance-redressal route that apply where a claim is rejected or delayed; the operative procedure belongs to that framework and to the policy document, and should be read there.

The second question matters more over long horizons. A term policy is an unsecured promise to pay decades after it was sold. For a policy written by the state-owned life insurer, the sovereign's ownership is part of the assessment; for a private insurer, the promise is as strong as that insurer's balance sheet plus the regulator's solvency supervision and the disclosures insurers must make. Insurer financial strength therefore belongs in the decision alongside the premium.

## Explain simply

Term insurance pays your family a large sum if you die during the policy period, and nothing if you do not. Because most people do not die young, the price of that promise is small relative to the sum it protects. It is not an investment and has no maturity value — which is exactly why it is cheap. Buy enough to cover what your family would actually have to pay for if your income stopped, read the policy document, answer the medical questions honestly, and price anything sold alongside it separately.

## Worked example: sizing a household's cover

An illustrative calculation, not advice and not a quotation. A 35-year-old earns ₹18,00,000 a year after tax. The household needs ₹12,00,000 a year, in today's rupees, for twenty years until the children are independent. A mortgage of ₹40,00,000 is outstanding; education is expected to cost ₹50,00,000 in fifteen years; final costs and a transition buffer are put at ₹5,00,000. Existing employer group cover is ₹40,00,000, and ₹10,00,000 of liquid assets is earmarked for this purpose.

At a 3 per cent real discount rate, the income component is the present value of ₹12,00,000 a year for twenty years:

$$ 12,00,000 \times \frac{1 - (1.03)^{-20}}{0.03} \approx 1,78,53,000 $$

The education commitment discounted at the same rate is about ₹32,09,000. Adding the mortgage and immediate costs gives ₹2,55,62,000; subtracting existing cover and earmarked assets leaves roughly **₹2,05,60,000**.

Now change one assumption. At a 5 per cent real discount rate the income component falls to about ₹1,49,55,000 and the education commitment to about ₹24,05,000, so the required cover becomes roughly **₹1,68,60,000**. The assumption, not the arithmetic, moved the answer by more than ₹35 lakh.

The honest output is a range of roughly ₹1.7 crore to ₹2.1 crore on these assumptions; the decision is where in that range to sit, not which single figure is correct.

## What this does not tell you

This article sets out contract structure and a sizing method. It does not identify an insurer, a rider or a bundled policy worth buying, because that requires the actual documents and prices. The discount rate used above is a judgement, and equally defensible judgements produce materially different figures, which is why the range matters more than any point inside it.

Tax is a separate lane and is not restated here. The treatment of premiums, of a death benefit and of any refund is governed by the Income-tax Act as amended by successive Finance Acts, and it changes; where a proposal rests on a tax advantage, read the provisions in their current form, treating the renumbering introduced by recent Finance Acts as pending verification. Nothing here is a recommendation, and no outcome is promised.

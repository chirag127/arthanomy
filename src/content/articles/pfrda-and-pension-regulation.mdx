Retirement saving fails in a way that is invisible for most of the time it is happening. The contribution leaves a salary today and the consequence arrives thirty years later, so the saver never sees the record that a manager's charges, an allocation choice or an interrupted contribution is quietly shaping. Pension regulation exists to make that long, quiet accumulation legible and to constrain the people handling it. In India the body that carries this work for the National Pension System is the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority, and its design rests on a single idea: no one institution should be able to record the money, hold the money and invest the money at the same time.

This article, part of the Indian Financial System series, sets out what the pension regulator does, how the National Pension System is layered, how investment choice and charges are constrained, how exit and annuitisation work, and where the pension perimeter ends.

## Explain simply

The pension regulator runs the rules for a national retirement savings account. You contribute through an approved point of presence, an agency keeps the records, a trust holds the assets, and licensed fund managers invest them in a small menu of funds. You choose how aggressive or cautious the mix is, within limits the regulator sets, and you pay a charge that is capped by regulation rather than set at the point of sale. When you reach the exit age, part of the accumulated corpus must be used to buy an annuity, which pays you a regular income for life. The regulator controls the structure — who does what, what you may invest in, what it costs and when you may withdraw — but it does not guarantee the market return, because the account is a defined-contribution pot whose size depends on contributions and performance.

## What the pension regulator is, and what it governs

The authority is the statutory regulator for the pension sector. Its remit covers the National Pension System and the related government-backed scheme that sits alongside it, together with the institutions that make the system work: the trustees that hold the assets, the fund managers that invest them, the central record-keeping agency, the points of presence through which contributions arrive, and the annuity service providers that convert a corpus into income.

The word to hold on to is *defined contribution*. The regulator does not fix the pension a subscriber will receive, as a defined-benefit scheme would. What it sets instead is the machinery around the contribution: the investment menu and the caps on how much may sit in each asset class, the charges that may be levied, the conditions on which money may be withdrawn, and the point at which a portion must be annuitised. Every one of those is a decision the regulator makes on the subscriber's behalf, because the subscriber cannot practically negotiate them.

## The layers: who records, who holds, who invests

The system is deliberately split into functions that a single asset-management company might otherwise combine. The separation is the protection.

- **The subscriber.** Owns the account and the contributions, and chooses the investment option within the permitted range.
- **The point of presence.** An approved intermediary — often a bank branch or a post office — that onboards the subscriber and transmits contributions, and that is the first place a servicing problem surfaces.
- **The central record-keeping agency.** Maintains the individual account, credits contributions, issues statements and processes withdrawals. It is the source of truth for what the subscriber holds.
- **The pension funds.** Licensed managers that invest the accumulated assets in the permitted asset classes. They do not hold the assets or the records.
- **The trust.** Holds the assets on behalf of the subscribers, keeping them outside the balance sheets of the managers and intermediaries.
- **The annuity service providers.** Insurers that, at exit, take the annuitised portion of the corpus and pay a periodic income.

The design answers a narrow question: what is the worst that a single failure can do? Because the manager does not custody the assets and the record-keeper does not invest them, a failure in one layer is contained. That is the same logic by which securities are held through depositories rather than with the broker, and the same logic that separates a mutual fund's assets from its asset manager.

## Tier I and Tier II

A subscriber generally holds two accounts. The first is the long-term, locked-in account, oriented to retirement, with restrictions on when money may be withdrawn and a requirement that part of the corpus be annuitised at exit. The second is a voluntary, withdrawable account that offers similar investment choices but no lock-in, so it can be used more like an ordinary savings or investment account.

The distinction matters because the two accounts are frequently conflated. The locked account carries the pension framework's protections and restrictions; the voluntary account carries the same investment machinery but almost none of the retirement discipline. A household's decision about how much to put in each is a decision about how much of its saving it is willing to lock away, and it has consequences for both liquidity and tax treatment.

## Investment choice within prescribed caps

The pension system offers a small set of asset classes and a choice of how to combine them. Broadly, they span government securities, corporate debt, equity and, in some configurations, alternative assets. Two modes are usually available.

- **Active choice.** The subscriber selects the allocation, but only within the caps the regulator prescribes for each asset class. The caps exist because the framework is designed for retirement money, not for a subscriber's willingness to take risk at any moment.
- **Auto choice.** A lifecycle pattern shifts the allocation with the subscriber's age, tapering the equity share as retirement approaches. The subscriber chooses a risk profile rather than a fixed mix, and the glide path does the rest.

The precise caps, ceilings and glide paths are set by regulation and revised from time to time. They should be read from the current rule rather than from any summary, including this one. The structural point is that investment freedom is real but bounded, and that the bound is drawn by the regulator on the subscriber's behalf.

## Charges: low by design

Pension saving over a working life is unusually sensitive to cost, because every basis point of charge compounds against the subscriber for decades. The framework therefore treats charges as a regulated ceiling rather than a commercial negotiation. Fund-management charges, and the fees of the record-keeper and intermediaries, are constrained within limits the regulator sets for the system as a whole.

The reason to keep the mechanism in view, rather than a specific number, is that the number changes. What does not change is where the cost sits: between the contribution and the corpus, so the effect shows up not in a deduction the subscriber sees but in a smaller retirement balance twenty or thirty years later. This is the same arithmetic that governs mutual funds and unit-linked insurance, and it is the reason the pension system's low, capped costs are counted among its main design features.

## Fund-manager oversight and NAV reporting

The managers are licensed, and their performance is reported through the net asset value of each scheme, published periodically. The NAV is the honest unit of comparison, because it already reflects the manager's charges and the portfolio's market value; comparing two managers on anything other than their published, net-of-charge NAV series is comparing presentations rather than results.

Oversight is not only about returns. A manager can underperform without misconduct, and can misstate without underperforming, and the framework treats the two differently. The reported NAV series, the periodic disclosure of the portfolio, and the trust's custody of the assets together give the regulator and the subscriber a basis for judging the manager that does not depend on the manager's own narrative. Pension savers with decades to run should care less about a single year's ranking and more about whether the manager's process and charges are stable, because the money will be invested through more than one market cycle.

## Exit, withdrawal and annuitisation

Retirement money must actually become retirement income, and that is where the framework's exit rules do their work. Partial withdrawals are permitted in defined circumstances before exit. At exit, a prescribed portion of the corpus must be used to purchase an annuity from a regulated insurer, while the balance may be taken as a lump sum. The proportions, thresholds and the conditions under which the whole corpus may be withdrawn in specific cases are all prescribed by regulation and change over time.

Two consequences follow. First, the annuity requirement converts part of the accumulated corpus into a periodic income and transfers longevity risk — the risk of living longer than the money lasts — to the insurer. Second, it makes the price of annuities at the moment of retirement a first-order determinant of the pension, because a large lump sum is being exchanged for an income stream at whatever rates prevail that year. The annuity itself is an insurance contract, supervised under the insurance perimeter rather than the pension one.

## Where the pension regulator stops

The pension perimeter is narrower than the phrase "retirement saving" suggests. Several large retirement vehicles sit outside it.

| Vehicle | Sits under | What this means for the saver |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National Pension System | Pension regulator | Scheme rules, investment menu, charges and exit conditions are set within this framework |
| Employees' Provident Fund | Separate employment framework | Contribution rates, interest crediting and withdrawal rules are set under that framework |
| Small-savings schemes | Government scheme rules | Rates are notified administratively rather than determined by the pension regulator |
| Annuities | Insurance regulator | The income product is a contract with a regulated insurer |
| Bank deposits | Banking regulator | Covered, up to a prescribed limit, by deposit insurance; pension accounts are not |

The household-level consequence is that "retirement money" is rarely one product. It is a portfolio of vehicles governed by different authorities, and a change to one — a new contribution ceiling, a rate reset, an annuity rule — is made by the body that owns that vehicle, not by the pension regulator.

## Worked example: an illustrative accumulation and annuitisation

The figures below are an illustrative calculation, not a forecast, and not a statement about any scheme, manager or insurer. They are chosen to show how the mechanics interact, not to predict an outcome.

Assume, purely for illustration, a monthly contribution of Rs 5,000 invested for 30 years, earning a nominal 9% a year compounded monthly. The future value of that contribution stream is:

$$ FV = 5000 \times \frac{(1 + 0.0075)^{360} - 1}{0.0075} \approx 91,50,000 $$

So the illustrative corpus is about Rs 91.5 lakh. Now suppose, again purely to illustrate, that the rules require two-fifths of the corpus to be annuitised and that an annuity is available at an assumed 6% annual payout on the purchase price. Then roughly Rs 36.6 lakh is used to buy an annuity producing about Rs 2.2 lakh a year, and about Rs 54.9 lakh may be taken as a lump sum.

Every input in that example — the contribution, the return, the annuitisation proportion and the annuity rate — is an assumption, and the last two in particular are prescribed by rules that change. Substitute the current figures and the answer changes, which is the point: the pension is the product of a contribution habit, a market return nobody guarantees, and a set of regulatory proportions that are not in the saver's control.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the architecture and the mechanics of pension supervision; it does not quantify any of them. It does not state the current charge ceilings, the asset-allocation caps, the annuitisation proportions, the exit thresholds or any annuity rate, because those are prescribed and revised and should be read at the source. It does not evaluate any fund manager, and it does not predict what a corpus will become, because the return is a market outcome the framework explicitly does not guarantee. And it does not resolve the interaction between the pension system and the other retirement vehicles a household may hold, which is a question of household balance-sheet design rather than of regulation.

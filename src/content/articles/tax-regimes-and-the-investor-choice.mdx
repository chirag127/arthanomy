Most Indian taxpayers now face a decision that did not exist in this form a decade ago: which of two personal income-tax regimes to be assessed under. The new regime is the default, and it asks for very little documentation because it gives up almost every deduction. The old regime keeps the familiar deduction architecture and pays for that with narrower slabs. Neither is universally better. The answer depends on the size and composition of a taxpayer's income, and on how much of it is investment income rather than salary.

This article sets out what genuinely differs between the two regimes, what does not differ, and the method by which a comparison should be run. Every rate and threshold carries the period to which it applies. It is a reference page: the numbers live in the tax registry that powers this site's calculators, and the primary sources sit one click away.

## The two regimes in one paragraph

Both regimes tax the same total income under the same charging machinery. What changes is the rate schedule applied to ordinary income and the deductions allowed against it. The new regime applies wider slabs with a higher nil band and a higher rebate threshold, but disallows most Chapter VI-A deductions, house rent allowance, leave travel allowance, and interest on a self-occupied property. The old regime retains those deductions and applies narrower slabs. The new regime is the default, and its provision was s.115BAC of the Income-tax Act, 1961 — now s.202 under the Income-tax Act, 2025.

| Position | Default | Opt-in |
| Nil band for an individual below 60 | ₹4,00,000 | ₹2,50,000 |

| Rebate amount | Up to ₹60,000 | Up to ₹12,500 |

| Standard deduction on salary | ₹75,000 | ₹50,000 |

## Slabs and the rebate

The new regime's slabs for FY 2025-26 are: nil up to ₹4,00,000; 5% on the next band up to ₹8,00,000; 10% up to ₹12,00,000; 15% up to ₹16,00,000; 20% up to ₹20,00,000; 25% up to ₹24,00,000; and 30% above ₹24,00,000. For an individual below 60, the old regime's slabs are: nil up to ₹2,50,000; 5% up to ₹5,00,000; 20% up to ₹10,00,000; and 30% above ₹10,00,000, with a higher basic exemption for resident senior and super senior citizens.

The rebate completes the picture. Under the new regime a resident individual with taxable income not exceeding ₹12,00,000 may claim a rebate of up to ₹60,000, which at that level effectively cancels the slab tax. Under the old regime the corresponding rebate is up to ₹12,500 where taxable income does not exceed ₹5,00,000. Two limits matter. First, the rebate is not available against income chargeable at special rates such as capital gains: it shelters slab income, not a s.111A or s.112A gain. Second, marginal relief operates where income just exceeds the new-regime rebate threshold, so income a little above ₹12,00,000 is not taxed at an effective rate far above the nominal band. Marginal relief is not the same as reducing a rate; it is computed at each boundary.

This is the structural claim the rest of the article rests on: the new regime is generous at low and middle income through its slabs and its rebate, while the old regime is generous only where the sum of the deductions it preserves exceeds that slab advantage.

## What the new regime gives up, and what the old regime keeps

The new regime disallows most Chapter VI-A deductions, house rent allowance, leave travel allowance, and interest on a self-occupied property. It retains three things: the standard deduction on salary of ₹75,000, the employer contribution to the National Pension System under s.80CCD(2), and the Agniveer contribution deduction. The survival of the s.80CCD(2) employer-contribution deduction is the most consequential of these for a salaried investor, because it is a deduction that ordinary salaried taxpayers can often increase by asking an employer to restructure, and it is the rare deduction that is not lost by defaulting to the new regime.

The old regime retains the older architecture. Contributions to instruments such as the Public Provident Fund, the Employees' Provident Fund and the Sukanya Samriddhi Account qualify for the s.80C-type deduction in the old regime; the National Pension System carries an additional ₹50,000 under s.80CCD(1B), again in the old regime only; and the interest deductions under s.80TTA and s.80TTB — up to ₹10,000 and ₹50,000 respectively — are available only in the old regime. The old regime also allows a loss under the house-property head to be set off against other heads up to ₹2,00,000; that set-off is restricted in the new regime. For a mortgaged owner-occupied property, this single difference can decide the comparison.

Note what does not change. The exempt treatment of Public Provident Fund and Sukanya Samriddhi interest and maturity proceeds is available in both regimes; only the deduction on contribution is confined to the old regime. A taxpayer who has already contributed does not lose the exemption by switching.

## What does not change: the capital-gains layer

A common error is to treat the regime choice as a capital-gains decision. It is not. The special rates sit outside the regime comparison:

- Short-term gains on securities-transaction-tax-paid listed equity shares and equity-oriented fund units are taxed at a flat 20% under s.111A, in place of slab rates, in both regimes.
- Eligible long-term gains on the same assets are taxed at 12.5% above an aggregate annual exemption of ₹1,25,000 under s.112A, without indexation, in both regimes.
- Other long-term capital gains are taxed at 12.5% without indexation under s.112, with a transitional option for a resident individual or HUF to pay 20% with indexation instead where land or a building was acquired before 23 July 2024, whichever produces the lower tax.
- Gains on specified mutual funds acquired on or after 1 April 2023 are deemed short-term under s.50AA and are added to total income at slab rates, with no indexation. This is the one capital-gains category in which the regime choice bites, because the gain is taxed as ordinary income and therefore flows through the regime's slabs.
- Redemption of a Sovereign Gold Bond by an individual at maturity is exempt; interest on the bond is taxable at slab rates and so is regime-sensitive, and a sale in the secondary market is taxable.

The regime therefore does not change the rate on a s.111A or s.112A gain. It changes the tax on the rest of the return, and thus the effective rate across the whole return. It also matters for the one capital-gains route that is taxed at slab — specified mutual funds under s.50AA.

## Surcharge and cess in both regimes

Surcharge is levied on the income tax itself at graduated rates keyed to total income: nil up to ₹50 lakh; 10% on the portion of tax where total income exceeds ₹50 lakh; 15% above ₹1 crore; 25% above ₹2 crore; and above ₹5 crore, 25% under the new regime or 37% under the old regime. Marginal relief applies at each boundary so that crossing a threshold does not reduce net income.

Two features matter to an investor. First, the 25% and 37% rates do not apply to income chargeable under s.111A, s.112 or s.112A, or to dividend income, where the maximum surcharge is 15% in both regimes. Second, because that cap is confined to those categories, ordinary income — interest, salary, business income — can carry the full scale. A high-income taxpayer with substantial fixed-deposit interest and a long-term equity gain pays a higher surcharge on the interest than on the gain, and the regime choice widens that gap above ₹5 crore. Health and education cess is 4% of income tax plus surcharge in both regimes, so the effective rate is always materially above the headline rate.

## Explain simply

There are two ways to be taxed, and you pick one each year. The new one has friendlier rates and a bigger free band, but it ignores most of the deductions people used to claim. The old one keeps those deductions but taxes you on narrower slabs and can charge a higher surcharge at the very top. Your salary, rent, home loan and investments decide which is cheaper. Equity gains are taxed the same way in both, so the choice is really about the rest of your income. Run both on your own numbers; the calculator will do it.

## How investment income slots into the choice

- **Bank and post-office interest.** Fully taxable at slab rates with no special rate, in both regimes. The old regime adds the s.80TTA and s.80TTB deductions, which the new regime does not: a senior citizen with a large deposit book can preserve up to ₹50,000 of interest from tax in the old regime, subject to the conditions of the provision.
- **Dividends.** Taxable in the shareholder's hands at slab rates. The 15% surcharge cap applies, but the rate itself is the marginal slab rate, so a high-income investor keeps less of the headline yield.
- **Listed equity capital gains.** As above: 20% short-term under s.111A, 12.5% long-term above ₹1,25,000 under s.112A, identical in both regimes.
- **Specified debt mutual funds.** Deemed short-term under s.50AA and taxed at slab rates, so both the regime and the level of other income change the outcome.
- **Sovereign Gold Bonds.** Interest at 2.50% per annum (tranche-dependent) is taxable at slab rates; redemption at maturity is exempt for an individual.
- **Virtual digital assets.** A flat 30% on the gain under s.115BBH, with no deduction other than cost and no loss set-off; surcharge and cess apply on top. This rule is recorded as provisional in the tax registry because its surrounding mechanics are not individually re-verified.
- **Real estate investment trusts and infrastructure investment trusts.** Distributions are taxed component-wise, with interest and dividend components at slab rates and a repayment-of-debt component treated as a return of capital; gains on the units fall under the s.111A and s.112A framework.

The pattern is consistent: where income is taxed at slab, the regime matters; where it is taxed under a special provision, it does not, subject to the surcharge scale above ₹5 crore.

## Worked example: one income, two regimes

This is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast or a recommendation. It uses only the figures stated above, and applies no Chapter VI-A deduction whose ceiling this article does not state.

A resident individual below 60 has salary income of ₹12,50,000 and savings-account interest of ₹20,000 for FY 2025-26.

**New regime.**

- Taxable income: ₹12,50,000 − ₹75,000 (standard deduction) + ₹20,000 (interest, no s.80TTA available) = **₹11,95,000**.
- Slab tax: 5% of ₹4,00,000 = ₹20,000, plus 10% of ₹3,95,000 = ₹39,500, total **₹59,500**.
- Taxable income is within ₹12,00,000, so the rebate of up to ₹60,000 applies and the slab tax becomes **nil**. Cess on nil is nil.
- **Total tax: nil.**

**Old regime.**

- Taxable income: ₹12,50,000 − ₹50,000 (standard deduction) + ₹20,000 − ₹10,000 (s.80TTA on savings interest) = **₹12,10,000**.
- Slab tax: 5% of ₹2,50,000 = ₹12,500, plus 20% of ₹5,00,000 = ₹1,00,000, plus 30% of ₹2,10,000 = ₹63,000, total **₹1,75,500**.
- The ₹12,500 rebate applies only below ₹5,00,000 of taxable income, so it does not apply here.
- Cess at 4% = ₹7,020. **Total tax: ₹1,82,520.**

Even adding the additional ₹50,000 NPS deduction under s.80CCD(1B), which the old regime alone allows, reduces the old-regime total only to about ₹1,66,920 — still far above the new regime's nil at this income level. The distance narrows as deductions grow and as income rises; at the top of the scale it can reverse, because the old regime retains the 37% surcharge above ₹5 crore on ordinary income while the new regime caps at 25%. The point of the example is method, not outcome: the same two returns must be computed, and the calculator that does it is the **post-tax return calculator**, which applies these dated values inline.

## Who each regime tends to suit

The new regime tends to suit a taxpayer with high slab income and few deductions: a salaried earner with no home loan, a modest rent, and no meaningful Chapter VI-A basket. It also suits a taxpayer whose income sits near the rebate threshold, where the wider slabs and the ₹60,000 rebate do the work. The old regime tends to suit a taxpayer whose deductions are large — house rent allowance, home-loan interest on a self-occupied property, a full s.80C-type basket, and, for a senior citizen, the s.80TTB deduction. It can also suit a taxpayer with rental losses to set off, up to the ₹2,00,000 limit the old regime allows.

The crossover is not a rule of thumb. It depends on the exact deduction stack, on the split between slab income and special-rate income, and on where the taxpayer sits relative to the surcharge boundaries. That is why the honest answer is a computation, not a slogan.

## The switch mechanics, and the Act transition

The new regime is the default. A taxpayer without business income may switch between regimes in each assessment year, which turns the choice into an annual exercise. A taxpayer with business income can opt out of the default only once in a lifetime, using the prescribed form and within the time allowed before the return due date; that first choice deserves more care than an annual one.

One forward-looking caveat, recorded plainly: the Income-tax Act, 2025 takes effect from 1 April 2026, replacing the 1961 Act with restructured provisions, a single tax-year concept in place of previous year and assessment year, and renumbered forms. The new regime remains the default under s.202 and the slabs are unchanged for Tax Year 2026-27. The individual section mapping between the two Acts is flagged in this site's tax registry as **pending verification**, and this article will be updated, with its reviewed date changed, as official guidance settles it.

## What this does not tell you

This article does not compute a taxpayer's liability, and it deliberately omits the deduction ceilings that the Income Tax Department publishes for the full Chapter VI-A basket, because only the limits stated above are used in the worked example. It does not cover special cases — a taxpayer with business income facing the one-time opt-out, a non-resident whose position differs on several heads, or the treatment of foreign assets. It does not quantify the value of the s.80CCD(2) employer contribution in an individual case, which depends on the employment contract. And it does not resolve the 1961-to-2025 mapping. Each of those is a reason to compute the comparison on the actual numbers rather than to rely on the pattern.

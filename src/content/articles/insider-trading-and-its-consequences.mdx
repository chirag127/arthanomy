Every Indian investor who has worked long enough in the markets has heard a version of the same sentence. It usually arrives informally, from someone who "happens to know", that a result is going to be good, a deal is going to be announced, or a management change is coming. The temptation it creates is real: a small trade on a strong lead, made quickly, can look like the most ordinary thing in the world. The trouble is that the sentence, if it is true and not public, converts that trade from an investment decision into conduct that the regulator calls insider trading, which is not a civil problem to be settled later but a matter with financial, professional and, in the appropriate cases, criminal consequence. This article sets out why that is, how far the prohibition stretches, and how the machinery of leaks and enforcement actually runs.

## Explain simply

Insider trading is buying or selling a company's securities while you hold information about that company that the market does not, and that is likely to move the price when it does become known. The kind of information that counts is defined in securities law, and the rule covers not only directors and employees but investment banks advising on transactions, lawyers writing deal documents, auditors seeing problems in the accounts, and people who pass the information on to friends. Two things surprise people. First, the trade is not the whole offence — communicating the information, and enabling another to trade on it, are themselves regulated. Second, profit is not the point: whether or not the tip produces money, the misuse of the access is the offence. The practical answer for an investor is plain: trade only on information that any stranger could have found on the exchange website, and verify every promising private lead against a public disclosure before acting.

## What counts as unpublished price-sensitive information

The protected category is called, in Indian securities terminology, unpublished price-sensitive information, or UPSI. At its core it is information that is not generally available and that, if it became known, would be likely to materially affect the price of the company's securities.

The archetypal examples are familiar to everyone who reads company announcements. Financial results before they are released. The preliminary stages of a merger, acquisition, disposal, demerger or other restructuring. Share issuances, buybacks, amalgamation schemes and delistings. Major changes in capital structure. Defaults on loans or default on payment obligations to lenders, and restructuring or resolution of debt. Material changes in senior management or control. Seizure of assets, serious litigation outcomes, and regulatory actions against the company. Joint ventures, licensing or other commercial agreements whose significance would be obvious to a reasonable investor.

The general definition matters more than any list. The test is functional: would a reasonable investor, on hearing this, revise their view of the company and therefore their price? A list of examples is useful, but it invites cleverness about what is not listed. The functional test does not invite the same evasions, and courts and regulators apply it in substance. The harder press comes in judging timing: information can be price-sensitive long before it is written down as a decision — the first exploratory approach for an acquisition, an internal forecast revision, a single large default in the book. People inside companies routinely know that something is brewing before there is a board resolution, and that is precisely the period in which the law expects confidentiality.

## Who is covered

The prohibition does not regulate names on an org chart; it regulates access. Those inside the company, the directors and employees, are plainly covered, but so are advisers, consultants, bankers, lawyers, auditors and valuers who hold the information for a purpose other than trading — research analysts depending on published disclosures are held to different standards, though the boundary blurs where their non-public contact is concerned. Then there are the connected and communicational categories: anyone who has a connection, professional, contractual, fiduciary or personal, with the company by virtue of which they may reasonably have access to the information. Relatives and friends who learn it from an insider sit squarely within the reach of the framework. People who merely observe conduct, a courier, a secretary, a catering contract worker at a deal meeting, have also been reached in enforcement. The law's thrust is to make the duty of confidentiality attach to the information rather than to the title.

A second consequence of this breadth is that a recipient is not excused by how far removed they are from the company. A tip passed through three hands and traded by the fourth is a chain, and the chain is exactly what investigators trace. The language of "information leakage" captures it: the duty is not discharged by readdressing the letter.

## The compliance architecture inside companies

Companies subject to these rules carry their own conduct obligations, and the office of the compliance officer exists more for this purpose than for any other. The architecture has four parts.

Trading windows and blackout periods restrict who may trade and when, particularly around expected results and corporate announcements. Pre-clearance requires senior personnel and designated employees to obtain approval before executing personal trades. Disclosure thresholds require officials to report their holdings and changes in them, so that the regulator and the market can see whether managers' trades align with what they know. Need-to-know protocols restrict access to material information within the company to those whose function requires it, which reduces both the risk of leaks and the circle of people who later have to answer questions about possession.

These rules are not bureaucratic formality. Because the prohibition attaches to the information, a company with vague practices finds that almost everyone becomes a potential inside trader, and a company with tight ones can show the regulator their demonstrated control. A further consequence sits in acquisitions: an offeror for a listed target, and its advisers, must wall off information about the offer so that the offeror's own trading does not become a problem before the announcement.

## How information leaks

Leaks rarely begin as malice. They begin as confidence, social pressure, and the instinct that a secret shared is a secret devalued. The reliable leak paths, in the order in which they appear in enforcement experience, are these.

Professional advisers. An investment banker, lawyer, auditor or valuer engaged on a transaction needs the information to do their work, and the transaction itself creates the knowledge. Consultants and developers with access to confidential project detail sit in the same camp. These people are professionals with confidentiality duties, and the breach occurs in the small suitcase of family or close friends, who are harder to check and less aware.

Vendors and service providers. Printers, travel agents, caterers and support services at events where the deal is discussed can learn the fact by position. None of them is a director, and all of them can trade or tip.

Corporate insiders and their circles. A divisional head who knows the quarterly run-rate, an executive who knows a pending resignation, a treasury functionary who knows a refinancing problem. They tell a spouse, a parent, a sibling, a business partner, who tells a friend, who trades. The second and third links in the chain do not think of themselves as part of a chain.

The media and research ecosystem. Briefings given on a background basis, under embargo, create information that a reporter or a buy-side analyst possesses before the market does. Embargoed information is still unpublished information in law.

Social and professional networks. Physical proximity at industry gatherings, school, alumni and family networks are common channels, not because anyone plots them but because information is currency in these groups.

The conduct itself is ordinary. The breach is in the use.

## Enforcement architecture and outcomes

Securities laws in India empower the regulator to investigate, adjudicate and enforce against insider trading. The specific statutes, sections and procedural rules are not summarised here, because their text is not among this article's sources, and any reader who needs precision should go to the official text rather than to a summary in general writing. What can be described from the supporting sources is the shape of the response and the public record.

The regulator's market-conduct framework governs registered intermediaries, ESOP and trading practices, and the dissemination of market-moving information. Its enforcement orders, published on the regulator's website, are the primary documentary source for how rules are applied in practice. Each order states the facts found, the reasoning, and any penalty, restriction, disgorgement or direction imposed. Reading a collection of them alongside the market-conduct framework gives a clearer picture of expected conduct than any single guidance document, because they are decisions in specific cases rather than generalities, and because they show where the regulator drew the line in situations that look close to the edge.

The remedies that the framework contemplates include disgorgement of profits made or loss avoided, monetary penalties, prohibition from accessing the markets for a period, bar from holding positions in listed companies, and directions to cooperate with future investigations. Where the facts are serious, referral to criminal authorities for prosecution under the penal framework is available. Suspension and cancellation of registration affect intermediaries; permanent market bars affect individuals. The severity in a particular matter depends on the size of the gross profit, the seniority and position of the person, the deliberateness of the conduct, the cooperation shown during investigation, and whether the party settles or contests.

Two observations from the public record are worth attention. First, the remedial approach treats profit as the measure: the consequence is scaled to the gain, which means the incentive structure is severe at the margin. Second, the investigation phase is long, and the outcome arrives years after the trade, which is itself a penalty: reputation, credit access and employment prospects are damaged by the pendency even before any finality. A regulatory investigation is not a private event, and the names in an interim order remain searchable long after the facts have ceased to be market moving.

## What the enforcement record tends to find

Reading the enforcement orders rather than reading about them changes what one thinks is true. A few patterns recur, and an investor benefits from knowing them.

Communication is usually traceable. Regulators do not rely on the content of trades alone; phone records, email, messaging, bank records, and synchronised trades between connected accounts are what prove the chain. Sophisticated participants who use multiple accounts or names are caught on the cross-referencing rather than on the trade.

Timing is the tell. Trades concentrated in the days and weeks before an announcement, and reversed immediately after, are a strong signal, and they are reviewed systematically now. A trader who wins repeatedly on events can expect documentary scrutiny.

Small sizes do not exempt. A modest profit on a small quantity is still a finding in the enforcement literature. The idea that small trades are below the radar is empirically false.

Settlements are common but not free. Settlement mechanisms allow parties to consent to directions and close a matter without an adjudicated finding, sometimes on terms. The public record of a settlement is still a record, and it still carries restrictions and penalties.

Cooperation counts. Parties who recognise the matter early, quantify the gain and cooperate have historically obtained more favourable outcomes than those who contest to the end. The converse is that delay, concealment and false explanations convert a trading matter into a much more serious one.

## The exposure of receiving a tip

The phrase "tip from a friend" is the clearest way to end up on the wrong side of this law. Suppose a friend who works at a company casually mentions at a dinner that a deal is imminent and suggests that trading is a good idea. Three separate things can happen next, each of them serious.

First, trading on the information. Even if the friend did not intend to confer an advantage, and even if the trader understood themselves to be in the wrong by trading on it, the trade is the conduct the rule was written to prevent. The chain — the corporate source, the intermediary, and the ultimate trader — is how insider-trading findings usually run, and the party at the end of the chain is commonly the trader rather than the source.

Second, communication. Passing the same information on to a spouse, a colleague or a chat group is a breach in its own right. The duty of confidentiality sits with whoever holds the information, and everyone in the chain owes it.

Third, the perception problem. Even an innocent trade that happens to coincide with an event will be reviewed against trading records and communication evidence. The burden of explaining it falls on the trader. The safest status, apart from the legal one, is simply not having made the trade.

## Inside information in ordinary situations

Information does not become price-sensitive only in the boardroom. Ordinary professional and personal situations routinely create it.

M&A and strategic advice. Investment bankers and lawyers on either side of a transaction know before anyone else. So do the corporate development teams at both companies, the accountants, the lenders arranging finance, and their respective associates.

Audit and regulatory work. An auditor who discovers a serious control failure, a tax inspector, a regulator at an enforcement agency, and the company's own internal audit team all hold information that the market would price instantly.

Results. Finance teams preparing quarter-end numbers, consolidating subsidiaries, and reviewing management accounts know the direction of the result before the board is shown, which means senior accounts staff, group controllers and some external consultants know too. So do their secretaries and assistants.

Credit stress. Lenders in a restructuring, advisors to distressed companies, suppliers about to get payment, and employees in divisions losing key contracts all know before the announcement.

HR and management. A pending CEO resignation, known to the board, to the search firm, to the chairman's office, is typical UPSI. Industry colleagues notice patterns before disclosures confirm them.

Deal teams in M&A situations face the sharpest conflicts because their day job is both to know and to trade, and the offering side has its own restrictions. The average investor does not have these conflicts. What the investor does have is the more dangerous one: the belief that the trade is small enough or the edge is soft enough that no one notices.

## What companies try to do about it

A well-run listed company treats unpublished information as an asset with a custodian. The controls are ordinary.

- Classification and access control. Material information is classified, stored, and disclosed on a need-to-know basis; external distribution is channelled through the investor-relations function.
- Blackout windows. No trading by designated people during the run-up to expected disclosures, and often around corporate events.
- Pre-clearance regimes. Designated people ask permission before trading, and windows are confirmed open before execution.
- Disclosure timing. Material developments, once finalised, are disclosed promptly and in a prescribed form so that the information is public before anyone can be accused of using it privately.
- Insider logs. Companies keep records of who has been given price-sensitive information for an event, so that the circle of concern is known to the company even if not to the regulator.
- Whistleblowing and breach escalation. Mechanisms by which employees can report suspected leaks without retaliation, and in which the response to a breach involves immediate tightening of controls.

These controls reduce leakage. They cannot eliminate it, and they are most vulnerable where the culture treats them as a formality rather than as the actual control.

## Myth and clarification

Myth: only directors commit insider trading. In fact the covered set reaches anyone with access through position or connection, and to the communication chain rather than the conduct alone.

Myth: not making a profit means not committing the offence. The prohibited conduct is the trading, procuring and communicating on the basis of the information; financial consequence is the remedy, not the definition.

Myth: information heard from a friend, as opposed to from the company, is harmless. The communication itself is prohibited whether the tipper intends a benefit and whether the source is identifiable.

Myth: pre-trading disclosure or pre-clearance screens out liability. Pre-clearance within one's own company's scheme is a control, not a defence to the securities-law prohibition, and the company's compliance framework does not ratify a trade that the law prohibits.

Myth: withholding publication to avoid market disruption is acceptable. The timing of market disclosure is regulated; private trading on the withheld information in the interim is not justified by the purpose.

## What investors should actually do

The exposure of an ordinary investor is neither dramatic nor abstract. It sits in the inbox, in messaging groups, and at family events. The following sequence keeps it controlled.

- Recognise the shape of the information. If a stock tip could not plausibly have come from a public source that the recipient has read, assume it is inside information.
- Not act on it. The rule is not "trade carefully". It is do not trade for the period during which the information remains private, and longer.
- Verify against official channels. Exchange announcements, company filings and press releases are the safe sources; the exchange website is the single reliable copy of what the market knows.
- Recognise that even accurate-looking tips can be inaccurate. The information content of a rumour is often wrong; a trade on it therefore carries both legal risk and accuracy risk.
- Disclose suspicions through the official channels. The regulator's unsolicited-tip route exists for reporting suspected unregistered activity and market misconduct; the use of it is the appropriate response to a suspicious lead.
- Set the house rule. No trade in a security within a defined period after any non-public-sounding conversation about it, except on the basis of public information alone. Written down, it removes the discretionary judgement at the moment when judgement is least reliable.
- Treat "I did not know it was inside information" as weak. The legal question is usually settled on the substance, and ignorance of the law's scope is rarely the controlling factor.

## The verification checklist before any trade on non-public-sounding information

1. Write down the source of the information and ask whether it could plausibly be public.
2. If it cannot, make no trade and no communication of the information, including to close friends.
3. Check the exchange and the company's own investor-relations page for the announcement that would correspond to the tip.
4. If no such announcement exists, treat the information as private and the trade as prohibited.
5. If an announcement does exist, the trade may be re-evaluated, but on its merits, slowly and with documentation.
6. Document the reasoning and the source trail in the event of a subsequent enquiry.
7. Report clearly abusive promotion or suspected leaks through the regulator's official channels.
8. Establish the house rule so that the first reaction to a promising private tip is systematically one of restraint.

Done in that order, an investor treats the law as a floor to stand above rather than as a line to be skirted. The techniques of this series — verifying sources, reading what is published rather than what is whispered, and building routines that make compliance automatic rather than discretionary — are the same techniques that keep the other frauds in this series at a distance. An edge built on public information can be evaluated, copied in robust form, and defended. An edge built on private information cannot.

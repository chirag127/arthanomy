Gold jewellery is bought for wearing and for value, but it is sold for metal. When an owner decides to resale or exchange an ornament, the price they receive is based on the value of the gold in the article — not on the amount originally paid, not on the design, and not on the sentimental or decorative value. The difference between what was paid and what is received is the round-trip cost of jewellery ownership, and it is the central fact of any resale transaction.

This article explains how resale valuation works, what deductions apply, and how to compare offers across jewellers. The goal is to let a seller walk into a buyback negotiation with a clear understanding of what their ornament is worth and what a fair offer looks like.

## What resale value is based on

The resale value of a gold ornament is computed from three inputs:

1. **The prevailing gold rate.** The current market price for gold of the relevant purity, quoted in rupees per gram or per ten grams. This rate moves daily and is the single largest variable in the transaction.
2. **The weight of the metal.** The gross weight of the ornament, which includes any stones, enamel or other materials. The jeweller must determine how much of that weight is actually gold.
3. **The purity of the gold.** The fineness of the metal, expressed in caratage or fineness in parts per thousand. A hallmark — the purity mark applied under the Bureau of Indian Standards regime — provides a statement about the article's purity to a declared grade.

The resale value is then the weight of pure gold multiplied by the prevailing rate, less any deductions the jeweller applies. The hallmark and the HUID — the six-digit alphanumeric identifier unique to each hallmarked article since July 2021 — help establish the purity without destructive testing, which makes the transaction cleaner and faster.

## The deductions that apply

Jewellers typically apply several deductions in computing a resale offer:

- **Purity loss.** If the article's actual purity is slightly below the declared grade, or if the jeweller assumes a margin for assay variation, a deduction is made for the shortfall. The specific deduction rate varies by jeweller and is not standardised.
- **Stone and non-gold weight.** If the ornament contains stones, enamel or other materials, the weight of those materials is deducted from the gross weight. Stones may be valued separately or taken at no value, depending on the jeweller.
- **Making charges.** These are never recovered. The fabrication fee paid when the article was purchased is a cost of converting metal into a finished product, and it is not part of the resale value.
- **Wear and damage.** Bent prongs, missing stones, worn surfaces and other damage may lead to a further deduction, or the article may be valued as scrap at the melt rate.

The net result is that the seller receives the value of the gold content at the prevailing rate, less a series of deductions. The gross metal rate quoted by the jeweller is not the amount the seller receives.

## Making charges: the permanent loss

Making charges are the fabrication fee charged by the jeweller for converting bullion into a finished article. They are quoted either as a lump sum per gram or as a percentage of the metal value, and they vary with design complexity and retailer. A plain bangle may carry a low making charge; an intricate necklace may carry a high one.

The critical fact about making charges is that they are **not recovered on resale**. When an ornament is sold back, the buyer values only the metal content. The making charge — which may have been a significant fraction of the purchase price — is simply lost. This is the single largest component of the round-trip cost of jewellery ownership and the reason jewellery is a poor vehicle for short-term gold-price exposure.

The purchase tax paid when the article was bought is also not recovered. The total round-trip cost is therefore: making charges on purchase, purchase tax on purchase, and the resale deduction on sale. All three must be overcome before the seller sees a profit.

## How to compare offers

Because deduction rates and buyback policies vary by jeweller, the same ornament may fetch different amounts from different buyers. The way to compare offers is not the gross metal rate quoted but the **net rupees per gram** the seller receives after all deductions.

A practical sequence:

1. **Weigh the article** on a reliable scale and note the gross weight.
2. **Note the purity** from the hallmark — the caratage and fineness declared on the article.
3. **Ask each jeweller for the net rate** they would pay per gram of pure gold, after all deductions.
4. **Compute the net realisation** by multiplying the net rate by the weight of pure gold in the article.
5. **Compare the net figures**, not the gross rates.

A jeweller who quotes a high metal rate but applies large deductions may offer less than one who quotes a lower rate with smaller deductions. The net figure is the only comparable number.

## Exchange versus cash buyback

Many jewellers offer an exchange rather than a cash buyback: the seller receives credit toward a new purchase, typically at the prevailing metal rate less deductions. The exchange may be more convenient, but it is not necessarily better value.

The exchange offer may include a waiver of making charges on the new article, which can be a genuine benefit if the seller intends to buy new jewellery. But it may also include less favourable deductions on the old article, or a requirement to spend more than the value of the old jewellery. The seller should compute the net value of the exchange — what the old article is worth in credit — and compare it to a cash buyback, then decide based on whether they want new jewellery or cash.

## Explain simply

When you sell gold jewellery, the buyer pays for the gold in it, not for the design or the workmanship. They weigh it, check how pure it is, and pay the current price for that much pure gold — minus a bit for any purity shortfall, stones or damage. The making charges you paid when you bought it are gone forever. So the amount you get back is always less than what you paid, unless the price of gold has risen enough to cover all the costs in between. To get the best price, ask different jewellers what they will pay per gram of pure gold after all their deductions, and compare those numbers, not the big rates they advertise.

## Worked example: the round-trip cost of jewellery

This is an illustrative calculation, not observed data, and the figures are hypothetical numbers chosen only to make the arithmetic visible. They are not the actual rates or deduction policies of any specific jeweller.

Suppose an investor buys a hallmarked ornament with a metal content of 20 grams of 22K gold. Assume the prevailing gold rate at the time of purchase is ₹6,500 per gram, making the metal value:

$$20 \times 6500 = 130000$$

Assume a making charge of 10% of the metal value and a purchase tax of 3% of the subtotal. The total purchase cost is:

$$130000 \times 1.10 \times 1.03 = 147290$$

Now suppose the investor sells the ornament two years later. The prevailing gold rate has risen to ₹7,800 per gram. The jeweller applies a deduction of 5% for purity loss and other adjustments. The resale value is:

$$20 \times 7800 \times 0.95 = 148200$$

The net round-trip result is:

$$148200 - 147290 = 910$$

So the investor makes a profit of ₹910 on a transaction in which the gold price rose by 20%. The making charges, purchase tax and resale deduction consumed almost the entire gain. If the gold price had been flat, the investor would have lost the sum of the making charges, the tax and the deduction — a loss of roughly ₹17,290 on this example.

The calculation is illustrative and depends entirely on the assumed figures. What is structural is the direction: the purchase costs and the resale deduction must be overcome before the seller sees any profit, and they can consume most or all of a moderate gold-price rise.

## The Gold Monetisation Scheme alternative

For an owner who wants to realise value from idle gold without selling to a jeweller, the Gold Monetisation Scheme offers an alternative channel. The scheme, administered under the aegis of the Government of India and the RBI, allows depositors to earn interest on deposited gold, which is melted and tested for purity. The specific terms — the minimum deposit, the interest rate and the redemption options — are set by the scheme and are not reproduced in this packet. The scheme suits owners who want to earn a return on idle gold rather than sell it, and it removes the making-charge and resale-deduction problem by valuing the metal at its melt worth.

Every market transaction rests on conduct that the parties cannot directly observe: whether an issuer's numbers are honest, whether a broker will settle faithfully, whether a clearing corporation will stand behind a defaulting member, whether the person who sold a product was entitled to sell it. Securities regulation is the apparatus that polices those questions. In India the apparatus is dominated by one institution — the Securities and Exchange Board of India — and its design has a specific shape: it protects investors mainly by compelling disclosure and regulating intermediaries, not by promising that investments will work.

This article, part of the Indian Financial System series, sets out what SEBI's mandate is, who sits inside its perimeter, how enforcement escalates, where its jurisdiction stops, and — the part with immediate practical use — where a market complaint actually belongs.

## What SEBI is, and where its authority comes from

SEBI began as an administrative body and became a statutory regulator when the SEBI Act, 1992 gave it powers anchored in the Act's stated purpose: to protect the interests of investors in securities, and to promote the development of, and to regulate, the securities market.

Those three verbs pull in different directions, and understanding that tension explains most contested decisions in Indian securities regulation. Investor protection argues for restriction and caution; market development argues for facilitation and lower friction. A regulator asked to do both will sometimes decline to prohibit something that would protect investors, because the prohibition would also suppress the market.

SEBI's authority in securities markets also rests on other statutes, principally the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956, which governs exchanges and contracts in securities, and the Depositories Act, 1996, which underpins dematerialised holding. The regulations SEBI writes under these statutes — on listing obligations and continuous disclosure, insider trading, takeovers, mutual funds, alternative investment funds, portfolio managers, investment advisers and research analysts — are the operative text that firms are actually governed by.

## A disclosure regulator, not a merit regulator

This is the single most important structural fact about Indian securities regulation, and the one most often misunderstood.

When an issuer raises money through a public offer, SEBI's role is not to certify that the business is sound, that the management is competent or that the price is fair. It reviews the disclosure document for adequacy and completeness, requires specified disclosures, and registers the offer. The investment judgement remains the investor's. A public issue can be entirely compliant and lose money; compliance is a statement about what was disclosed and how, not about what happens next.

Two consequences follow.

First, registration is a statement about process, not about merit. A SEBI-registered intermediary met entry conditions, holds a licence that can be suspended or cancelled, and is subject to conduct rules — which is valuable, and different from an endorsement of the products it sells.

Second, the whole architecture leans on disclosure being true. Because SEBI does not substitute its judgement for the investor's, the correctness of the information investors act on is the load-bearing wall. The most serious enforcement cases are therefore rarely about poor investment outcomes; they are about false or incomplete information, and about conduct that distorts price discovery — misleading statements, circular trading, market manipulation, misuse of inside information.

## The perimeter: who SEBI regulates

Four groups sit inside the securities perimeter.

- **Issuers.** Companies and other entities that raise money from the public or list securities must meet issue requirements and then continuous obligations: timely financial results, disclosure of material events, governance standards, and rules on insider trading and takeover conduct.
- **Intermediaries.** The licensed plumbing of the market — stock brokers and trading members, merchant bankers, registrar and transfer agents, underwriters, credit rating agencies, depository participants, custodians, investment advisers, research analysts and portfolio managers. Each category has entry conditions, conduct rules, record-keeping obligations and reporting duties.
- **Market infrastructure institutions.** Stock exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories, regulated with additional governance requirements because their failure would be systemic rather than individual.
- **Pooled vehicles.** Mutual funds through their asset management companies and trustees, alternative investment funds through their managers, and, at the fringes, collective investment schemes.

The perimeter concept is the most transferable idea in this article. Every financial product and every person offering one sits either inside a regulator's perimeter or outside it. Being outside is not automatically unlawful — much activity is legitimately unregulated — but the conduct standards, disclosure requirements and complaint routes that exist inside do not apply outside. A loss caused by an unregistered entity is not a regulatory failure to be remedied; it is a matter for the civil and criminal systems, and the protections are absent rather than deferred.

## Why exchanges and depositories get a different rulebook

A broker's failure is a problem for that broker's clients. A clearing corporation's failure is a problem for every participant, because settlement is the point at which the market's promises are kept or broken. That asymmetry explains why market infrastructure institutions are regulated more like utilities than like firms: ownership and governance restrictions, requirements that key decisions be taken by independent committees, restrictions on related-party dealings, and mandatory risk-management and settlement-guarantee arrangements.

It also explains why grievance handling is not centralised in SEBI alone: exchanges and depositories run front-line grievance cells and administer the investor protection funds described below, and a member's default is handled through the exchange's own default process so that the rest of the market keeps settling.

## Three powers in one body

SEBI exercises three distinct kinds of authority, and conflating them creates confusion about what it can and cannot do.

- **Quasi-legislative.** It writes regulations, circulars and directions that bind regulated entities with the force of subordinate legislation.
- **Quasi-executive.** It inspects intermediaries, obtains information, conducts surveillance and investigations, and issues directions.
- **Quasi-judicial.** It adjudicates — passes reasoned orders, imposes penalties and issues directions — subject to appeal, ordinarily to the Securities Appellate Tribunal and onward to the Supreme Court.

The concentration is deliberate and efficient: the same body that writes a rule can investigate a breach and decide the consequence. But it places a heavy burden on procedure, because the party on the other side is facing an investigator and a decision-maker under one roof. Show-cause notices, reasoned orders, disclosure of the material relied on, and statutory appeal rights are not formalities here; they are what makes the arrangement defensible.

## The enforcement ladder

Enforcement is a sequence rather than a single act, and the rungs escalate.

1. **Surveillance and inspection.** Automated monitoring of trading activity, periodic inspections of intermediaries, and information requests to issuers and market participants.
2. **Investigation.** A formal investigation by an investigating authority, with statutory powers to summon persons, require documents and examine witnesses.
3. **Interim orders.** Where conduct appears to be ongoing or the market needs immediate protection, SEBI can issue interim directions — for example to cease and desist, or to refrain from accessing the securities market, pending the outcome.
4. **Final orders.** After a show-cause notice and an opportunity of hearing: monetary penalties, disgorgement of unlawful gains, directions to persons and entities, market bans of varying scope and duration, and remedial directions.
5. **Referral and coordination.** Referral for criminal prosecution where the conduct involves offences, and coordination with other agencies and regulators.

Two features of this ladder are widely misread.

A **penalty is not compensation.** Money collected as a penalty accrues to the government. The investor who lost money is not the beneficiary of the penalty. Compensation requires a separate civil claim, or a settlement that provides for it, or — in some cases — a restitution direction requiring the wrongdoer to give up unlawful gains, which can return money to identifiable victims. Even then, recovery depends on the money still existing and being traceable.

An **interim direction is not a finding.** It is a protective measure taken before adjudication, and it can be contested and modified.

## The investor protection funds, and their limits

Alongside SEBI's own action, the exchanges and depositories maintain investor protection funds, financed by a share of transaction charges and unclaimed benefits. These compensate investors in limited circumstances — most commonly where a broker has defaulted and the default has been verified by the exchange — and coverage is capped per investor per default.

The limits are as instructive as the coverage. An investor protection fund does not compensate for market losses, for fraud in a scheme that was never registered, for advisory failures, or for an issuer's business collapse. It is a backstop for the specific failure of a member of the trading and settlement system, funded by that system, and it exists so that a single member's default does not destroy confidence in settlement. Treating it as investing insurance is a category error.

## Where SEBI stops

Much retail confusion in Indian finance comes from expecting one regulator to own every question. The perimeters have different owners.

| Question | Regulator | What protects the household |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bank or non-banking lender conduct, deposits, lending terms | RBI | Prudential supervision, the integrated ombudsman route, and deposit insurance at insured banks up to the statutory limit |
| Insurance products and claims | IRDAI | Product and conduct regulations, insurer disclosures, grievance-redressal obligations |
| National Pension System | PFRDA | Scheme rules, investment guidelines, exit and annuity conditions |
| Listed-company securities conduct | SEBI | Disclosure requirements, market-conduct rules, enforcement orders |
| Insolvency of a company or intermediary | NCLT and the insolvency framework | Creditor-triggered resolution, moratorium, and a distribution waterfall |
| Cheating, misappropriation, criminal fraud | Police and central investigating agencies | Criminal investigation and prosecution |

Deposit insurance is worth sitting with because it is the one place where a statutory compensation scheme exists. Cover runs to a prescribed limit per depositor per bank, as administered under the DICGC framework, and it covers deposits as defined — not securities, not insurance policies, and not units of a scheme. It also does not extend to every category of deposit or institution. Households that assume a deposit-like product is protected without checking which regulatory address it has are relying on a protection that may not exist.

## Explain simply

SEBI is the referee for the securities market. It writes the rules, inspects the players, and can fine, ban or prosecute those who break them, but it does not endorse investments and it does not pay you back when a bad investment loses money. Its core protection is that you must be told the truth and that the people handling your money must hold a licence and follow conduct rules. If a product or a seller is not registered with any financial regulator, none of that applies, and the right place to complain may be the police rather than any financial forum.

## Worked example: routing a market complaint

The routing below is illustrative of the process structure, not legal advice. The operative procedure is always the one published by the regulator or forum concerned, and it changes.

- **A broker has not paid out settled proceeds, or has traded without instruction.** Start with the broker's own grievance channel, then the investor grievance cell of the exchange of which the broker is a member, and then SEBI's SCORES platform, which forwards the complaint to the regulated entity and tracks the response. Where the failure is a verified broker default, the exchange's investor protection fund is the compensation route; where the complaint is about conduct rather than default, it is a disciplinary matter.
- **A listed company has withheld a dividend, failed to send documents, or its disclosures look false.** The route runs through the company's grievance channel, then the exchange, then SCORES against the company. Continuous disclosure obligations and enforcement against false disclosure fall inside SEBI's remit; the company's commercial performance does not.
- **Someone unregistered is advising on securities or running a pooled scheme.** The decisive check is registration status at the regulator's source, not in the marketer's material. SEBI publishes material on unregistered investment schemes and acts against unauthorised collective investment schemes and unregistered advisers. Absence of registration is not a technicality to be reassured about; it is the information that determines which protections exist.
- **The problem is a bank, a non-banking lender, an insurer or a pension product.** The route belongs to that regulator. The general packet rule of thumb applies across all of them: take the complaint to the regulated entity first, and expect a decision within roughly thirty days before escalating to the ombudsman, whose award is binding on the regulated entity up to a statutory limit.
- **The complaint is against a company that is simply failing.** Insolvency, liquidation and the distribution of whatever remains run through the tribunals under the insolvency framework, where claims rank differently and where retail unsecured recovery has often been low. That is a different institution with a different objective — maximising recoveries — not investor protection.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the architecture. It does not quantify enforcement outcomes — how many orders result in actual recovery, how long proceedings take, or what proportion of investor losses is made whole — because those figures require case-level data that the sources cited here do not carry. It also does not set out the detailed content of any single regulation; those documents are long, they are amended, and they should be read directly. And it does not resolve the perimeter's genuinely grey areas, the products and activities deliberately designed to sit between regulators, which are precisely where the protections described here are weakest. Where a specific penalty, limit or timeline matters, treat any summary, including this one, as a map rather than the territory.

Most financial failures that reach the newspapers are not bank failures. They are failures of companies that lent like banks, priced like banks and, to the person at the counter, looked like banks, while sitting outside the framework that makes a bank failure survivable. The non-banking financial company is the legal form behind a large share of Indian credit — vehicle and consumer finance, gold loans, microfinance, equipment leasing and investment companies. Understanding what the RBI actually supervises when it supervises an NBFC, and what that supervision deliberately does not do, is the difference between reading a rate as a yield and reading it as the price of a risk.

## Explain simply

A bank takes money that can be withdrawn on demand and promises to give it back, which is why banks are wrapped in protections: a licence that is hard to obtain, rules on capital and liquidity, continuous supervision, and an insurance scheme that pays depositors up to a statutory limit if the bank fails. An NBFC does much of the same lending business, but it is not a bank. It cannot take demand deposits, it is not part of the payment system the way a bank is, and if it is permitted to accept public deposits those deposits are not insured. Supervision of an NBFC is real and it is not decorative, but it is the supervision of a company that carries credit risk. It is not a guarantee that the money comes back.

## What an NBFC is, and the line it cannot cross

A non-banking financial company is a company, incorporated under the companies legislation, that has obtained a certificate of registration from the RBI to carry on the business of a financial institution. Its activity is defined by what it does with its balance sheet — lending, investing, leasing, hire purchase — rather than by any single product. The RBI sets the test for when a company's financial assets and financial income make it an NBFC in the first place, and that test is a notified threshold rather than a fixed idea; the operative numbers belong in the direction, not in a summary.

The line that matters most is the one an NBFC cannot cross. It is not a bank, so:

- it may not accept deposits repayable on demand, which is the defining privilege of a bank;
- it is not a member of the payment and settlement arrangements in the way a bank is, so it does not issue the instruments that clear as money;
- a public deposit with it is not a bank deposit for the purposes of deposit insurance.

Everything else about an NBFC — its size, its lending discipline, its funding mix — varies enormously across the sector. That variation is real, and it is precisely why the regulatory treatment is not uniform either.

## The distinction that matters most: deposit-taking or not

Regulation of non-bank lenders is easiest to hold in your head once you accept one split above all others. Most NBFCs are **non-deposit-taking**: they raise money from banks, from the bond market and from their own shareholders, and they lend it out. Their failure is a problem for their creditors and investors, but the public was never on the other side of the balance sheet.

A smaller set of NBFCs is permitted to accept **public deposits**. That permission is not a formality. It comes with conditions the RBI sets on the firm's capital, on the credit rating it must carry, on liquidity, and on the duration and the maximum size of the deposits it may accept. Those conditions are revised from time to time and are best read from the RBI's master direction for the relevant category; this article describes the mechanism and flags the figures as needing verification.

For a saver, the practical question is not "is this an NBFC?" but "is what I am buying a deposit, or something else?" A fixed deposit with a deposit-taking lender, a secured or unsecured debenture, a loan to a lending platform and a unit in a pooled vehicle can be marketed with near-identical language and leave the holder in completely different legal positions. The label on the scheme is not the position; the instrument is.

## Scale-based supervision: the rules rise with size

The RBI does not supervise a small equipment-leasing company and the largest non-bank lender the same way. A **scale-based framework** sorts NBFCs by asset size into layers, and raises the prudential requirement as a firm climbs: more capital, tighter concentration and exposure limits, stronger governance and disclosure obligations, and for the largest entities a regime that increasingly resembles bank supervision.

Two consequences follow for anyone reading about the sector. First, a sentence beginning "the RBI requires NBFCs to…" is usually a sentence about one layer, and applying it to the whole sector produces errors in both directions. Second, the boundaries between layers are notified and change; they should be read from the master direction in force. What is durable is the design principle rather than the numbers: the more other people's money a firm holds and the more systemically it is connected, the more it is asked to demonstrate that it can absorb losses before its creditors do.

## What supervision actually protects

It helps to separate two protections that are often spoken of as one.

The first is **prudential supervision**: capital requirements, asset classification and provisioning standards, exposure limits, liquidity rules for deposit-taking entities, fit-and-proper tests on management and periodic inspection. This protects depositors and the wider system by reducing the probability that a firm fails, and by giving the regulator powers to restrict a weakening firm's business before the public is exposed. Its logic is loss absorption — the firm's own capital takes the first hit so that creditors and depositors do not.

The second is **conduct regulation**: fair-practices codes on how loans are sold, how interest and charges are disclosed, and how recovery is carried out, plus a grievance-redress obligation. This protects a customer against being misled or mistreated. It is a different object from solvency.

Both matter, and neither is a guarantee. Prudential supervision reduces the chance of failure; it does not promise to prevent it, and it does not move the cost of failure from the firm's creditors to the state. Conduct rules protect you as a customer of a firm that is still solvent; they do not make a firm solvent.

## If a non-bank lender fails

A bank failure has a resolution framework built around depositors and the deposit-insurance backstop. A non-bank failure does not. When an NBFC becomes insolvent, the route for creditors, including public depositors and bondholders, runs through the insolvency framework: a petition to the National Company Law Tribunal, the appointment of a resolution professional, a moratorium that freezes recovery actions, and a resolution plan on which creditors vote, or else liquidation.

Recovery in Indian insolvency proceedings has varied widely by sector and vintage, and the realised recovery for unsecured creditors — which is what an unprotected depositor or an unsecured debenture holder is — has often been low. That is the honest baseline against which a higher headline rate on an NBFC product should be judged.

## Complaints: where a non-bank customer can actually go

Conduct disputes have a defined escalation route. Under the RBI's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026 — in force from 01-Jul-2026 and replacing the earlier scheme — a customer must first approach the regulated entity itself. The ombudsman route then covers certain NBFCs with asset size of ₹100 crore and above, or which accept deposits, alongside banks including co-operative banks with deposit size of ₹50 crore and above, non-bank prepaid-instrument issuers and credit information companies.

The scheme also states its exclusions plainly: housing finance companies, core investment companies and certain other entities sit outside it. Housing finance companies have their own dedicated article in this series because their regulator and their scheme are different.

Where the scheme applies, the mechanics are worth knowing: the escalation window opens after 30 days, or a higher period set by RBI, NPCI or card-network guidelines; the customer then has 90 days to escalate, measured from that deadline or from the last communication; filing is through the RBI's complaint portal or the centralised receipt and processing centre; there is no fee; there is no cap on the disputed amount; and compensation can run up to ₹30 lakh for consequential loss and up to ₹3 lakh for time, expenses and harassment, with a 30-day appeal window.

That is a genuine protection, and it is a protection against conduct, not against insolvency. An award in your favour against a firm with no money to pay remains a piece of paper.

## What a higher rate is buying

When a non-bank lender offers more than a bank on a comparable tenor, the gap is not a free lunch. It is the market price of the differences a saver is accepting: uninsured credit exposure to a single company, weaker secondary liquidity, thinner standardised disclosure than a listed bank, and a funding model more dependent on wholesale confidence. None of that makes the offer wrong, and none of it makes it right. It makes the rate a description of risk rather than a substitute for assessing it.

## Worked example: the arithmetic of the extra rate

Suppose, purely to make the arithmetic visible, that a bank deposit and a non-bank deposit differ in rate by 1.5 percentage points, and that a saver places ₹5,00,000 for one year:

$$\text{extra interest} = 5,00,000 \times 0.015 = 7,500$$

An **illustrative calculation, not a forecast**: the 1.5 percentage point spread is a hypothetical figure chosen to make the arithmetic legible, not an observed market rate, and no tax, compounding convention or penalty is modelled.

The shape of the trade is the point. The additional ₹7,500 is earned on the whole principal, whereas the protection being given up — the deposit-insurance cover available at an insured bank — is capped by a statutory limit per depositor per bank that is revised from time to time. That asymmetry, not the size of the headline rate, is what a careful reader weighs.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes an architecture. It deliberately does not state the deposit-insurance limit, the ceiling on public deposits an NBFC may accept, the minimum credit rating a deposit-taking NBFC must hold, the permitted deposit tenors, the asset-size boundaries between scale-based layers, or the capital ratio that applies to any category. Each of those is a number set by regulation and revised by notification, and each belongs in the notification. It also does not assess any particular lender, and it predicts no outcome for any complaint or insolvency proceeding.

A corporate bond scheme is defined by a quality floor. The framework requires the category to hold predominantly corporate debt of the highest rating tiers, which removes the lower-rated credit that gives other debt categories their higher yields. That sounds like a narrow, uniform product. In practice the opposite is true: because everyone in the category must sit above the same floor, the interesting differences are all inside the high-grade band — how concentrated the portfolio is, how much of it sits at the very top of the scale and how much one tier below, how long the maturity runs, and what the scheme charges. This article sets out the mandate, where the yield actually comes from, and how the disclosures let an investor separate two schemes that share a label.

## Explain simply

A corporate bond fund holds bonds issued by companies rather than by the government, and the rules require it to hold mainly the highest-rated ones, so the chance of a default in the portfolio is designed to be low. Because those bonds are safer than lower-rated ones, they pay a smaller margin over government paper. The fund's return therefore comes from that margin, from holding bonds a little longer than cash so the curve pays for time, and from the fact that some corporate bonds are less easy to trade. The rules keep the credit safe; they do not make the unit value fixed, and a credit event in even a top-rated issuer is still felt in the NAV.

## The minimum-rated mandate

The categorisation framework separates debt schemes by duration band and by credit quality. Corporate bond schemes sit at the high-quality end of the credit axis: the mandate requires the portfolio to be invested predominantly in corporate bonds of the highest rating tiers, with a minimum share in the top-rated category. This is what distinguishes the category from credit risk schemes, whose mandate is explicitly to hold lower-rated corporate credit and therefore to carry higher expected default risk.

Two features of the mandate are worth stating precisely, and one is worth refusing to state. The mandate is a **floor on quality**, enforced through portfolio rules. It is also a **minimum**, which means the scheme's remaining assets can be deployed in government securities, cash and other permitted instruments — which is why two schemes can hold visibly different portfolios while both complying. The exact percentages and the rating tiers they reference are set in the categorisation circular, which has been amended, and this article does not restate them; anyone relying on a figure should read the current circular.

## Why the high-grade band is not uniform

Requiring the same floor from every scheme in the category pushes differentiation upward, into the quality band the floor allows. That differentiation shows up in at least five places.

1. **Issuer concentration.** A scheme may hold a broad spread of issuers or may be concentrated in a handful. Concentration does not change the average rating, but it changes the consequence of any single issuer deteriorating.
2. **Sector concentration.** The corporate bond universe is not evenly spread across sectors, and schemes can lean toward the issuers that are most prolific in the market, often financial issuers, or spread more widely.
3. **The split within the band.** The floor allows a range of high ratings. A scheme that stays entirely at the very top tier is different from one that uses the tier immediately below, even though both comply.
4. **Duration posture.** The mandate fixes credit quality, not maturity. Two schemes in the category can run very different weighted-average maturities, and therefore very different interest-rate sensitivity.
5. **Cost.** The expense ratio is charged against the portfolio every year, and in a category whose spread is not large, it is a material part of the return the investor keeps.

Two schemes with the same label can therefore differ in every risk that matters, and the category name will not tell the investor which posture a scheme has taken.

## Where the yield actually comes from

The category's yield is a sum of compensations, and separating them is the only way to judge whether any of it is being overpaid for.

- **Credit spread.** Corporate issuers must pay more than the government because they can fail to pay. Even at the top of the rating scale, that margin exists, and it is payment for default and downgrade risk that has not yet materialised.
- **Term premium.** The mandate does not confine the scheme to short maturities, so holding longer paper earns the difference between near-term and later-dated yields when the curve slopes upward.
- **Liquidity premium.** Corporate bonds generally trade less readily than government securities, and part of the spread is payment for accepting that some holdings cannot be sold quickly at a fair price.
- **Roll-down.** As a bond ages toward a shorter maturity and the curve is upward-sloping, its price can gain over time. This is conditional on the shape of the curve and reverses when the curve changes.
- **Issuer selection.** Within a high-grade mandate, finding an issuer whose spread is wider than its risk genuinely warrants is the active contribution — and the one that is hardest to verify in advance.

Against all of it, the **expense ratio** is deducted annually. A corporate bond scheme yielding more than a peer is not necessarily better run; it may be holding more of the tier below the top, concentrating in fewer issuers, or running more duration.

## The four risks, separated

- **Credit risk** is the risk that an issuer fails to pay or is downgraded. The mandate constrains it to high-quality issuers, but a concentration in a few names makes the portfolio's outcome sensitive to any one of them, and a rating is an opinion rather than a guarantee.
- **Interest-rate risk** is the risk that yields rise and revalue the portfolio. Because the mandate does not cap maturity tightly, this risk varies a great deal between schemes in the category.
- **Liquidity risk** is the risk of selling into a thin market. Corporate bonds are less liquid than government paper, so a redemption-heavy period can force sales at unfavourable prices.
- **Mark-to-market behaviour** is how all of the above reaches the investor. Both rate changes and credit news are reflected in the daily NAV, so a high-grade portfolio is not an accrual product even when its holdings are safe.

## Worked example: an illustrative rate move and credit event

The following is an illustrative calculation from assumed inputs, not a forecast and not any scheme's observed result. Suppose an investor holds ₹10,00,000 in a corporate bond scheme whose portfolio is assumed to have a modified duration of 2.5 years.

**A rate move.** If yields rise by three-quarters of a percentage point, the approximate change in value is the modified duration multiplied by the yield change, sign reversed:

$$ \Delta P \approx -D \times \Delta y \times P $$

That is 2.5 multiplied by 0.75%, an assumed fall of about 1.875%, or roughly ₹18,750 on ₹10,00,000, before the interest the portfolio continues to earn. If the scheme is assumed to yield 7.5%, that is about ₹75,000 a year, roughly ₹6,250 a month, so the assumed price fall is recovered in about three months of income. The longer duration of this category, relative to the short-term bands, is what makes the recovery period longer.

**A credit event.** Assume separately that a single holding representing 10% of the portfolio defaults, with a recovery of 45% assumed, so the loss given default is 55%. The loss is the weight multiplied by the loss given default:

$$ \text{Loss} \approx w \times LGD $$

That is 0.10 multiplied by 0.55, or about 5.5% of NAV, roughly ₹55,000 on ₹10,00,000 — nearly nine months of the assumed income, and not recoverable by waiting. The illustration is deliberately uncomfortable: a portfolio of top-rated issuers can still lose more to one credit event than to a large rate move, which is why issuer and sector concentration deserve close reading even when the average rating is at the top of the scale. The assumed inputs are printed beside the results so that they can be challenged.

## How to compare two schemes in the category

The comparison reduces to three disclosures, and none of them is the trailing return.

- **The monthly portfolio.** Holdings and their ratings, the maturity profile, and the weights of the largest positions. This is where concentration and the split within the quality band become visible.
- **The total expense ratio.** The recurring cost charged against the portfolio, and the figure that decides how much of the spread the investor keeps.
- **The turnover ratio.** How actively the portfolio is traded. High turnover raises transaction costs borne by holders and shifts the timing of taxable gains.

Read together, the three answer the question the label cannot: which risks has this scheme taken, and what is it charging to take them?

## The four lanes, in this context

- **Contractual.** The coupons promised by specific corporate issuers are the contractual part of the return.
- **Model-estimated.** The duration arithmetic and any roll-down projection are estimates from stated assumptions.
- **Historical realised.** What corporate bond schemes delivered over a past window, computed from scheme-level NAV data.
- **Speculative.** The gain from falling yields or narrowing credit spreads and the loss from the reverse. In a high-grade portfolio this lane is quieter than in a credit risk scheme, but it is present.

## What this does not tell you

- **It does not state the mandate.** The minimum-rated percentage and the rating tiers are set in a circular that has been amended; verify the current text.
- **It does not rank rating tiers.** A top rating is an assessment of default likelihood, not a statement about price, liquidity or the investor's holding period.
- **It does not pick a scheme.** Two schemes in the category can carry materially different credit and duration exposure, and only the disclosures separate them.
- **It does not handle tax.** Gains from debt-oriented schemes are taxed under rules that depend on holding period and change over time.
- **It does not treat the category as protected.** There is no guarantee of capital or return, and the NAV is marked to market every day.

## Open questions

- Within a minimum-rated mandate, does the tier just below the very top rating earn enough extra spread to justify the step down once defaults and downgrades are counted across a full cycle?
- How much of the observed dispersion between corporate bond schemes is explained by issuer and sector concentration rather than by duration positioning or by cost?
- If the spread over sovereign paper compresses toward zero, does the category retain a distinct role, or does it become a government-securities fund carrying issuer risk for negligible reward?
- As credit ratings themselves come under scrutiny in stressed periods, how much weight should a portfolio rule place on a label rather than on the underlying analysis?

Owning a slice of the world from India is not difficult in principle. What makes it a discipline is that the permission to move money abroad arrives with a ceiling, a purpose test, a bank in the middle and a tax tail — and confusing any one of those with another is how people end up with a stalled transfer, an unexpected tax demand or a compliance notice. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme governs the first of those questions: what a resident individual may send out, and for what.

## Explain simply

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme lets a resident individual in India send up to US$2,50,000 abroad in a financial year for a list of permitted purposes. You do it through your bank, which is both the gatekeeper and the reporting channel. The ceiling is a permission, not a tax exemption: some remittances attract tax collected at source, and once you hold a foreign asset you will usually have to declare it. The scheme tells you what you may do. It does not tell you whether the investment is any good, and it does not make foreign income tax-free in India.

## What the scheme is

The scheme is a facility under India's foreign-exchange framework, aimed at resident individuals. It permits them — and minors, acting through a guardian — to remit funds abroad for specified current-account and capital-account transactions up to a ceiling set out in the RBI's master direction. Remittances are routed through authorised-dealer banks, which carry the diligence and reporting obligations, and certain purposes are either prohibited outright or permitted only subject to conditions. The scheme was introduced in February 2004 with a ceiling of US$25,000; the figure was raised to US$2,50,000 in May 2015 and that remains the operative limit.

Three words do most of the work. *Resident individual* defines who may use it — corporates, partnership firms, Hindu undivided families and trusts cannot. *Specified purposes* defines what the money may be used for. *Ceiling* defines how much. Everything else in this article elaborates those three.

## The annual ceiling

The headline number is US$2,50,000 per financial year. Two details about it are as important as the figure itself.

First, it is a **dollar** ceiling, not a rupee budget. As the rupee moves, the amount of domestic currency that US$2,50,000 represents moves with it, so a remitter planning near the limit should think in the foreign currency of the remittance rather than in rupees.

Second, it is an **annual** permission measured against the Indian financial year, April to March. It is not a lifetime budget and it does not carry forward. The figure has been revised before, and it can be revised again; the master direction, and the RBI's published LRS FAQs that restate it, are the operative sources, read at the time of the transaction. The same discipline applies to the sub-limits and conditions attached to particular purposes, which can change without the headline figure moving.

The ceiling is the least interesting thing about the scheme, though. Its *design* is where the practical consequences live, and five features do most of the work.

1. **It is per person.** The unit is the individual resident, including a minor through a guardian. A household of several eligible individuals therefore has several allowances in principle.
2. **It is per financial year.** The allowance refreshes with the Indian financial year. An unused allowance is not banked for the next.
3. **It aggregates across purposes.** Education, travel, gifts and investment all count against the same person's single ceiling. Spending on one does not create a separate allowance for another.
4. **It aggregates across banks.** The ceiling belongs to the person, not to the relationship with a particular institution. Moving to a second bank does not create a second allowance.
5. **It does not carry forward.** Planning is therefore annual, and a large requirement may need to be spaced across years.

The fourth feature deserves emphasis because it is the one people get wrong. The framework enforces the ceiling through the remitting bank's diligence and the remitter's own declaration, rather than through a single real-time ledger shared by every bank in the country. The obligation sits on the person who signs the declaration. A remitter designates one bank branch for the scheme, and PAN is mandatory, so the identity of the remitter is fixed even as the transaction is processed.

## Permitted purposes

The permitted list spans the ordinary reasons a resident might need foreign currency.

**Current-account transactions** include private visits (other than to Nepal and Bhutan), gifts and donations, employment abroad, emigration, maintenance of close relatives, business travel, medical treatment, and studies abroad.

**Capital-account transactions** include the acquisition of immovable property abroad, overseas direct and portfolio investment under the foreign-exchange overseas-investment rules, and rupee loans to non-resident relatives.

The categories are described in the master direction and are periodically adjusted. The distinction between current and capital matters because it maps onto different reporting and, in some cases, different tax treatment and conditions — a capital-account remitter must generally have held the account for a minimum period, and the framework does not permit credit facilities to fund a capital-account remittance. For the individual the practical test is simpler: is the purpose on the permitted list, and is the total within the ceiling?

## What is prohibited or restricted

A permission to remit is not a permission to remit for anything. The framework excludes a defined set of transactions — among them the current-account transactions prohibited in the rules, margin or margin calls to overseas exchanges, trading in foreign exchange abroad, and remittances to persons or jurisdictions subject to specified restrictions, including capital-account remittances to countries identified as non-cooperative by the global anti-money-laundering body. If a remittance exceeds US$2,50,000, prior approval from the RBI is required rather than a bank-level decision.

Any foreign exchange acquired for a permitted purpose that is not spent must be repatriated within the prescribed period unless it has been reinvested. The honest summary is this: a bank that asks a lot of questions about your purpose is doing the job the framework assigns it. Friction at the counter is often the mechanism working, not an obstacle to be engineered around.

## The bank in the middle

An authorised-dealer bank is not a passive pipe. It must satisfy itself that the remittance is permitted and within the ceiling, capture the declared purpose, apply customer-identification standards, and report the transaction as the rules require. In practice this is why the sequence matters: onboarding and purpose documentation come first, and the transfer follows. The remitter certifies the purpose on the prescribed form and declaration, and while the bank relies on that declaration, the ultimate responsibility for its accuracy rests with the remitter.

That also sets the limit of what a bank can promise you. A relationship manager who says a transfer is "fine" is expressing an operational judgement, not issuing a legal permission. If a remittance is later found to fall outside the scheme, the consequence attaches to the transaction and to the remitter, not to the reassurance. For sums that matter, get the position in writing through the bank's compliance channel before instructing the transfer.

## The tax and reporting tail

The tax tail begins with collection at source. For prescribed categories of remittance, the remitting bank must collect tax at a rate prescribed by the Income-tax Act and the Finance Acts, deposit it with the government and record it against the remitter's tax identity. The collected amount then appears in the remitter's tax-credit record and is available for set-off against final liability, subject to the conditions in the law.

Three things change over time here: the categories that attract collection, the rates, and any thresholds below which collection does not apply. The RBI framework requires banks to comply with tax law but does not itself set the figures, so they belong in the Income-tax Act and the current Finance Act rather than in a summary. What a reader should internalise is the *cash-flow* consequence: a remittance can cost more than the amount leaving your account, and the extra amount is a payment toward tax rather than a fee to the bank. The dedicated article on tax collected at source in this series carries the mechanics and the current figures.

Remitting money is the visible half of the compliance story. The quieter half follows ownership.

- **Foreign assets are reportable.** A resident holding foreign bank accounts, custodial holdings, foreign equity, immovable property or interests in trusts must declare them in the prescribed schedule of the income-tax return. Non-disclosure is treated severely under the black-money statute, with penalties and prosecution contemplated for undisclosed foreign income and assets. This is the single most consequential obligation in the whole area, and it applies whether or not the asset produced any income.
- **Foreign income is taxable for a resident.** Residence-based taxation means foreign dividends, interest and gains generally enter the Indian tax computation. Relief for tax already paid abroad is available through the foreign tax credit mechanism, governed by the income-tax rules and the applicable treaty, and generally limited to the Indian tax attributable to that income.
- **Treaties allocate taxing rights.** India's network of double-taxation agreements determines, for each class of income, which country taxes and how relief is given. Claiming treaty benefits usually requires evidence of residence and, where prescribed, further declarations.

None of this is optional, and none of it is handled by the remitting bank. The bank reports the remittance; you report the asset.

## Worked example: why the person is the unit of planning

An illustrative calculation, not observed data. Take the ceiling of US$2,50,000 and a household of four resident adults, each eligible in their own right. The household's theoretical aggregate capacity for the year would be:

$$\text{household capacity} = 4 \times 250,000 = 1,000,000$$

So on paper the household could move up to US$1,000,000 a year if four separate people each remitted their own money for their own permitted purposes. The complexity is not the arithmetic; it is the boundary between planning and circumvention. Each person's allowance belongs to that person and must be exercised for that person's own permitted purpose. Routing one person's money through another person's allowance in order to move more than the ceiling permits is not a planning technique; it is a breach of the scheme, and it exposes both parties. What is legitimate is that a family with several independent adults may each pursue their own permitted purposes within their own allowance, which is a qualification test rather than an optimisation.

## Routes that compete with a direct remittance

A resident seeking foreign exposure does not have to use the scheme directly. Collective investment vehicles that invest overseas provide exposure without an individual remittance, and they are subject to their own industry-level limits, which are periodically adjusted. Those limits are a separate constraint from the LRS ceiling, and they have occasionally left such funds closed to fresh inflows or trading at a premium. The practical implication is that the available route can change without any change in your own circumstances.

Two further structural forces shape the decision. The first is **home bias**: the tendency of investors to hold disproportionately domestic assets out of familiarity and tax comfort, at the cost of concentration in one country's policy, inflation and currency. The second is **currency itself**, which is a source of return and of risk that can be partly neutralised by hedging — at the cost of the interest-rate differential between the two currencies. Neither an unhedged nor a hedged position is correct in the abstract; the choice depends on whether currency exposure is a deliberate part of the thesis.

## What the scheme does not do

- It does not reduce tax. It is a permission, not a relief.
- It does not validate the investment. A permitted remittance can still fund a bad decision, including a fraud.
- It does not cover every purpose. Some are prohibited or conditioned.
- It does not remove the consequences of breach. Exceeding the ceiling or misdeclaring a purpose is dealt with under the foreign-exchange framework.
- It does not, by itself, discharge your reporting obligations. Declaring the asset is a separate duty.

## What this does not tell you

This article states the headline ceiling but not the sub-limits and conditions attached to particular purposes, and not the tax-collection rates, thresholds or penalty amounts, because those are variable and belong in the current rules. It does not compare remittance routes, assess currency outlook, or evaluate any particular overseas investment. It also does not cover the position of non-residents or of residents whose status changes during the year, which raises a distinct set of questions better answered by the specific rules that apply to that status.

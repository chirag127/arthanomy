Most people who lose money to an unregistered adviser were not careless. They were shown a registration number, a certificate, an office with a receptionist and a logo that looked like a regulator's. Every one of those signals is cheap to produce, and one of them — the number — is usually real. It simply belongs to a different entity, or covers a different activity, than the one being sold.

Registration in Indian securities markets is permission for a defined activity. SEBI maintains public registers of the intermediaries it has authorised, and those registers exist so that an investor can confirm, before dealing, that the entity in front of them holds the registration the activity requires. This article sets out what the categories mean, what a registration does not buy you, and a verification routine short enough to run in the ten minutes before a payment.

## Explain simply

Not everyone who offers to manage or advise on your money is allowed to. In India, a broker, an investment adviser, a research analyst, a portfolio manager, an alternative investment fund and a depository participant must each be registered with SEBI, and each registration permits only that specific activity. The regulator publishes a register that shows who holds what. Checking it is a search: type in the name, look at the category, confirm the entry is current, and see whether the name matches the entity asking you for money. Fraudsters rarely fake a whole register. They quote a real registration number that belongs to somebody else, or claim a category they do not hold. A five-minute check catches almost all of it.

## Registration is permission for a defined activity

The mental model that prevents most mistakes is this: registration is a licence with a scope, not a badge with a value.

A licence states what its holder may do. It implies that a supervisor knows the holder exists, can inspect its records, and can act if its conduct breaches the rules under which it was licensed. It implies disclosure obligations towards clients. None of that is a statement about skill or honesty, and none of it transfers to a different activity, a different legal entity, or a different person.

Two consequences follow. First, the question is never "is this firm registered" but "is this firm registered **for this**". Second, the answer is time-stamped. Registrations can lapse on non-renewal, be suspended while proceedings are pending, or be surrendered. A verification performed when a relationship began says nothing about the relationship today.

## The categories that require registration

The register covers several distinct roles, and the difference between them is the substance of most impersonation attempts:

- **Broker / trading member.** Executes and settles trades in securities. A broker's licensing is about market access and execution, and using a broker's platform to place your own orders is not the same as receiving investment advice.
- **Investment adviser.** Advises clients about their investments in securities for a consideration. This is the registration that matters when a person or firm is telling you what to buy, hold or exit.
- **Research analyst.** Produces and distributes research, analysis and recommendations about securities. Research is general and published; it is not the same as advice tailored to your circumstances, and the registration is a separate one.
- **Portfolio manager.** Manages a portfolio on a client's behalf under a mandate, typically with a minimum commitment and a formal agreement that sets out discretion, custody and reporting.
- **Alternative investment fund.** A pooled vehicle for a defined class of investors, run by a manager within a regulated structure.
- **Depository participant.** Services demat accounts and the holding infrastructure through which securities are held.

Each of these is verified in the same place, and each answers a different question. The most common mismatch is a person holding research-analyst registration — or no registration at all — while selling a service that is functionally discretionary portfolio management.

## Adviser, analyst, manager, broker: who may do what

Put the four roles side by side and the confusions become obvious.

| Role | What it is authorised to do | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Broker | Execute and settle your trades; provide market access | Give you a personalised plan or manage discretion |
| Research analyst | Publish research and recommendations | Manage your money or hand-tailor advice to your position |
| Investment adviser | Advise you on securities for a fee, within a disclosure regime | Take custody of your money or trade on your behalf without a separate mandate |
| Portfolio manager | Manage a portfolio under a written discretionary mandate | Sell you a research subscription or a pooled scheme |

Two habits follow. Match the category in the register to the service being described, in plain words, and be suspicious when someone claims to do all four under one registration. Where the activity involves handing money over for a manager to deploy, the mandate itself — who holds the assets, who can move them, how performance is reported — matters as much as the registration.

## What a genuine registration does not give you

A register entry is a starting condition, not an assurance. It does not certify that the advice is good, that fees are fair, or that the firm will behave. It does not make a product suitable for you. It does not cover activities outside its scope: a SEBI registration says nothing about whether an entity may accept deposits, lend money, or run a scheme of a kind that requires a different authorisation entirely.

The distinction that matters most is between *authorisation* and *custody*. A registered adviser is authorised to advise. Money moving directly and repeatedly into an individual's personal account, or into an account of a firm with no registered relationship to the mandate, is not a feature of a registered advisory relationship — it is a feature of a collection.

## The ten-minute check

The routine is deliberately mechanical. Run it in order.

1. **Write down the exact legal name** of the entity that will receive your money or give you the advice. Not the brand, not the app name, not the logo — the name that will appear on the agreement and on the receiving bank account.
2. **Search the register of intermediaries.** The register is searchable by name and by registration number, and it shows the category of registration held.
3. **Match the category to the activity.** If the offer is discretionary management, the entry should be a portfolio manager. If the offer is personalised advice, it should be an investment adviser. A mismatch is the finding.
4. **Confirm the entry is current.** Names are similar to well-known ones by design; the register match is exact, and the status shown is what counts.
5. **Look for the entity and its principals in SEBI's investor alerts** on unregistered advisers, tip providers and guaranteed-return pitches. These lists exist for precisely this moment.
6. **Search SEBI's published orders** for the entity, its directors and its promoters. Orders state findings of fact, and a public pattern of past findings is information you would otherwise never get.
7. **Contact the entity independently.** Use contact details you found yourself — the register, the firm's own website reached through a search you initiated — never the number in a message. Ask who owns the receiving account, in writing.

Steps one through five take a few minutes. Step seven is the one most people skip, and it is the one that defeats impersonation.

## Worked example: checking a pitch before paying anything

An illustrative walkthrough, not a description of any real firm or person. A message introduces a "SEBI-registered research analyst" who will manage a trading account and target a fixed monthly return. The message includes a screenshot showing a registration number.

- **Step 1** produces nothing usable: the brand in the message is a marketing name, and the bank details in the follow-up message name an individual rather than an entity.
- **Step 2** returns a register entry for the quoted number — but it belongs to a different legal entity in a different city, and the category shown is research analyst.
- **Step 3** is therefore a mismatch twice over. A research-analyst registration does not authorise managing a trading account, and the entity that holds it is not the entity asking for money.
- **Step 5** finds the entity that actually holds the number named in an investor alert about impersonation.
- **Step 7** reverses the names in the message against the firm's independently obtained contact details. The firm has never heard of the sender.

Every step here is a public lookup. None of it depends on the investor's judgement about markets, and the conclusion is not a matter of opinion: the activity offered is not authorised by the registration quoted, and the money is being directed to a personal account. The correct decision is to stop, not to negotiate.

## The two failure modes: no registration, and borrowed registration

Unregistered advisory and "tip" services are one failure mode. There is no register entry, no disclosure obligation and no supervisor. SEBI publishes alerts about such entities and takes action against them, and its orders record findings of fact about what they were doing.

Borrowed registration is the other mode, and it is harder to spot because a genuine number appears in the response. It takes three common shapes: quoting a real number that belongs to another entity; holding a registration in one category while performing the activity of another; and using a group company's registration while the contracting entity is unregistered. In each case the register is still the instrument that resolves the question, because it shows the *name* attached to the number and the *category* attached to the name.

A third, quieter mode is the registered entity fronted by an unregistered individual. The registration attaches to an entity, and the person in front of you may be an employee, an agent or a stranger using the entity's name. Where a relationship is being established, the agreement should be with the registered entity itself.

## Reading the register like an auditor

Five habits raise the quality of a check.

- **Match names exactly.** Similar-looking names are the most common camouflage. Compare the register entry character by character with the name that will appear on your agreement.
- **Treat the category as a claim to be tested.** Ask what the registration permits, in the regulator's own published language, and compare it with the service described in the pitch.
- **Look at the status, not just the existence.** An entry that exists but is not current is not authorisation.
- **Check the entity's history as well as its present.** Alerts and orders are the public record of past conduct.
- **Verify the money path.** Whose name is on the receiving account, and does that name match the registered entity? This single question does more work than any other.

## Where to complain

If the entity is registered, the complaint goes first to the entity's own grievance channel and then to SEBI SCORES, the regulator's grievance-redressal portal for investors, which is designed for escalations of exactly this kind. If the entity is not registered, the appropriate route is the reporting route for unauthorised entities that SEBI publishes alongside its investor alerts.

The registration question also fixes the regulator. A SEBI registration covers the securities activity only; it is not authority to accept deposits or to lend. So where the entity being dealt with is a bank or an NBFC rather than a market intermediary, the complaint route is the RBI's, not SEBI's: the matter goes first to the entity and then through the RBI's centralised complaint portal at cms.rbi.org.in under the Reserve Bank – Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026, in force from 1 July 2026 and replacing the 2021 scheme, with matters received before that date, and appeals and awards under the 2021 scheme, continuing under it, and with the complaint to the entity made before the limitation period under the Limitation Act, 1963 expired.

Because unauthorised advisory is also a criminal matter, a police complaint is available in parallel; cheating and criminal breach of trust fall under the criminal law that has applied since July 2024, and state Economic Offences Wings handle these cases. Where the harm was a misleading representation or a mis-sold service, a consumer commission route exists as well. In every case, the documentary trail — agreements, screenshots of the pitch, payment records showing the receiving account, and the identity of the contact — is what makes the complaint actionable.

## What this does not tell you

A registration check establishes one thing: that the entity in front of you is authorised to perform the activity it is describing, on the day you check. It says nothing about whether the advice is any good, whether the fees are reasonable, whether the strategy suits you, or whether the firm will treat you fairly. A registered intermediary can still sell you an unsuitable product, charge more than the service is worth, or lose your money through incompetence rather than dishonesty. Registration is not a substitute for reading the mandate, understanding the product, and knowing what you are paying.

It also does not travel. A SEBI registration is not authority to accept deposits, to lend, to run a pooled scheme, or to sell insurance; those are different permissions under different frameworks, and a register entry in one of them tells you nothing about the others. And it does not resolve the boundary case that matters most in practice — a person who is a registered adviser for some activities and an unregistered salesperson for others. Where the same individual wears both hats, the question to ask is which activity you are being invited into at that moment.

Finally, none of this is a substitute for the arithmetic. A verified adviser recommending a product with a promising number is still recommending a product with a number, and the rest of this library exists to help you read it.

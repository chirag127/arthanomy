A multi-level marketing plan and a pyramid scheme can look identical from the inside: a product, a starter kit, an upline, a downline, incentives, ranks, a conference call, and a story about financial freedom. The difference is not the enthusiasm or the branding. It is a single structural fact — where the money that reaches participants actually comes from — and that fact can be tested before any money changes hands.

## Explain simply

In a legitimate direct-selling business, a participant earns a commission when a real product is sold to a real customer at a real price, and most of the revenue to the company comes from those sales. In a pyramid or money-circulation structure, a participant earns mainly by enrolling new participants, so the money paid out comes from the entry fees of the people below them rather than from customers. Because each new participant must find more participants, the structure needs to keep expanding, and a chain that must keep expanding cannot do so forever. The product, if there is one, is often a formality whose price carries the entry fee.

## Two separate things are being sold

Every such offer contains two transactions, and they should be evaluated separately.

- **The product transaction.** Something is sold — a wellness supplement, a training course, an insurance-like product, a consumer durable, a "business kit". Ask for its price, its market comparables, its refund rights and whether it can be bought without joining anything.
- **The position transaction.** A place in a hierarchical payout structure is sold, which entitles the participant to a share of the money that flows in from people they recruit and from the recruits of those recruits.

A lawful business can have both. What makes a structure unlawful is the ratio between them: when the position transaction is the point and the product transaction is the packaging, participants are being paid to recruit rather than paid to sell.

## Where the money comes from: the structural test

Write the payout to participants as the sum of two sources:

$$ \text{payout to participants} = \text{share of retail sales} + \text{share of new entry fees} $$

The test is which term dominates. If, at any realistic level of retail activity, the first term cannot fund the second, then the difference is made up from the enrolments of new participants — which is the money-multiplier mechanism, described in the glossary as a fraud structure that promises to multiply money and pays entrants from the entry fees of later participants. The arithmetic is that of a chain letter. The promised mechanism is fiction.

This is also why the presence of a genuine product does not settle the question. A supplement that exists, ships and can be consumed is still packaging if it is bought almost exclusively by people who joined the scheme to qualify for commissions. The question is not whether the product is real; it is who buys it and why.

## The recruitment arithmetic

A structure that pays on multiple levels is a tree. Each participant must recruit a number of people, each of whom recruits a number, and so on. The defining feature of a tree is that each level must be larger than the whole of the tree above it, because every participant's qualification depends on the level beneath.

If every participant must recruit three people to qualify, the size of successive levels is 3, 9, 27, 81, 243, 729, 2,187 and 6,561. The cumulative number of participants required to reach the eighth level is 9,840. Nothing about this arithmetic depends on the product, the market or the country — it is a property of the tree, and it is the reason these structures always have a last cohort. The people in the final level have nobody beneath them, and their entry fees are what paid the commissions above.

| Level | Participants at that level | Cumulative participants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 27 | 39 |
| 5 | 243 | 363 |
| 8 | 6,561 | 9,840 |

Compare this with the two-person chain in a classic money-multiplier scheme, where the required depth is reached even faster. Either way, the number of participants needed grows geometrically while the pool of willing recruits does not.

## Worked example: the commission split decides the case

This is an illustrative construction, not data from any scheme. A company sells a "business starter pack" for ₹10,000. Of that amount, ₹5,000 is paid out as recruitment commissions across the upline — a hypothetical split of ₹3,000 to the recruiting participant, ₹1,000 to their sponsor, and ₹1,000 distributed across three further levels — and ₹5,000 remains with the company to cover the product cost, administration and profit.

Now consider the flow of money when one hundred people buy packs:

- Money in from the hundred buyers: ₹10,00,000.
- Paid out to existing participants as recruitment commissions: ₹5,00,000.
- Retained by the company: ₹5,00,000.

Half of every rupee that enters the structure leaves it immediately as a payment to an existing participant, funding what looks like income but is in substance a redistribution of the latest cohort's money. For the participants as a group to be net positive, the money must keep arriving from outside the group — that is, from people recruited later. This is the Ponzi cash-flow logic wearing different clothes, and it is why the structure is vulnerable the moment recruitment slows.

If the same ₹10,000 pack were sold instead to ordinary consumers who never joined the scheme, the commissions would be funded by external revenue and the test would come out differently. That is the whole distinction, and it is measurable: what proportion of the company's revenue comes from sales outside the network, and what proportion from enrolments inside it?

## Direct selling versus a money-circulation scheme

Indian law does not prohibit direct selling as such; it prohibits schemes whose purpose or effect is to make money principally by enrolling others. The practical checks that separate the two are concrete:

- **Source of participant income.** Commissions on sales to consumers outside the network, or commissions on the enrolment of new distributors.
- **Guarantees.** A lawful plan does not promise a fixed income, a fixed monthly payout or a return on joining.
- **Product reality.** Can the product be bought, at a comparable price, by someone who has no intention of joining? Is there a genuine market for it among people who never heard of the plan?
- **Start-up load.** Is a large upfront purchase of inventory required, and is it returnable? A requirement to buy stock that cannot be sold converts the participant into the customer.
- **Disclosure.** Are income figures typical, substantiated and labelled as examples rather than as expectations?
- **Exit.** Can a participant leave, return unsold stock and stop paying without penalty?

The inventory-loading variant deserves its own warning. In it, the "sale" that generates the commission is made to the new participant, not to a consumer, and the participant is left holding goods with no retail demand. The commission has been paid out of the participant's own money. Nothing has been sold outside the pyramid at all.

## The chit-fund confusion

The word "chit" is widely misused. A chit fund properly registered under the chit-funds statute is a regulated savings-and-borrowing arrangement in which members contribute periodically, the pooled amount is auctioned or allotted to a member each period at a discount, and distribution rotates until every member has received a draw. It is not a pyramid, and the pooled money is not a promise of profit but a rotating pool among the members themselves. The critical questions for any arrangement calling itself a chit are registration, conduct of the auctions and the foreman's statutory security. Unregistered "chit-like" schemes offering deposit-style returns are a different thing entirely and fall outside the regulated framework.

## What the law provides

Three overlapping bodies of rules bear on these structures.

Collective investment schemes that pool money from the public must be registered with SEBI, and enforcement actions have repeatedly addressed arrangements marketed as business opportunities that were, in substance, unregistered pooled schemes. Where the pitch includes a promised return on money contributed, the securities-market framework and the deposit-taking prohibitions both apply.

On the criminal side, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 replaced the Indian Penal Code with effect from 1 July 2024 and carries the provisions on cheating, criminal breach of trust, forgery and organised crime that a recruitment-driven fraud engages. Money-circulation and unregulated deposit-taking arrangements are also addressed by their own prohibition statutes, and cases under them are typically registered with the state police economic offences units rather than with a market regulator.

For a person who was misled into a purchase by a false representation, consumer-protection law provides a separate civil route, with district, state and national commissions hearing complaints. That route addresses the mis-selling; it does not by itself unpick the structure or recover money from other participants.

## Test any opportunity before joining

1. **Write down where the payout comes from.** If the sentence involves "people I recruit" more than "customers who buy", stop.
2. **Ask for the proportion of company revenue from sales outside the network.** If the figure cannot be produced, assume it is not favourable.
3. **Ask for the median earnings of participants**, not the top performer's, and ask how many participants earn nothing.
4. **Check registration.** Verify any entity that pools money or offers a return against the relevant regulator's register.
5. **Check the refund and exit terms in writing**, including the treatment of unsold inventory.
6. **Refuse to buy inventory you cannot return** and refuse to pay for a "position" or "rank" as a separate purchase.
7. **Notice the time pressure.** Urgency, events and limited "slots" are designed to prevent exactly the reading you are doing now.

## Reporting routes

- **SEBI channels** for an unregistered pooled scheme, a promised investment return, or a misleading claim about earnings from an investment-like product. SEBI publishes alerts on unauthorised entities and guaranteed-return schemes, and these are the fastest way to check whether an entity has already been flagged.
- **The police, through the state economic offences unit**, where cheating, criminal breach of trust or a money-circulation arrangement is involved; these units handle such cases as a matter of routine.
- **A consumer forum** where the harm is a mis-sold product or a false representation about what was being purchased.
- **Preserve everything first.** Receipts, agreements, rank and commission statements, the identity of the receiving bank accounts, and the messages that contained the promises. A complaint with documents proceeds; a complaint without them stalls.

## What this does not tell you

Not every commission-driven seller is a fraud, and not every participant in a flawed scheme is a fraudster — most are people who were themselves sold a story and who recruit others because they believe it. This article describes structures, not individuals, and it makes no finding about any named company. Nor can a structure test substitute for legal advice in a specific dispute. What it can do is answer the question that a pitch is designed to avoid: if nobody new ever joined again, would the people already in the structure be paid? If the answer is no, the structure is the product, and the arithmetic of the tree has already written the ending.

## Open questions

- Where precisely is the line between an aggressive but lawful commission-heavy direct-selling plan and a prohibited money-circulation scheme, and how consistently do enforcement actions draw it?
- What proportion of participants in such structures ever recover any part of their money, and how does that compare with the recovery rate in securities-market enforcement?
- Would a mandatory disclosure of the share of revenue coming from outside the distribution network settle most cases without litigation?

A pyramid structure is not detected by the intensity of its claims but by the direction of its money. Follow the rupee: if it travels from the newest participant to the oldest, the plan is not a business with a recruitment channel — it is recruitment with a business attached.

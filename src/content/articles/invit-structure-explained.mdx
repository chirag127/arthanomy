A toll road, a power transmission line, a gas pipeline and a solar plant have something in common beyond being large and expensive. They are businesses whose output is essentially metered: a vehicle passes, a unit of power is transmitted, a volume of gas flows. That quality — measurable, contracted, cash-generating, and far too expensive for an individual to own alone — is what an Infrastructure Investment Trust is built to package.

An InvIT is the infrastructure counterpart of a REIT, and it uses the same legal skeleton. What differs is what goes inside it and how the cash behaves once it does. This article explains the architecture, then turns to the part that makes InvITs genuinely distinct: distributions drawn from assets whose lives are finite and whose revenues are only as firm as the contracts and the counterparties behind them.

## Explain simply

Imagine a trust that owns a road, or a set of power lines, or a pipeline. The road collects tolls; the lines collect regulated charges; the pipeline collects tariffs. The trust pays the people who keep the asset running, pays the interest on any money it has borrowed, sets aside what is needed to maintain the asset, and then hands at least ninety per cent of what is left to the people who hold its units. Those units are listed, so they can be bought and sold like shares. What a holder receives is a share of the cash the infrastructure produces, and unlike a company dividend the payment is largely mandated by regulation. What a holder does not receive is a promise of a fixed amount, because traffic, tariffs and costs all move.

## The same trust skeleton as a REIT

The structural logic is deliberately familiar. Rather than a company with shareholders, an InvIT is a trust in which a trustee holds the assets for the benefit of unit holders, whose units represent a beneficial interest in the trust. A sponsor establishes the trust and seeds it with infrastructure assets and capital. A manager runs it. The regulations prescribe eligible asset categories, minimum asset size and unit-holder thresholds, distribution requirements and leverage limits. If you have read the REIT articles in this series, the shape will look identical, and that is intentional: the regulator has built two parallel structures for two classes of yield-producing real assets.

The parties carry the same duties as they do in a REIT.

| Party | Role | Accountable for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sponsor | Establishes the trust, contributes assets and capital, and typically retains a stake | The suitability of the assets placed in the trust and the alignment created by its continuing holding |
| Manager | Operations, asset and contract management, financing, acquisitions and disclosures | Asset performance, capital allocation and the accuracy of disclosure |
| Trustee | Holds the assets for unit holders and oversees the manager | Independent scrutiny of valuations, related-party transactions and distributions |
| Unit holders | Provide capital and hold the beneficial interest | Nothing operational; the remedies are the market price and whatever vote the framework provides |

## What an InvIT is allowed to hold

The eligible universe is infrastructure rather than property: assets such as toll roads, power transmission lines, gas pipelines and renewable energy projects sit at the centre of it. What these assets share is that they produce a cash flow tied to a defined right — a concession, a tariff order, a long-term contract — rather than to the general demand conditions that drive office rents.

That defining feature splits the universe in a way that matters more than the asset labels. Some assets earn on usage: a toll road earns more when more vehicles pass. Others earn on availability or capacity: a transmission asset may be paid for keeping the line available and compliant, with the revenue less sensitive to how much power actually flows. Both are infrastructure, but one exposes you to demand and the other leans on the credit and conduct of the paying authority. Reading an InvIT offer document means identifying, for each asset, which of the two you are looking at.

## Where the cash flows come from

The revenue line of an InvIT is contractual or concession-based, and it usually has a much longer nominal horizon than a commercial lease. A lease of a building might run for five or nine years; a concession might run for decades. Length is an advantage for visibility, but it imports a different set of risks. Traffic volumes can undershoot projections. Tariffs can be reset, capped or delayed by the authority that sets them. Operating and maintenance costs can rise faster than the contracted revenue. And the whole stream depends on the counterparty — often a government body or a public utility — actually paying on time and in full.

When these risks are described together as traffic, tariff and counterparty risk, that phrase is doing real work. Traffic risk is a volume problem, tariff risk is a price and regulatory problem, and counterparty risk is a credit problem. An InvIT can be strong on one and weak on another, and the distribution figure alone will not tell you which.

## Why an InvIT distribution is not a dividend

Equity dividends are discretionary and paid out of profit at the direction of a board. An InvIT distribution is different in three ways that compound. It is mandatory to the extent of the regulated share of net distributable cash flow. It is measured against cash rather than against accounting profit, so depreciation and fair-value movements do not by themselves cause a shortfall or a windfall. And it frequently carries a return-of-capital element, because the assets have finite lives.

That last point deserves emphasis because it changes how the payment should be read. A concession that expires in fifteen years is not a perpetual claim: the cash it throws off over those fifteen years has to do two jobs, paying income and returning the capital that was invested. A holder who treats the entire distribution as income is, in effect, spending part of the investment. The composition of an InvIT payout, not just its size, is therefore the thing to watch, and the split between interest, dividend and return of capital is what the unit holder actually receives.

## Distribution mechanics and the ninety per cent rule

As with a REIT, the framework requires distribution of at least 90 per cent of net distributable cash flows. NDCF is the cash left after operating expenses, interest and debt service, maintenance capital expenditure and mandatory reserves. Because the trust retains so little, growth in an InvIT usually comes from acquiring further assets or from the contractual escalation built into existing contracts, rather than from reinvested earnings. Leverage limits constrain how much of that growth can be borrowed.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the distribution is structural in form and variable in amount: you can expect a payment, but its size depends on the assets. Second, distributions tend to be more frequent than company dividends, which is convenient for income planning but also means more frequent opportunities for the composition to shift from one period to the next. Reading the trend in distribution per unit, alongside the asset base, is more informative than reading a single period.

## REIT and InvIT side by side

| Dimension | REIT | InvIT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it holds | Income-producing real estate, commonly offices and retail assets | Operating infrastructure assets such as roads, transmission lines, pipelines and renewable projects |
| Revenue driver | Occupancy and rents under leases | Traffic, tariffs and contracted or concession-based charges |
| Asset life | Long-lived; the land typically does not expire | Often finite, where a concession or contract has a defined term |
| Primary risks | Tenant concentration, re-letting risk, property cycle | Traffic and volume risk, tariff and regulatory risk, counterparty credit |
| Distribution base | At least 90 per cent of net distributable cash flows | At least 90 per cent of net distributable cash flows |
| Return of capital | Can arise | Arises structurally where assets have finite lives |
| Trust structure | Sponsor, manager, trustee, listed units | Sponsor, manager, trustee, listed units |

The two structures converge on governance and diverge on what is being governed. That is why the same questions — who is the manager, what does the sponsor retain, how independent is the trustee, how much debt is used — apply to both, while the asset-level questions differ almost completely.

## Worked example: tracing a concession-based distribution

The following is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast and not a description of any listed trust. Every figure is chosen for arithmetic clarity, and none is drawn from a market.

Suppose an InvIT holds a single asset under a concession with fifteen years remaining, and that in the first year it collects tolls and charges of ₹500 crore.

| Line | Illustrative first-year figure |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue from tolls and charges | ₹500 crore |
| Operating and maintenance cost | −₹120 crore |
| Net operating income | ₹380 crore |
| Interest on debt | −₹80 crore |
| Maintenance capital expenditure | −₹25 crore |
| Mandatory reserves | −₹15 crore |
| Net distributable cash flow | ₹260 crore |

The trust must distribute at least 90 per cent of ₹260 crore, which is ₹234 crore. If 100 crore units are outstanding, that is a distribution of ₹2.34 per unit for the year. Because the concession expires in fifteen years, a meaningful part of that ₹2.34 is the gradual return of the capital invested in the asset rather than income earned on it — the balance is the difference between a finite claim and a perpetual one.

Now apply a traffic shock. Suppose volumes fall by 10 per cent, so revenue drops to ₹450 crore, while the cost base and the debt service do not fall in proportion.

| Line | Illustrative stressed figure |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue from tolls and charges | ₹450 crore |
| Operating and maintenance cost | −₹120 crore |
| Net operating income | ₹330 crore |
| Interest on debt | −₹80 crore |
| Maintenance capital expenditure | −₹25 crore |
| Mandatory reserves | −₹15 crore |
| Net distributable cash flow | ₹210 crore |

The mandatory distribution becomes 90 per cent of ₹210 crore, or ₹189 crore, and with the unit count unchanged the distribution per unit falls to about ₹1.89 — roughly a nineteen per cent decline in the payout for a ten per cent decline in revenue. This amplification is the operating leverage of an infrastructure asset financed partly with debt: revenue moves, but the obligations below it do not. It is worth noting that the same arithmetic runs upward when traffic exceeds the projection, which is precisely why the projection is a forecast and not a fact.

## What this does not tell you

This article explains how an InvIT is organised and how cash reaches a holder; it does not value any trust or judge its manager. It does not state the terms of any concession, the credit of any counterparty or the leverage of any trust, because those are specific facts that belong in the documents rather than in a structural account. It does not translate a distribution into a yield, which would require a price it does not supply, and it does not resolve the tax treatment of the three components of a payout, which depends on the rules in force and on the holder circumstances.

It also cannot tell you how to weigh a finite asset life against a higher current cash flow. That trade — more cash now, less asset remaining later — is the central valuation question in InvITs and has no structural answer. It is a question about price, remaining life and the reinvestment assumption, and it belongs to the valuation article rather than to this one.

## Open questions

- For availability-based assets, how much of the apparent steadiness of the cash flow is simply the credit of a single public counterparty, and how would a unit holder observe that credit deteriorating?
- When a distribution is composed largely of return of capital, what reinvestment assumption is a holder implicitly making about the remaining concession life?
- As InvIT portfolios mix usage-based and availability-based assets, does diversification between them genuinely reduce risk, or does it mainly diversify the label while concentrating the dependency on one regulator?
- Do InvIT units trade with enough frequency for the listed price to be informative, or does the price reflect the difficulty of valuing long-dated contracts more than it reflects their value?

A portfolio without named purposes is a collection, not a plan. The money in it earns a return, but no one can say whether that return is good, because there is no stated job for the money to do and no date by which it has to be done. The discipline this article describes is simple and slightly uncomfortable: every holding should be able to answer two questions — what is this for, and when is it needed.

This is the second entry in the Operating a Portfolio series. The first wrote the policy that governs the household's decisions; this one supplies the objectives that the policy is built from, by translating aspirations into dated, quantified goals and the horizon buckets they imply.

## Explain simply

A goal is not a wish; it is an amount of money, needed by a date, for a stated purpose. Because prices rise, the amount actually needed will be larger than the amount that would be paid today, so the target has to be adjusted for inflation. The date matters because money needed soon cannot be placed where it might fall just before it is needed. Once every holding is tied to a goal and a date, a portfolio stops being a pile of products and becomes a set of plans.

## A goal is a dated, quantified claim on money

The word "goal" is used loosely. In portfolio operations it means something specific. A goal has five parts, and if any one is missing it is still an aspiration:

- **Purpose.** What the money is actually for — a house deposit, a child's education, a replacement vehicle, retirement income, or a stated giving commitment.
- **Amount.** A figure, in today's money, with a note on how confident that figure is.
- **Date.** The point at which the money is needed, given as a month and year where possible.
- **Priority.** Whether the goal is mandatory, important or discretionary, in the sense that the household would notice if it failed.
- **Funding source.** Which accounts and instruments are designated to fund it.

The purpose matters because it determines the horizon. The date matters because the horizon determines which assets are acceptable. The priority matters because when two goals want the same rupee, the ranking is the only thing that decides fairly. The funding source matters because a goal with no named source is a wish.

## From aspiration to arithmetic

Turning an aspiration into a goal is a short piece of estimation, and the estimator should be honest about precision. A goal of "enough for a good education" is impossible to fund; the same goal expressed as "a four-year course at a stated standard of institution, starting in September 2034, with an estimated cost in today's money and a stated confidence level" can be funded, reviewed and adjusted.

Two estimates are needed. The first is the amount in today's money — what the goal would cost if it were payable now. The second is the amount at the target date, which is larger, because prices rise. The first figure is the one the household recognises; the second is the one that has to be funded. The gap between them is frequently large enough to change the saving required by a third, which is why the two must be kept side by side rather than replaced by intuition.

## Inflation-adjusting the target

The cost of a future expense is the present cost compounded at the rate at which that expense is expected to rise. The arithmetic is:

$$ F = P \times (1 + i)^n $$

where $P$ is the amount in today's money, $i$ the assumed rate of price increase, $n$ the number of years to the target, and $F$ the amount that has to be funded. The result is a planning input, not a prediction — the assumed rate is a judgement and the answer is only as good as it. But it is far more useful than ignoring the rise altogether, because it converts a goal the household believes it is halfway to funding into a goal it may not have started on.

The rate to use should reflect the goal rather than the general price level. A goal whose cost is driven by education, healthcare, or a property purchase in a specific market may rise at a very different rate from the general consumer price index, and the register should note which rate was used and why.

## Horizons and what they imply

A goal's horizon is the distance to its date, and it does the most important work in the article: it decides which assets are acceptable. Money needed in a short horizon has little time to recover from a fall, so it cannot carry the volatility that money with a long horizon can absorb. Money needed in a long horizon has the opposite problem: held only in low-volatility instruments, it may fail to keep pace with the rising cost of the goal.

A working convention treats horizons in three bands:

- **Short — up to roughly three years.** The requirement is availability and stability. The relevant question is whether the money will be intact on the date it is needed, not what it might earn.
- **Medium — roughly three to seven years.** A mixed treatment is defensible, with the balance shifting as the date approaches. As the goal nears, the tolerance for volatility falls.
- **Long — roughly seven years and beyond.** There is enough time for volatility to average out and for growth assets to do the work the goal requires, provided the household can tolerate the interim path.

The boundaries are not laws. They are the household's declared lines, and their value is consistency: the same line is applied every time, so a goal cannot drift from one band to another simply because the household has become more optimistic. This is where the goal register meets the policy statement, which should state which horizon bands the household uses.

## Bucketing: assigning every holding to a purpose

The most useful output of this exercise is a mapping in the opposite direction: from each holding to the goal it serves. Bucketing means grouping assets by purpose and horizon rather than by product type, and its effect is to make the portfolio auditable.

The audit is a single question applied to every line on the balance sheet: which goal does this fund, and is the horizon of that goal compatible with the volatility of this holding? A long-horizon equity fund sitting in a short-horizon bucket is a mismatch; so is a three-year goal's money sitting in a small-cap fund. When the mapping is written down, mismatches become visible before they become costly.

Two practical cautions. First, a single holding may serve more than one goal, especially in retirement accounts where the assets are commingled; the mapping should note the split and be honest that it is approximate. Second, some assets are hard to bucket because they are effectively a store of value rather than a funding source — the household home being the usual example. These are recorded on the balance sheet and discussed in the policy, but they should not quietly be treated as available to fund other goals.

Bucketing also fixes the meaning of a sale. Selling a holding that is marked for a long-horizon goal to fund an emergency is not a portfolio decision; it is a symptom that the emergency reserve is too small. That is the link to the reserve article and to the emergency-fund calculator, which answers how much of the required outflow the liquid assets actually cover.

## Ranking goals when they conflict

Goals compete for money, time and attention. Three are commonly in direct conflict: paying down debt, funding long-horizon growth, and holding liquid reserves. The ranking should be set in advance, and a workable order of reasoning is:

1. **Protect the ability to earn.** Insurance and an adequate reserve come before investment, because they prevent a temporary shock from becoming a permanent sale.
2. **Fund the mandatory and near-dated goals.** A goal that fails if it is missed outranks one that can be deferred.
3. **Fund the long-horizon mandatory goals steadily**, accepting that the path will be volatile.
4. **Fund discretionary goals last.** They are the ones for which a delay is an acceptable response to a shortfall.

Writing the ranking down means that when income dips, the household reduces the discretionary goal rather than raiding the one that matters. The alternative — deciding in the moment — reliably favours whichever goal is most emotionally present.

## Worked example: sizing a goal and bucketing it

The following is an illustrative calculation, not observed data or a recommendation. Suppose a household estimates that a child's education will cost ₹20,00,000 in today's money, ten years from now, and assumes the relevant cost rises at about 6% a year. The amount to fund is:

$$ F = 20{,}00{,}000 \times (1.06)^{10} \approx 35{,}81{,}695 $$

so the target is roughly ₹35.8 lakh at the date, not ₹20 lakh. If the household intends to fund it through monthly contributions earning an illustrative 8% a year, the required monthly amount is about ₹19,580:

$$ \text{monthly contribution} = \frac{35{,}81{,}695 \times 0.00667}{(1.00667)^{120} - 1} \approx 19{,}580 $$

The numbers are illustrative and depend entirely on the assumed rates; change the inflation assumption by a point and the required contribution moves materially. The useful output is not the figure itself but the discovery of its size: a goal that looked like a ₹20 lakh problem is a ₹36 lakh problem, which changes where it sits in the ranking.

Bucketing follows immediately. The education goal has a ten-year horizon, so it belongs in the long band and can hold growth assets for most of that time, with the treatment shifting toward stability as 2034 approaches. The emergency reserve, by contrast, sits in the short band and stays in same-day instruments regardless of what returns they offer — the Opportunity Cost calculator quantifies what that idle money gives up, which is the honest price of the availability it buys.

## Reviewing and revising goals

A goal register has two clocks, mirroring the policy's. The first is the annual review, at which every date is one year closer, every amount is re-estimated, and the ranking is reconsidered. The second is event-driven: a new dependant, a change in employment or income, a health event, an inheritance, or the completion of a goal all require the register to be reopened.

When a goal becomes underfunded, there are only three levers: move the date, reduce the amount, or accept more risk. The policy should ideally say which lever is preferred for which class of goal, so that the choice is not improvised. Deferring a goal whose date is flexible is usually the least costly; accepting more risk to rescue a near-dated goal is usually the most expensive, because the horizon does not allow the risk to be recovered.

## What this does not tell you

This article supplies a structure and a piece of arithmetic, not a set of target amounts. The estimates of future cost, the assumed inflation rates and the ranking are household judgements, and none of them is verifiable in advance — the method is what can be checked. Nor does goal definition say anything about which products to use; bucketing only states the horizon a holding must respect, leaving instrument selection to the account and product discussions elsewhere in this series.

The mapping is also only as good as its maintenance. A goal register that is not revisited once a year decays into a list of intentions, and the buckets quietly stop matching the holdings. Finally, precision has a cost: a goal estimated to the rupee implies a confidence that does not exist, and it is better to record the assumption and the confidence level alongside the figure than to present a spurious exactness.

## Open questions

- How much precision in a goal amount is genuinely useful before the estimate stops adding information and starts implying false confidence?
- Should horizon boundaries be fixed across a household or set goal by goal, and does consistency itself change behaviour?
- When a goal becomes underfunded, should the policy pre-commit to moving the date, reducing the amount, or accepting more risk — and for which classes of goal?
- How should an asset that serves both a store-of-value purpose and a funding purpose, such as a home, be represented so it is neither silently available nor entirely ignored?

The register and the buckets are the household's answer to a question the market never asks: not "what did this holding earn?", but "what was it for, and was it there when it was needed?" Everything that follows in this series is easier once each rupee has a purpose and a date.

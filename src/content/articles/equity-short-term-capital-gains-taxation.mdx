Short-term equity gains are the most expensive common outcome in an Indian investor's tax year, and the most misunderstood. They are taxed at a flat rate that ignores the slab structure entirely, they receive no annual exemption, they attract a surcharge that is capped rather than banded, and they become an advance-tax obligation in the quarter they are realised. Classification is decided by a single line — a holding period of 12 months — and the rate behind it changed for transfers on or after 23 July 2024.

This article states the position as verified against the cited sources for **AY 2026-27 (FY 2025-26)**, with every figure dated. It is a reference page: the numbers live in the tax registry that powers this site's calculators, and the primary sources are one click away. The companion article on the [long-term framework](/library/equity-long-term-capital-gains-and-section-112a) covers the other side of the 12-month line.

## The rule in one paragraph

Where a listed equity share, an equity-oriented fund unit, or a business-trust unit is transferred with **securities transaction tax (STT) paid** on the transfer, and the asset was held for **12 months or less**, the resulting short-term capital gain is taxed at a **flat 20% on the gross gain**, instead of the slab rates that would otherwise apply. No annual exemption applies. Surcharge is capped at 15% for this income, and health and education cess at 4% is charged on tax plus surcharge. The flat rate has applied for transfers on or after 23 July 2024, replacing an earlier 15% rate.

Each clause carries weight. Take them in order.

## The 12-month line decides the treatment

The classification is by holding period, not by intent, and the line for these assets is a holding period of more than 12 months for long-term status and 12 months or less for short-term status. There is no partial treatment: a position held for eleven months and three weeks is short-term in full, and the only way to change the answer is to change the dates. Because the outcome turns on the exact acquisition and transfer dates, the contract notes are the evidence, and the boundary cases are where records matter more than rules of thumb.

The line is asset-specific. Listed equity shares, units of equity-oriented funds and units of a business trust use the 12-month threshold. Other assets use longer thresholds — unlisted shares and immovable property use a period of more than 24 months, for instance — which is why the same trading profit can carry a very different rate depending on what was traded. A debt-oriented mutual fund is a separate case again: units acquired on or after 1 April 2023 are deemed short-term under the specified-fund provision regardless of how long they are held, and are taxed at slab rates with no indexation, so they never reach the long-term framework at all.

## What qualifies, and the securities transaction tax condition

Three asset types feed the flat rate: listed equity shares, units of an equity-oriented fund, and units of a business trust such as a real-estate or infrastructure investment trust. The operative test in each case is whether STT was paid on the transfer. A delivery of listed shares through a recognised exchange carries it; certain off-market transfers and some other transactions do not, and where STT has not been paid on the transfer, the concessional flat-rate treatment can fall away and the gain reverts to the ordinary framework. Off-market gifts, physical-share scenarios and transfers outside the exchange mechanism are the classic places where the assumption that the flat rate applies breaks down.

For funds, the classification turns on whether the fund is equity-oriented, which is tested against a prescribed domestic-equity threshold — the 65% test referred to in this site's rules — so a fund that sits below that threshold is not an equity-oriented fund for these purposes and its gains follow a different route. The distinction matters because the flat rate and the annual exemption both attach to the equity-oriented class, and a fund on the wrong side of the line is taxed differently on identical performance. The tax registry records the current securities transaction tax rate structure as **provisional**, because the rates differ by instrument and by side of the transaction; what is verified is the condition that STT must have been paid on the transfer for the flat rate to apply.

## The 20% rate, surcharge and cess

The flat rate is 20% on the gross gain, applied instead of the slab rates. That phrase — instead of the slab rates — is the whole point of the provision: a taxpayer whose other income sits in the nil band still pays 20% on a short-term equity gain, because the gain is not stacked into the slabs at all. The rate moved from 15% to 20% for transfers on or after 23 July 2024, which means the effective date, and not the date of acquisition, decides which rate applies to a given sale.

On top of the flat rate, surcharge is charged on the tax within the ordinary graduated bands keyed to total income — nil up to ₹50 lakh, 10% where total income exceeds ₹50 lakh, 15% above ₹1 crore, 25% above ₹2 crore, and above ₹5 crore 25% under the new regime or 37% under the old regime — but with an important qualification for this income class. The 25% and 37% rates do not apply to income chargeable under the short-term and long-term capital-gains provisions or to dividend income, where the maximum surcharge is 15% in both regimes. A high-income investor who is otherwise facing a 37% surcharge in the old regime therefore does not pay it on a short-term equity gain. Marginal relief operates at each surcharge boundary. Health and education cess is 4% of income tax plus surcharge, so the effective rate on a short-term gain is always somewhat above the headline 20%.

## Explain simply

If you buy and sell listed shares or an equity mutual fund within a year, the profit is treated as a short-term gain and taxed at a flat 20%, whatever your income. You get no free allowance on this. Long-term gains have their own lower rate and a small annual exemption, but short-term gains do not. Taxes called surcharge and cess are added on top, though the surcharge is capped for this kind of gain. Keep your contract notes, because the exact dates decide whether a holding is short-term or long-term.

## Worked example

This is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast or a recommendation. It uses the rate and the holding-period rule stated above.

A resident individual buys listed shares for **₹5,00,000** and sells them after **7 months** for **₹6,20,000**, with STT paid on the sale.

- **Holding period:** 7 months, so the gain is short-term.
- **Gross gain:** ₹6,20,000 − ₹5,00,000 = **₹1,20,000**.
- **No exemption:** the ₹1,25,000 annual allowance does not apply to short-term gains, so the whole ₹1,20,000 is taxable.
- **Tax at the flat rate:** ₹1,20,000 × 20% = **₹24,000**.
- **Cess at 4%:** ₹960. **Total:** **₹24,960**, with no surcharge at this income level.
- **Effective rate on the gain:** about **20.8%** — the flat rate plus cess.

Two variations show the framework at work. If the same shares had been sold after **14 months** instead, the gain would fall under the long-term framework, where the first ₹1,25,000 of aggregate eligible long-term gain is exempt and the balance is taxed at 12.5% — a materially different answer on the same price move. And if the ₹1,20,000 above were part of a year in which total income exceeded ₹50 lakh, a surcharge of up to 10% would apply to the tax, still within the 15% cap that protects this income class. ## Losses under the section 111A framework

Losses are the more flexible side of the framework. A short-term capital loss may be set off against both short-term and long-term capital gains in the same year; a long-term capital loss may be set off only against long-term gains. Unabsorbed capital losses may be carried forward for up to eight assessment years, and the short-term or long-term character of the loss is preserved in the carry-forward. Carry-forward requires the return to be filed by the due date, and a belated return loses it even where the loss is disclosed — the point most often missed.

Two sequencing rules deserve attention. A short-term loss arising in this class cannot be set off against gains that are not subject to the same provision, other than long-term capital gains, so it cannot be used against salary, interest or other heads. And a long-term loss on an asset eligible for the long-term concessional rate is confined to gains of the same character, which means a correcting market can leave losses that are real but temporarily unusable. The **tax-loss harvesting planner** models how much loss can absorb gains in a year and how much remains to be carried forward.

## Advance tax: due in the quarter the gain arises

A realised short-term gain is not invisible until the return. Advance tax is payable in instalments as the year runs, and capital gains are treated differently from ordinary income: because a future gain cannot reasonably be estimated, the gain is treated as income of the quarter in which it arises for the purpose of the instalment in which it is included. The practical consequence is direct — a gain realised in September puts two instalments in play, and a large gain realised in the last quarter should be covered by the instalment due on 15 March rather than left to the return. Waiting until filing invites interest on the shortfall, computed on the assessed tax less any tax deducted or collected at source. The **advance tax schedule calculator** lays out the dates and computes the instalment effect of a gain realised in any quarter. The obligation is not confined to traders: one large redemption after a strong run is enough.

## When the income is business income instead

Not every short-term profit is a capital gain. Where transactions are frequent and systematic, or derivative-based, the income may be assessed as business income, which follows a different route: slab rates on the net trading profit, with the ordinary surcharge scale and 4% cess. The difference is not cosmetic, because business income does not receive the 15% surcharge cap — it can carry a surcharge of 25% or 37% — and the loss rules are different, with business losses, speculative losses and capital losses each governed separately. This site records the business-income rule, including the treatment of derivative transactions, as **provisional**, and the classification of a personal account as investment or business depends on facts such as frequency, turnover and how the activity is presented. It is a question to settle with a professional rather than to assume from a single trade.

## What this does not tell you

This article states the rate and the classification rule but does not compute a taxpayer's liability, because that needs the full year's gains, losses and other income. It does not list current securities transaction tax and stamp-duty rates, which differ by instrument and side and are recorded as provisional; it states only the condition that STT must have been paid on the transfer. It does not draw the investment-versus-business line, which is fact-dependent. It does not cover the position of a non-resident, whose treatment differs on several points. And it does not resolve the 1961-to-2025 section mapping. For all of those, the primary sources and the taxpayer's own records decide.

One forward-looking caveat, recorded plainly: the **Income-tax Act, 2025** takes effect from 1 April 2026 and carries the capital-gains framework forward in substance, with restructured provisions and renumbered forms. The individual section mapping between the two Acts is flagged in this site's tax registry as **pending verification**, and this article will be updated, with its reviewed date changed, as official guidance settles it.

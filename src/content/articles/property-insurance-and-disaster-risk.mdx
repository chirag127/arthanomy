A homeowner usually buys insurance against the wrong question. The question is not whether a policy exists, but which of the many ways a building can be damaged, or a tenancy interrupted, will actually be compensated, at what value, and after which deduction. A property contract is written as a short grant of cover followed by a much longer set of qualifications, and the qualifications are where the design of the contract really lives.

That ordering is not an accident of drafting. Insurance prices a defined peril over a defined period at an agreed value, and everything outside that definition falls on the owner. The useful skill, for someone assembling cover for a home or a let-out flat, is therefore not choosing a brand. It is reading a schedule and an exclusion clause accurately, and knowing the arithmetic that decides how much of a loss comes back.

## Explain simply

Imagine your flat is damaged. Three separate questions decide how much money returns to you. The first is whether the cause of the damage is one the policy responds to at all. The second is what value the contract puts on the property, because a policy that insures a five-million-rupee rebuilding job for four million will not pay a five-million-rupee claim in full if it carries an underinsurance condition. The third is what the policy subtracts before it pays: a fixed excess, maybe a cap on a category such as contents or a particular peril, and in many contracts a deduction for the age of what was destroyed. Most disappointment after a loss traces back to one of those three questions, and all three are answered in writing before you ever need to claim.

## What a property contract is, and what it is not

Property insurance is a contract of indemnity. It is meant to put the insured back into the financial position they were in before the loss, not to improve it and not to supply a capital gain. That single purpose explains a large share of the conditions that surprise policyholders: a deduction for wear and tear, a cap at the pre-loss value rather than the replacement cost, and a refusal to pay for improvements made during the repair. It also explains an important limit that no amount of premium can remove. Insurance covers events that are accidental and outside the control of the insured, and a loss that the owner could have prevented, or that arises from the building being in a condition it should not be in, sits awkwardly with that definition.

Insurance is also not a substitute for the physical condition of the asset. How a structure behaves in a hazard is determined by how it was designed and built, and the standards for that are set out in the building codes and Indian Standards rather than in a policy. A building that complies with the applicable code for its zone is a different risk from one that does not, and the difference shows up in the loss itself long before it shows up in a claim settlement. Anyone assessing a property as an asset, whether to live in or to let, is therefore assessing its hazard exposure as well, and that assessment starts with the structure and only later reaches the insurer.

## The cover is the short part of the document

A householder schedule typically deals with a small number of things: the structure, the contents, and for some products a liability element and an element for alternative accommodation or loss of rent. The way to read it is not to look for reassurance but to locate four facts.

| What to establish | Why it decides the outcome | Where it is stated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The perils covered and each one's sub-limit | Decides whether the loss is an insured loss at all | The grant of cover and the schedule of limits |
| The basis of valuation | Decides whether a claim is settled at replacement cost or at a depreciated value | The sums insured and the settlement clause |
| The sum insured for each section | Decides the ceiling, and whether an underinsurance condition bites | The schedule |
| The excess or deductible | Decides the first slice of every loss, which falls on the owner | The schedule and the claims conditions |

The table is deliberately a set of instructions rather than a list of what any product contains. Product design varies, the wording is filed with the regulator, and the only authoritative statement of what a given policy covers is that policy's own schedule and clauses. Where cover is bought through a society or an employer, the schedule belongs to that arrangement and the individual flat owner may not have seen it, which is a good reason to ask for it.

## The perils that are frequently uninsured

The interesting pattern in property cover runs in one direction. The perils that are easiest to bear are the ones most readily included, and the perils that would hurt most are the ones most likely to be conditioned, sub-limited or left out. Fire and a burst pipe are inexpensive to cover and are usually within the base grant. Earthquake, flood and storm are the classic catastrophic exposures, and the treatment of each depends on the product: included within an overall limit, included subject to a separate sub-limit, available as an add-on, or excluded.

There is a structural reason for this, and it is worth stating plainly. Catastrophe risk is not independent across properties. A single insurer holding cover on many homes in one city faces the possibility that a single event damages all of them at once, so the loss it might bear is correlated rather than diversified. That is why catastrophe cover is priced and structured differently, and why the treatment of these perils is the first thing to verify rather than the last.

For an owner, three additional exposures deserve naming, because each is commonly assumed to be covered when it is not:

- **Loss of rent or of use.** After a disaster, the structure may be repair-worthy but the income stops, and a tenant may lawfully leave. Rent is a separate insurable interest and usually needs its own cover.
- **Damage to improvements or fit-outs.** Two fits-out flats in the same building can be worth very differently, and generic contents cover rarely reflects a fitted kitchen or flooring at replacement value.
- **Damage that was never the owner's to claim.** Where the society insures the structure and the owner insures only contents, the two may or may not meet. Gaps between the two are the ordinary place where a real loss goes unclaimed.

## Reading an exclusion list properly

An exclusion is not a defect; it is the boundary of the promise, and reading it is a matter of translation. Four kinds of clause do most of the work.

1. **The peril exclusion.** A named peril outside the grant, such as an event the policy simply does not respond to.
2. **The condition precedent.** A duty that must be satisfied before the insurer becomes liable, such as steps to prevent further damage after a loss.
3. **The valuation condition.** The rule that converts a loss into a settlement figure: reinstatement or market value, depreciation, and the proportionate-reduction condition described below.
4. **The maintenance and legality condition.** An expectation that the building is lawfully constructed, occupied and maintained. A structure that lacks its occupancy certificate, or that has been altered without approval, is a difficult claim before it is a difficult sale.

The occupancy certificate is worth pausing on, because it sits at the intersection of insurance and the RERA framework. Handing over possession of a building that is not authorised for occupation is treated as a defect in the transaction, and RERA places the responsibility for obtaining the certificate on the promoter. The same fact that gives an allottee a remedy also marks a gap in insurable condition, which is why the certificate is worth obtaining and keeping before a dispute is ever needed.

## Underinsurance, and the arithmetic that amplifies it

Underinsurance is the most quietly expensive feature of household cover. The sum insured is not a suggested retail price. It is a statement of the value at risk, and where the contract includes a proportionate-reduction condition, a claim is paid in the ratio that the sum insured bears to the actual value at risk.

| Element | Illustrative figure |
| --- | --- |
| Current cost to rebuild and refurnish the flat | ₹50,00,000 |
| Sum insured on the building | ₹40,00,000 |
| Damage assessed after a fire | ₹10,00,000 |
| Ratio of sum insured to value at risk | 0.80 |
| Settlement after proportionate reduction | ₹8,00,000 |
| Shortfall borne by the owner | ₹2,00,000 |

Those figures are an illustrative calculation, not a quotation from any policy, premium or claim. The point they carry is structural: a sum insured set at eighty per cent of the true value does not merely leave a twenty per cent gap, it applies that gap to every partial loss as well as to a total one. Whether a particular policy carries such a condition, and on what basis the value at risk is measured, is stated in the wording.

The second half of the problem is that the value at risk moves while the sum insured does not. Rebuilding and replacement costs drift with prices generally, and a number fixed several years ago is a smaller claim on today's cost of reinstatement. The illustrative arithmetic is unforgiving:

$$ 1 / 1.06^5 \approx 0.747 $$

At an assumed six per cent a year of cost inflation, an unrevised sum insured covers roughly three-quarters of the cost it was originally written against after five years, again as an illustrative calculation rather than a forecast. Cover that is faithfully paid for and never reviewed is cover that slowly shrinks.

## Disaster risk for a let-out asset

A rented flat is a business, however small, and its insurance needs follow the business rather than the family home. Three exposures matter, and they fail in different ways.

**The income stream.** The year in which a flood makes a flat uninhabitable is the year the rent stops and the mortgage does not. Because the rental yield is the return the owner is buying, a credible property model should treat loss of rent as part of the return that needs protecting, not as an afterthought. The rental-yield arithmetic that is used to judge whether the property is worth holding is the same arithmetic that quantifies what is at stake when the tenancy is interrupted.

**The tenant's property, and the tenant's duty.** Standard wear is a cost of letting. Tenant damage beyond wear is a liability question that turns on the lease, and the owner's policy and the tenant's policy are two separate contracts that need not dovetail. Leaving the allocation to the lease and checking that both sides actually hold the cover stated in it is the only way to close the gap.

**The redevelopment timetable.** A society going through redevelopment is a transfer of risk in time as well as in law, and the period of demolition and reconstruction is a period in which the rules for insuring and the parties responsible can shift. Rediscovering which cover applies to a building that is being pulled down is not a conversation to have after a loss.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the structure of property cover and the arithmetic of the losses that sit outside it. It does not quote the terms of any product, because products differ and their terms are filed with the regulator rather than fixed by this website. It does not give premium rates, sub-limits, excess amounts or standard exclusions numerically, because none of those is stable across products or across time, and a number copied from one policy would be wrong for almost every reader.

It also does not value a property, assess its hazard, or judge whether a particular sum insured is adequate. Those require the current local cost of reinstatement and a reading of the specific schedule. What it does do is set the order of work: fix the physical condition of the asset first, then decide what value is at risk, then read the exclusions against that value, and review the whole thing periodically rather than once.

## Open questions

- In a large flood or earthquake, how much of the gap between economic damage and settled claims is attributable to underinsurance rather than to uninsured perils, and can that split be measured from published data?
- For a let-out property, is there a level at which catastrophe cover costs more than the income it protects is worth, and how should an owner think about that trade-off?
- If a certificate of completed construction were a condition of standard cover as well as of legal occupation, would that change promoter behaviour before handover or only the paperwork afterwards?
- How should a flat owner establish, in a building where the society insures the structure, the exact boundary between that cover and their own?

The habit that makes property insurance useful is unglamorous. Read the schedule, find the valuation basis, put a defensible number on what is at risk, name the perils that would matter most if they happened, and check that the two documents in the building, the certificate of construction and the policy, are both current.

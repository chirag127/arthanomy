Sending money out of India looks like a payment and is actually a compliance event. Before any rupee moves, the purpose must be permitted under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, the money must pass through an authorised dealer bank, and the transaction must be reported in a way the regulator can see. Only then does the ordinary question arise: which channel moves the money cheapest. This article separates the compliance frame from the cost anatomy, because conflating them is how remittances either get refused or get overpaid.

## Explain simply

If you want to send money from India to someone abroad, you cannot simply hand it to any app. The government allows resident individuals to remit money abroad up to a yearly limit, but only for approved purposes such as education, travel, gifts or investment, and only through a bank that is authorised to deal in foreign exchange. The bank checks the purpose, collects any tax that must be collected at source, and then moves the money. The cost of doing this is not just the exchange rate you are quoted. It is the gap between the real market rate and the rate you get, plus fixed charges, plus the tax collected up front, plus the time the money takes to arrive. A channel that looks cheaper on the exchange rate can be more expensive once all of these are counted.

## The LRS frame: what may be remitted, through whom

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme is the Reserve Bank of India's framework under which resident individuals, including minors through guardians, remit funds abroad. The RBI master direction on the scheme and on FEMA current-account transactions sets out the annual limit, the categories of permitted current and capital account transactions, and the purposes that are prohibited. The limit is per person per year, not per transaction, so a remitter who has already used part of the allowance has less headroom left. The scheme does not create a right to remit for any purpose; it creates a permission bounded by purpose categories, and a transaction outside those categories is not a higher-cost version of the same thing, it is a different and potentially impermissible transaction.

Two features of the frame matter for the cost discussion. First, remittances must be routed through an authorised dealer bank. A bank is not merely a conduit; it is the entity that classifies the purpose, applies the reporting, and collects any tax due at source. Second, the scheme interacts with tax-collected-at-source rules: specified remittances attract TCS at prescribed rates, which the bank or remitter collects and deposits with reference to the remitter's identity. The current limit and the current TCS rate are both set by the authorities and change over time; the packet behind this article does not carry the numbers, so both must be verified from the latest RBI master direction and the prevailing Finance Act before any figure is relied upon.

## The channels: bank wire, cards and platforms

For an LRS remittance to a foreign beneficiary, the practical channels are fewer than the marketing suggests.

- **Bank wire (SWIFT).** The remitter's bank debits the rupee account, converts at the customer rate, and sends a SWIFT message to the beneficiary's bank. This is the default for larger amounts and for purposes such as education or investment, because the bank can classify the purpose and report it. The cost is the exchange-rate spread plus a fixed outward-remittance charge, and the credit can take one to several business days depending on correspondent banks and time zones.
- **Forex cards and wallets.** A prepaid card or wallet loaded with foreign currency can pay abroad without a fresh wire each time. The spread is locked at load time, which is useful when the rupee is moving, but the card network and issuer add their own charges, and not every LRS purpose is payable by card.
- **Fintech platforms.** Several platforms offer outward remittances in partnership with banks. The platform is usually the interface and the bank is the authorised dealer behind it. The spread can be tighter than a bank's quoted rate, but the compliance and recourse still sit with the bank, and the platform's charges must be added to the bank's to get the all-in figure.

The domestic payment rails that dominate Indian retail payments are a different category. The NPCI operates UPI and other systems that move rupees instantly between bank accounts inside India. Those rails are fast and cheap, but they are domestic. They do not by themselves constitute an outbound remittance channel under the LRS, and the fraud typologies that affect retail payments — social engineering, phishing, account takeover — are a reminder that the convenience of instant domestic money movement does not transfer to cross-border compliance.

## What a remittance actually costs

The all-in cost of a remittance has four parts, and the quoted exchange rate is only one of them.

- **The spread.** The interbank rate is the rate at which banks trade currency with each other. The customer rate is worse than that, and the gap is the spread. It is usually the largest cost on a large remittance and is rarely quoted openly; it is embedded in the rate offered.
- **Fixed charges.** Banks charge a flat outward-remittance fee, and platforms may add a service fee. On a small remittance a fixed charge can dominate the spread.
- **Tax collected at source.** On specified remittances, TCS is collected at a prescribed rate and deposited with reference to the remitter's identity. It is not an additional tax; it appears in the remitter's tax-credit records and is available for set-off against final tax liability, subject to the conditions in the Act. Its cost is the cash-flow timing, not the amount.
- **Time.** A wire that takes three days and a card that settles instantly are not the same product, and the difference has value when the rupee is moving or the beneficiary is waiting.

The honest comparison is therefore not "which channel has the best rate" but "which channel has the lowest all-in rupee cost for this amount, this purpose and this urgency, after spread, fixed charges, TCS and time."

## Documentation, purpose codes and the compliance trail

Because the bank is the authorised dealer, the remitter supplies the documentation. For a permitted purpose such as education or travel, this typically means a purpose code, supporting documents such as an invoice or admission letter, and a declaration. The bank reports the transaction under the LRS, and the TCS, where applicable, is deposited against the remitter's permanent account number. The trail matters later: a remittance that was not properly classified or reported can create a problem when the remitter later tries to explain the foreign asset or income in the return. The schedule in which a resident declares foreign assets — bank accounts, custodial holdings, foreign equity, immovable property and trusts — is where an unexplained remittance surfaces, and non-disclosure carries severe consequences under the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) Act, 2015. The compliance step is not a formality to be skipped for a better rate; it is the difference between a remittance that is clean and one that is a future liability.

## Timing: when the money arrives

The credit timing depends on the channel and the corridor. A bank wire to a major currency corridor can settle in one to three business days, but the chain runs through correspondent banks, and a delay at any leg delays the whole. A forex card settles at the point of sale, which is effectively instant for the merchant but exposes the remitter to the rate at the moment of the transaction. A platform may quote a faster credit because it pre-positions funds, but the underlying bank leg still takes its time. For a purpose with a hard deadline — a tuition fee, a medical payment — the relevant question is not the average time but the worst-case time, and the channel should be chosen with that in mind.

## Which channel costs least in practice

The answer is conditional, and the condition is the amount. For a large remittance, the spread dominates, and a bank wire — where the spread can be negotiated and the compliance is certain — often has the lowest all-in cost even after fixed charges. For a small remittance, the fixed charges dominate, and a card or platform with a low or zero fixed charge can win even at a slightly wider spread. For a purpose with a hard deadline, the fastest channel may be worth a modest premium. The mistake is to compare headline rates and ignore the other three cost components, or to choose a platform for its spread and discover that the compliance or recourse sits with a bank that is harder to reach. The method is to compute the all-in rupee cost for the specific amount and purpose, and to verify the LRS limit and TCS rate before computing anything at all.

## Worked example: an illustrative cost comparison

The following is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast and not a live quote. It shows how the four cost components combine.

Suppose a remitter sends an illustrative ₹10,00,000 under a permitted purpose. The interbank rate is an illustrative ₹85 per dollar. A bank quotes a customer rate of ₹84.50, an illustrative spread of 0.59 per cent, and charges a fixed ₹500 outward-remittance fee. A platform quotes ₹84.75, an illustrative spread of 0.29 per cent, and charges a fixed ₹200 service fee. TCS, where it applies, is collected at a prescribed rate on the remittance and is creditable, so it is a timing cost, not a final cost, and is excluded from this comparison.

- Bank: spread cost $1000000 \times 0.0059 = ₹5,900$, plus ₹500 fixed, total ₹6,400.
- Platform: spread cost $1000000 \times 0.0029 = ₹2,900$, plus ₹200 fixed, total ₹3,100.

On these illustrative numbers the platform is cheaper by ₹3,300. But the comparison is incomplete. If the amount were ten times larger, the spread would dominate and the ranking could hold or reverse depending on the exact rates; if the amount were small, the fixed charges would dominate and the platform's advantage would grow. And if the platform's compliance or recourse were weaker, the cheaper spread would be buying a worse product. The honest conclusion is not that one channel always wins, but that the winner is the one with the lowest all-in cost for the specific case, and that the all-in cost must be computed, not assumed.

## The remittance checklist

Worked in this order, the questions are answerable and the decision becomes a payment rather than a problem:

1. **Purpose.** Is this purpose permitted under the LRS, and is the amount within the annual limit?
2. **Channel.** Can an authorised dealer bank execute this purpose, and is a platform or card suitable?
3. **All-in cost.** What is the spread, the fixed charge and the TCS, computed for this specific amount?
4. **Documentation.** What purpose code and supporting documents does the bank need, and are they ready?
5. **Timing.** What is the worst-case credit time, and does it meet the deadline?
6. **Trail.** Is the transaction properly classified and reported, so it does not become a future liability?

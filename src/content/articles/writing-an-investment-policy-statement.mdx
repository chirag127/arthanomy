Most investment failures are not failures of knowledge. They are failures of sequence: a decision that would have been reasonable on a calm Tuesday is made instead on the Friday that a headline arrived. The instrument that addresses this is unglamorous and cheap. It is a written statement of what the portfolio is for, what it may hold, how it will be adjusted, and when it will be reviewed.

This article belongs to the Operating a Portfolio series and is its first entry. The series is about the half of investing that never appears in a returns table: record-keeping, mechanics, discipline and documentation. This piece sets the standard that the rest of the series is measured against — the policy that governs every later decision.

## Explain simply

An investment policy statement is a short written plan, made while you are calm, that says what your money is for, what it may be invested in, how it will be rebalanced and when it will be reviewed. Its purpose is not cleverness — it is to stop a large decision being made in the middle of a bad week. The single most important line in it is the mix between broad asset classes, because that mix decides most of the risk you are taking. Everything else in the document exists to keep that mix on track.

## What a policy statement is for

An investment policy statement is a short written document that states a portfolio's objectives, its target allocation, the asset classes and instruments it may and may not hold, the rules for rebalancing, the liquidity it must preserve, and the process for reviewing and changing all of the above. Institutions have used them for decades because committees need a shared, auditable standard. A household needs the same thing for a simpler reason: memory is unreliable and mood is not.

The document's value is behavioural rather than analytical. It is written by the version of the investor who is calm so that it can be read by the version who is frightened. That is the entire trick, and it is why the policy must be written before it is needed, kept short enough to actually reread, and specific enough that its next instruction is not a matter of interpretation.

Note what a policy statement is not. It is not a forecast, not a product recommendation and not a promise of returns. It is closer to a constitution: it defines the process by which ordinary decisions get made.

## The allocation decision comes first

The reason a policy leads with asset allocation rather than with which securities to buy is a result from portfolio theory. Harry Markowitz's 1952 paper "Portfolio Selection" formalised the idea that risk and return are chosen together, that the risk of a collection of assets is not simply the sum of their individual risks because imperfectly correlated assets partly offset each other, and that this yields a distinction between the risk that can be diversified away and the risk that cannot. The set of portfolios that offer the most expected return for a given level of risk became known as the efficient frontier.

Two practical consequences follow, and they are the ones a policy statement is built on. First, the mix between broadly different sleeves — equity, debt, real assets, cash — is the decision that most affects the risk of the whole portfolio, and it is made at the level of the statement rather than in the moment. Second, adding more holdings of the same kind does not keep reducing risk; past a point it only adds complexity. Diversification is a design property, not a collection of products.

The framework is a way of thinking, not a prescription. It does not tell a household what its target should be, and it should never be read as a claim that the future will resemble the past estimates fed into it. It tells the household *where* to make the decision, and that the decision should be made deliberately.

## Objectives: return, risk, liquidity and horizon

A policy states what the portfolio is trying to achieve, in terms specific enough to be checked. The four objectives that matter are:

- **Return objective.** A target real return over a stated period, expressed as a range and with the reason for it. "Enough to fund a stated goal" is more useful than a bare percentage.
- **Risk objective.** The drawdown the household can tolerate without abandoning the plan, ideally stated as a scenario: what would a fall of a given size in the equity sleeve mean for the goals, and would the policy instruct a sale or a top-up?
- **Liquidity objective.** The cash needed and when. This is where the policy meets the emergency reserve: a stated number of months of required outflows that must remain in same-day instruments.
- **Time horizon.** When each pool of money is needed. A horizon is the reason an asset is acceptable or not, and mapping every holding to a dated purpose is what the next article in this series is about.

Writing these down forces the household to admit that some objectives conflict. The policy's job is not to remove the conflict but to rank it in advance.

## Constraints: the things the portfolio cannot ignore

A policy is incomplete without the constraints it must respect. The classical set is worth adopting:

1. **Liquidity constraints.** Money that may be needed soon cannot be held in assets that fall hardest exactly when cash is required.
2. **Tax constraints.** The tax position of each account and holding period affects what should be held where.
3. **Legal and regulatory constraints.** What the household is permitted to hold, and any lock-ins or eligibility conditions that apply.
4. **Unique circumstances.** Ethical or religious screens, employer restrictions on trading certain securities, concentrated positions inherited or earned, and family obligations.

Constraints are where a generic target allocation becomes a specific household's allocation. They should be written as plain statements of fact, with the source of each constraint noted — an employer policy, a trust deed, a preference.

## Permitted and prohibited instruments

This is the section that does the most work, because it removes whole classes of decision from the moment. A useful format is a positive list, a negative list, and a set of concentration limits.

The positive list might name the categories the household intends to use. The negative list names what it will not use and, crucially, why: instruments whose mechanics are not understood, products sold with return guarantees that the seller cannot lawfully make, anything requiring leverage the household has not modelled, or any asset whose liquidity is worse than the horizon demands.

Concentration limits set ceilings: a maximum weight in a single security, a single issuer, a single fund house, a single property, or a single employer's stock including the value of future salary. Limits convert a vague intention into a rule that can be checked with a number.

Prohibitions are not a sign of timidity. They are the pre-commitment that makes the whole document credible. A policy that permits everything states nothing.

## Rebalancing rules

Rebalancing is the periodic realignment of portfolio weights after market movements have shifted them, done by selling what has drifted above target and adding to what has fallen below, or by directing fresh contributions to the lagging sleeve. It is the mechanism that keeps the realised risk of the portfolio near the risk the policy selected.

The policy should specify three things:

- **The trigger.** Calendar-based (review on fixed dates), threshold-based (act when a sleeve drifts beyond a band), or a combination. A common hybrid is to check on a fixed date but only act if a band has been breached.
- **The band.** How far a weight may drift before action is required, stated in a way that is unambiguous. An absolute band in percentage points and a relative band — a proportion of the target weight — behave differently across sleeves, so the policy should say which it is using.
- **The order of operations.** Use new contributions first, then dividends and distributions, then trimming, then selling. Doing it in that order reduces transaction costs and, in taxable accounts, defers the tax that a sale would trigger.

Two further notes belong in this section. First, rebalancing has a cost — brokerage, exit loads, and the tax on realised gains — and the policy should acknowledge that the band is a tolerance for that cost as well as for risk. Second, rebalancing is not a return strategy; its justification is risk control and the removal of timing decisions, and stating that plainly prevents it from being repurposed later into an attempt to trade.

## Review cadence and event triggers

A policy needs two clocks. The first is a calendar: an annual review on a fixed date, and a lighter quarterly check that asks only whether any band has been breached. The second is a list of events that reopen the policy regardless of the date: a change in employment or income, the arrival of a dependant, a change in health, the taking on or clearing of significant debt, an inheritance, a change in time horizons, or a material change in the household's tax position.

What should *not* trigger a review is the market moving. If the equity sleeve falls, the rebalancing band is the mechanism that responds; the policy document itself should stay shut. Writing this down is what makes the distinction enforceable.

The review produces a record. A decision log — a dated list of what was changed and why — is the difference between a policy that is followed and a policy that is remembered as having been followed. It also makes the next review faster, because it replaces recollection with evidence.

## A template you can fill in

The following structure works at household scale and fits on a few pages. It is a template, not advice.

**1. Purpose and scope.** One paragraph: whose money this governs, which accounts it covers, and the single sentence describing what the portfolio is for.

**2. Objectives.** Target real return range and its reason; maximum tolerable drawdown stated as a scenario; liquidity requirement in months of required outflows; time horizon for each pool.

**3. Constraints.** Liquidity, tax, legal and any unique circumstances, each on one line with its source.

**4. Target allocation.** A table of sleeves with target weights and permitted bands.

| Sleeve | Target weight | Lower band | Upper band | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Growth assets | as decided | as decided | as decided | Long-horizon goals |
| Stability assets | as decided | as decided | as decided | Nearer goals and smoothing |
| Liquidity reserve | as decided | as decided | as decided | Emergency outflows |
| Real assets | as decided | as decided | as decided | Diversification |

**5. Permitted and prohibited instruments.** Positive list, negative list with reasons, and concentration limits including employer stock.

**6. Rebalancing rules.** Trigger type, band definition, order of operations, and the acknowledgement of cost.

**7. Review process.** Annual review date, quarterly band check, event triggers, and where the decision log is kept.

**8. Governance and administration.** Who has authority to act, who may deviate and under what conditions, where account records, nominations and transmission details are documented, and how the policy itself is amended.

The administration clause is easy to skip and expensive to omit: nominations on demat accounts and fund folios, and the record of which accounts hold what, are part of the household's operating system, and the policy is the natural place to name who keeps them current.

## Worked example: a policy clause, resolved

The following is an illustrative example of how a policy converts a decision into arithmetic. It is not advice and the numbers are chosen for clarity, not recommended.

Suppose the policy's target allocation is 60% growth assets and 40% stability assets, with a band of five percentage points in absolute terms, and it instructs the household to use new contributions first. After a strong run, the portfolio reaches 66% growth and 34% stability. The growth sleeve is one point beyond its upper band, so the policy requires action.

The amount that must be moved is:

$$ \text{trim} = 66\% - 60\% = 6\% \text{ of the portfolio} $$

If the portfolio is worth an illustrative ₹50,00,000, the required move is ₹3,00,000 from growth into stability. The order of operations says to look first at the contributions already scheduled: if ₹40,000 a month is being directed to stability and no further contributions are queued for growth, then part of the correction happens without a sale. Only the remainder needs to be trimmed, and only that remainder incurs transaction cost and, in a taxable account, realised tax. The Rebalancing Planner linked below performs exactly this sequence — drift, required trades, and the estimated cost and tax — while the Tax-Loss Harvesting Planner covers the separate question of what to do when a sleeve is below target and a loss is available to realise.

The clause is valuable precisely because none of this had to be decided at the moment the drift appeared. The rule did the deciding.

## What a written policy prevents

It prevents three specific failure modes. It prevents **action triggered by news**, because the calendar and the band decide when action is required. It prevents **silent risk creep**, because allocation drift is checked against a written target rather than absorbed by optimism. And it prevents **product-led selling**, because any new instrument has to pass the positive and negative lists before it can be bought.

It does not prevent being wrong. A policy can encode a target return that the market does not deliver, or a band that is too tight to be worth the trading cost. It cannot make a forecast accurate, and it cannot substitute for insurance, an emergency reserve, or the basic arithmetic of saving enough. What it can do is ensure that when the household is wrong, it is wrong in a way it chose, deliberately, in advance, and can therefore examine afterwards.

## What this does not tell you

This article gives a structure, not a set of targets. The appropriate allocation, the correct number of months of liquidity, and the right bands depend on objectives and constraints that only the household can supply — and they should be derived before they are written, not reverse-engineered from a document template. Nor does a policy statement validate the products inside it: a well-written policy can still be implemented with expensive instruments, and the cost of implementation is a separate question covered elsewhere in this series. Finally, the policy is only as good as its maintenance. A document that is not reread, not logged and not revised on life events quietly stops governing anything.

## Open questions

- Do tolerance bands expressed in absolute percentage points behave better than relative bands across very different portfolio sizes and sleeve volatilities?
- How much of the behavioural benefit survives when the household is the only reader, and does a second reader — a spouse, a trustee — change the outcome?
- Should the review cadence be fixed by a date or by an event, and does one choice produce more consistent behaviour than the other?
- Where should the boundary sit between matters that belong in the policy and matters that belong in operational checklists, so that the document stays short enough to be used?

The document is not the point. The point is the gap it opens between the impulse to act and the action itself. Everything else in this series — the balance sheet, the cash-flow plan, the reserve, the account structure, the nominations — is the machinery that a written policy switches on.

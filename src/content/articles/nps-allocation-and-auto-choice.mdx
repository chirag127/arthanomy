A subscriber pouring money into the National Pension System is making two decisions, whether or not they are treated as decisions. The first is how the corpus is divided across the four asset classes. The second is which schemes implement that division. The framework deliberately separates them, and it provides two very different routes to the first: active choice, in which the subscriber sets the mix within prescribed caps, and auto choice, in which a life-cycle option sets it by a rule that moves with age. The choice between the two routes is itself the most consequential decision most subscribers make, because the route they take decides who is minding the allocation for the next thirty years.

This article, part of the Insurance and Pensions Decoded series, covers active choice, auto choice and the default, what the caps constrain, and how reallocation and switching work. The asset classes themselves are covered in the companion treatment of E, C, G and A.

## The four classes the mix is built from

The allocation is a set of weights across four delegated mandates, and each is constrained in two ways: by the guidelines that define what it may hold, and by the position limits prescribed for it.

- **Class E — equity.** Shares. The growth engine, and the source of most short-term volatility. What constrains it is the equity mandate itself: the scheme is built to stay invested in equity rather than to de-risk at will.
- **Class C — corporate debt.** Company bonds and debt instruments. It earns a spread over government debt and carries credit risk. What constrains it is the credit quality permitted by the guidelines and the scheme's position limits.
- **Class G — government securities.** Sovereign debt. It carries interest-rate risk rather than default risk. What constrains it is the mandate to hold government paper and the limits on instrument and tenure.
- **Class A — alternative assets.** A small, separately limited sleeve of instruments outside the core three. What constrains it is the tightness of its own cap, because less liquid and less transparently priced assets are deliberately kept marginal.

The weights applied to these classes are the allocation; the schemes chosen within them are the implementation. Holding a good scheme in the wrong class is not the same as holding the right class in a less impressive scheme.

## Active choice: setting the mix yourself

Active choice lets the subscriber set the allocation across the classes, within the caps prescribed for each, and select the specific schemes. It is a genuine delegation of a portfolio decision to the account holder.

What it demands in return is attention. An active allocation is a plan that assumes someone will revisit it — that the equity share will be reconsidered as retirement approaches, that a scheme whose approach has changed will be noticed, and that the caps, which are revised, will be re-read. An active allocation that nobody revisits is not really active; it is a self-made default, and it should be judged as one.

The design implication is that active choice suits a subscriber who will treat the allocation as an annual decision rather than a one-off form. For anyone else, the question is not whether active choice offers more control, because it does, but whether the control will be exercised.

## Auto choice: delegating to a life-cycle option

Auto choice delegates the allocation to a life-cycle option. The subscriber does not set the mix; the option does, according to a schedule that moves with the subscriber's age. Contributions flow in, and the weighting between the classes changes over the years without any instruction.

What the option buys is a method that does not depend on the subscriber staying engaged. Its weakness is the mirror image: it cannot respond to anything the schedule does not know about, such as a change in the subscriber's other assets, their health, or the size of the corpus relative to the income it must replace. Auto choice optimises for not requiring a decision, which is a real benefit and a real limitation.

## What a glide path actually does

The mechanism at the centre of auto choice is the glide path. As the subscriber approaches the exit age, the option reduces the share of the corpus that carries the most short-term risk and increases the share held in the steadier classes. In the standard framing, equity exposure tapers and the debt classes take up the slack.

The reasoning rests on the arithmetic of recovery, not on a forecast. A fall in a volatile asset early in the accumulation phase has decades in which to be repaired by subsequent returns and contributions. The same fall in the final years before the corpus must be converted into income has almost no time to repair, and the loss is effectively locked in at the worst moment. The glide path shortens the exposure to that specific risk exactly as the repair window closes.

Two caveats keep it honest. First, the taper is a statement about the shape of the risk, not about expected returns; nothing in the schedule says equity will fall or debt will rise. Second, a glide path cannot know the subscriber's circumstances, so it applies one rule to everyone. It is a defensible default precisely because a rule that is roughly right and always applied beats a statement of intent that is never revisited.

## The default when nothing is chosen

Where a subscriber makes no election, a default applies and contributions are invested anyway. The default is not a neutral state; it is a fully specified allocation chosen by the framework rather than by the subscriber.

The consequence is worth stating plainly. Because the default is silent, a large share of subscribers are invested according to it without having decided anything, and their outcomes are the default's outcomes. That makes the design of the default — and of the taper inside it — more consequential for the system than any individual's active choice. A subscriber who has never logged in should read the default as though it were their own decision, because in effect it is.

## The caps that frame both routes

Both routes sit under the prescribed asset-allocation caps. The caps serve two purposes: they prevent a subscriber or a default schedule from concentrating the pension corpus in the highest-risk class, and they keep class A marginal by limiting it separately.

The caps are prescribed in the framework and revised, and this article deliberately states none of them. The number that matters to a subscriber is the current ceiling on each class, and the correct way to obtain it is to read the PFRDA framework and the NPS Trust's scheme information rather than to rely on any summary, including a remembered one. The structural point survives whatever the numbers are: the caps define the outer edge of what active choice may set and the band the glide path moves within.

## Reallocation and switching

The framework treats the allocation as revisable rather than fixed at joining. Two operations matter.

A **switch** changes the scheme or the class into which future contributions flow and into which existing units are moved. Mechanically, it is a redemption of units in the old scheme and an issue of units in the new one at the applicable net asset values, so the timing of a switch affects the outcome and each switch is an investment decision, not a clerical one.

**Reallocation** is the broader revision of the weights across the classes. It is where an active subscriber adjusts the equity share as retirement approaches, or where a change in circumstances prompts a different mix.

Both are permitted within limits. The framework allows a prescribed number of switches between schemes each year at no charge, with charges applying beyond that. The limit and the charges are set in the framework and change, so they should be read from it. The design intent is visible in the structure: enough free movement to discipline a manager who changes approach, not so much that the account becomes a trading vehicle.

## Explain simply

You decide how your NPS money is split between shares, company bonds, government securities and a small alternatives sleeve — or you let an automatic life-cycle option decide for you and shift the mix as you age. The automatic route tapers the riskiest exposure as retirement approaches, because a fall late in the game has little time to recover. If you never choose anything, a default applies, and that default is the single most consequential part of the design because so many people leave it in place. You can switch schemes and change the mix, but only a limited number of switches are free each year, and every switch means selling units in one scheme and buying in another at their current values.

## Worked example: a fixed mix and a taper

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast. Every return used is an invented assumption chosen to make the arithmetic visible; none is an NPS return and none is a prediction.

Assume, for the arithmetic only, that class E returns 11 per cent a year and the steadier classes return 7 per cent a year on average.

Subscriber A holds an illustrative fixed mix of 75 per cent equity and 25 per cent debt throughout, giving a blended return of:

$$0.75 \times 11 + 0.25 \times 7 = 8.25 + 1.75 = 10.0$$

Subscriber B, on an illustrative taper, holds 25 per cent equity and 75 per cent debt by the final phase, giving:

$$0.25 \times 11 + 0.75 \times 7 = 2.75 + 5.25 = 8.0$$

Now suppose both contribute ₹5,000 a month for twenty years, compounded monthly, and, purely to isolate the allocation effect, assume each earns their blended rate throughout. Subscriber A's corpus works out to roughly ₹37,97,000 and subscriber B's to roughly ₹29,45,000, an illustrative difference of about ₹8,52,000.

The example shows the trade in the simplest terms available. Holding the higher-risk class longer raises the illustrative outcome, and it also means B's corpus is steadier in the years when it is about to be converted into income. Neither number is a promise. Real returns vary year to year, a bad sequence late in the period can overwhelm the average, and the taper's value is precisely that it reduces the chance of that specific outcome — which the arithmetic above, by using constant assumed returns, cannot show.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes the allocation framework, not its parameters. No cap, taper schedule, switching limit, charge or return is quoted, because each is prescribed in the PFRDA framework and revised, and each should be read from the current framework. The arithmetic above uses invented assumptions and demonstrates a method, not a result.

The article does not recommend active or auto choice for any particular subscriber, does not rank schemes, and does not predict a corpus or a pension. The decision to annuitise a share of the corpus at exit, and the annuity rate available then, sit outside the accumulation question entirely and are covered separately. Tax treatment is governed by the Income-tax Act as amended and is not restated here. Nothing here is advice.

## Open questions

- If the default allocation were published as a rate of return, would that help subscribers or encourage a return-chasing reading of a risk design?
- Does active choice change behaviour, or does it simply move an unexamined default to a new position?
- Should the taper key off years to retirement, or off the corpus relative to the income it must replace?
- Do free switches discipline managers, or do they tempt subscribers into timing decisions they cannot win?

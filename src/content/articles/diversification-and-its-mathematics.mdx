The argument for diversification is usually made with an image — eggs, baskets — and an image settles nothing. The serious argument is two lines of algebra, and it says something narrower and stronger than the slogan: combining assets whose returns are imperfectly related reduces the volatility of the whole, without reducing the average of the parts, and the size of that reduction is governed almost entirely by the correlation between them.

This article derives the result for two assets, extends it to many, and is explicit about the one input that makes the whole thing work and the one that makes it fail.

## Explain simply

If two assets do not move up and down in lockstep, then some of the bad years for one will be offset by ordinary years for the other. Because the wobbles partly cancel, the combined portfolio wobbles less than the average of its pieces — even though the average return is unchanged. How much they cancel depends on how closely they move together, a number called correlation. The lower the correlation, the more cancels. Nothing cancels if they move in perfect lockstep, and diversification cannot remove the risk that all assets share.

## Expected return adds; risk does not

Take the average first, because it is the reassuring half. The expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected returns of its holdings:

$$ E[R_p] = w_1 E[R_1] + w_2 E[R_2] $$

If half the money is in an asset expected to return 10% and half in one expected to return 6%, the portfolio is expected to return 8%, no matter how the two move relative to each other. Diversification changes nothing here. This is why it is misleading to describe diversification as a way to "boost" returns — it is a way to hold the expected return steady while taking less risk.

Risk is where the arithmetic turns. Portfolio variance in the two-asset case is:

$$ \sigma_p^2 = w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \rho_{12} \sigma_1 \sigma_2 $$

The first two terms are the individual variances, scaled by the squared weights. The third term is the covariance: the product of the weights, the two volatilities and the correlation between the assets. That third term is the entire point. It can be positive, zero or negative, and it can pull portfolio variance below the weighted average of the parts whenever the correlation is below one.

## The three cases that matter

Correlation is a unit-free number between minus one and plus one. Two assets with a correlation of plus one move in perfect lockstep, one with a correlation of zero show no linear relationship, and one with a correlation of minus one move in perfect opposition. The formula behaves very differently in each case.

| Correlation | What it means | Effect on portfolio volatility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| +1.0 | Perfect lockstep | Volatility is exactly the weighted average of the parts; no benefit |
| 0.0 | No linear relationship | Volatility falls below the weighted average; the covariance term vanishes |
| −1.0 | Perfect opposition | Volatility can fall to zero with the right weights, in theory |

The plus-one case deserves emphasis because it is the honest baseline. If two assets move together perfectly, holding both is the same as holding a blend of one, and the volatility of the portfolio is pulled toward the more volatile holding rather than reduced. "Own several things" is not diversification; "own several things that behave differently" is. A portfolio of five assets that are each the same bet in a different wrapper has a correlation near one in the only dimension that matters, and its volatility reflects that.

The minus-one case is a theoretical limit rather than an achievable one. Real assets rarely move in perfect opposition for long, and the case is useful mainly to show the direction of the effect: the lower the correlation, the greater the share of risk that combination can cancel.

## Adding assets: what survives is the average covariance

With one asset, all of the risk is the asset's own. With a portfolio of many equally weighted assets, the variance of the whole resolves into two parts — an average of the individual variances, and an average of the covariances between every pair:

$$ \sigma_p^2 = \frac{1}{N}\overline{\sigma^2} + \left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right)\overline{\text{Cov}} $$

As the number of holdings rises, the first term shrinks toward zero and the second rises toward the average covariance. The average of the individual variances — the risk unique to each asset — can be spread away almost entirely. The average covariance cannot, because every holding shares it. That irreducible remainder is systematic or market risk, and it is the reason a broadly spread portfolio still falls when everything falls together. Diversification removes the risk that is *specific to holdings*; it does not remove the risk that is *common to them*.

This is also why the marginal value of another holding falls fast. Most of the available variance reduction is captured within a modest number of genuinely independent exposures. Beyond that point the portfolio is priced for the market risk it still carries, and adding names buys very little risk reduction in exchange for tracking error, costs and the loss of any concentration premium.

The correction that follows from this is that diversification should be counted in *risk factors*, not in holdings. Twenty banks, three financial-sector funds and a lending platform can look like a diversified portfolio of two dozen positions while behaving, in the calculation, as a single exposure to credit conditions. The formula does not care how many names are held; it cares how many distinct covariances the portfolio is exposed to. Two investments that rise and fall for the same underlying reason are one investment with two labels, and their correlation will say so sooner than the count of holdings will.

## Where the mathematics is honest about its limits

Four limits are worth stating plainly, because each one describes a way the calculation misleads.

**The inputs are estimates.** The formula is exact given the volatilities and the correlation. The volatilities and the correlation are not given; they are estimated from history, and the estimate carries sampling error. A benefit computed from a short, calm sample is a description of that sample.

**Correlation is a summary of a relationship, and that relationship is not constant.** Diversification depends on correlation, and correlation itself moves — often upward in falling markets, when the diversification is most wanted. A portfolio that looks well spread in the estimate can behave like a single bet exactly when the investor needs it not to. This is treated as its own subject later in the series.

**Symmetric risk is not everyone's risk.** The formula reduces the variance of returns, symmetric in gains and losses. An investor who cares about the depth of the drawdown, or the order in which returns arrive, has a risk definition the variance only partly tracks.

**Valuation can hide the covariance.** Assets that are marked infrequently, or at smoothed prices, will report a lower measured correlation with everything else than their true common exposure warrants. The formula will then promise a benefit that a sale would not confirm. A similar problem arises where holdings are genuinely unlisted: with no traded price, the recorded relationship between them is partly an artefact of valuation practice, and the diversification the numbers show is only as good as the valuations behind it.

**Correlation measures only the linear relationship.** Two assets can be strongly dependent and still show a low correlation if the dependence is non-linear — the same direction in the tail, uncorrelated in the middle. Because the formula uses correlation alone, it can understate the risk that shows up in exactly the stress periods diversification is meant to address.

Against all of this there is a behavioural point. Diversification is disliked precisely when it is working: the concentrated bet is the one that wins big, and the diversified portfolio always contains a holding that is having a bad year. Experimental work on decisions under risk shows how reference points and loss aversion push investors toward exactly that concentration. The mathematics says diversify; the psychology says concentrate in whatever is currently winning. That tension is real and the arithmetic does not dissolve it.

## Worked example: the same two assets, four correlations

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast. Two assets are held in equal halves of a portfolio. Both estimate the same expected return, 8% a year, and the asset volatilities are 20% a year and 10% a year. The only thing that changes between the four cases is the correlation between them. All figures are chosen for the arithmetic.

| Case | Correlation | Portfolio variance | Portfolio volatility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | +1.0 | 225 | 15.00% |
| B | +0.5 | 175 | 13.23% |
| C | 0.0 | 125 | 11.18% |
| D | −1.0 | 25 | 5.00% |

The arithmetic behind one row, using the two-asset formula with weights of one half and volatilities of 20% and 10%:

$$ \sigma_p^2 = (0.5)^2(20)^2 + (0.5)^2(10)^2 + 2(0.5)(0.5)\rho(20)(10) $$

At a correlation of zero, the covariance term is zero and the variance is the sum of the two weighted variances. At a correlation of plus one the covariance term is at its maximum and the portfolio volatility is the weighted average of the two volatilities, 15% — the no-benefit baseline. At a correlation of minus one the term turns negative and the theoretical volatility collapses.

Read the row that is achievable, case B: a correlation of 0.5, entirely realistic for many pairs, takes annualised volatility from 15% to about 13.2% while leaving the expected return at 8%. That is the whole product of diversification. It is not exotic, it does not depend on timing, and it is available for the price of combining exposures rather than concentrating them.

## What this does not tell you

- **It does not tell you which assets to hold.** The formula takes the holdings as given and reports their combined risk. Selecting them is a separate question, and one where the mathematics of covariance is only one input.
- **It does not tell you that more diversification is always better.** Cost, taxes and the loss of a deliberate concentration each subtract from the benefit, and past some point the marginal reduction is negligible.
- **It does not make the portfolio safe.** It removes specific risk and leaves systematic risk, and the remaining exposure can still be large enough to fail a plan.
- **It does not survive a stale correlation input.** A benefit measured on a calm period is a measurement of that period.
- **It does not address the sequence in which returns arrive.** Two portfolios with identical variance can produce very different outcomes for someone withdrawing money.

## Open questions

- What is the most honest way to present a diversification benefit — the point estimate, a range from different historical windows, or the correlation at which the benefit disappears?
- Do assets that appear to de-correlate only when measured at low frequency genuinely diversify, or does the measurement itself manufacture the appearance?
- How quickly does the benefit of a diversified portfolio degrade as correlations drift upward in a stress period, and can that drift be bounded in advance?
- For an Indian investor with a strong home bias in domestic equity and property, does adding international exposure reduce variance in the way the formula suggests once currency and remittance frictions are included?

The two-line result is small and exact. Everything difficult about diversification lies in the quality of the two inputs it takes on trust.

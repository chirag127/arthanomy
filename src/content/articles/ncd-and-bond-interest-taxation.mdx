A coupon is the most legible number in a debt portfolio and one of the most heavily taxed. Interest paid on a corporate bond or debenture is added to the holder's total income and taxed at the applicable slab rate. It is not a capital gain, not a concessional receipt and not exempt, and no length of holding converts the coupon into something better. The instrument wrapper does not change that. What the wrapper changes is the treatment of the gain when the instrument is sold, and for a defined category of recent debt issues that treatment has itself become slab-rate.

This article states what the packet's tax registry supports for **FY 2025-26 (AY 2026-27)**, and it states plainly where the registry carries no figure. Interest, gain and surcharge are three separate questions, and only the first belongs to the coupon.

## The rule in one paragraph

Interest on a bond, debenture or market-linked debenture is ordinary income taxed at the holder's slab rate. Nothing in the wrapper supplies a concessional rate and no annual exemption applies to the coupon. Two rules shape everything around that core position. First, timing: interest is taxed on accrual or receipt according to the holder's method of accounting, so a cumulative instrument generates a taxable amount before any cash arrives. Second, character on sale: under section 50AA, market-linked debentures and unlisted bonds and debentures acquired on or after 1 April 2023 are treated as producing a short-term gain regardless of how long they were held, so the gain is added to total income and taxed at slab rates with no indexation. The coupon and the gain therefore need different computations even on the same instrument.

## Where the interest sits, and where the registry is silent

This site's tax registry places interest under the head income from other sources. It carries no tax rule keyed specifically to corporate-debt coupon interest. The nearest verified entries are the bank and post-office deposit interest rule and the peer-to-peer lending interest rule, both of which describe interest as taxable under income from other sources, together with the surcharge rule, which treats interest as ordinary income on the ordinary surcharge scale. That characterisation is what this article relies on, and the section references are left to the operative provisions rather than restated from memory.

The practical consequence is worth stating rather than hiding. A reader looking for a single registry entry headed "corporate bond coupon" will not find one, and this article does not invent one. It uses the registry's characterisation of interest and points to the statute for the specific charging provision.

## Interest is ordinary income, and that decides the rate

Interest is added to total income and taxed at the slab rate that applies to the holder. For **AY 2026-27** the registry records the new-regime slabs as nil to **₹4,00,000**, then 5% to **₹8,00,000**, 10% to **₹12,00,000**, 15% to **₹16,00,000**, 20% to **₹20,00,000**, 25% to **₹24,00,000** and 30% above **₹24,00,000**. The old regime for an individual below 60 is recorded as nil to **₹2,50,000**, 5% to **₹5,00,000**, 20% to **₹10,00,000** and 30% above **₹10,00,000**, with a higher basic exemption for resident senior and super-senior citizens.

The registry also records a rebate of up to **₹60,000** under the new regime where taxable income does not exceed **₹12,00,000**, and up to **₹12,500** under the old regime where taxable income does not exceed **₹5,00,000**. The rebate applies to the tax on total income and is not available against income chargeable at special rates. Because interest is ordinary income, it sits inside the rebate and slab machinery in the ordinary way — unlike a capital gain under the special provisions.

## Timing: accrual, the coupon date and the cumulative option

The registry's interest entries state the timing rule directly: interest is taxed on accrual or receipt, according to the method of accounting, regardless of whether the instrument has matured. On an accrual basis the charge arises as the interest builds, not when the cash is credited. A debenture that pays a coupon periodically and a cumulative debenture carrying the same coupon can therefore produce the same lifetime cash and a very different sequence of taxable years.

For a holder with interest as a meaningful part of income, the timing has a second consequence: the advance-tax instalments. The registry records instalments falling due at prescribed percentages on **15 June**, **15 September**, **15 December** and **15 March**, and records that interest under the relevant sections can accrue where a known liability is not paid on schedule. Interest credited periodically through the year is easier to track for this purpose than a single accrual recognised at redemption.

## Section 50AA: the gain on sale of recent debt instruments

Section 50AA is the provision that ended the practice of treating a long-held debt instrument as an indexation-based capital-gains vehicle. The registry records the following features.

- It applies to units of a specified mutual fund where not more than **35%** of proceeds are invested in equity shares of domestic companies.
- It applies to market-linked debentures and to unlisted bonds and debentures.
- It applies where the instrument was acquired on or after **1 April 2023**; the gain is then always deemed short-term regardless of how long it is held.
- The gain is taxed at the applicable slab rate with no indexation, and the ordinary surcharge scale applies because the gain is not taxed under the capital-gains provisions that carry the 15% cap.

The vintage of acquisition, not the date of sale, decides which regime applies. For units acquired before 1 April 2023 the registry records a grandfathered position: at least **24 months** for long-term treatment, a **12.5%** long-term rate, and no indexation for transfers on or after **23 July 2024**. A holder must not apply the section 50AA slab treatment to a whole holding that predates the change; broker and registrar statements normally identify holdings by acquisition lot for exactly this reason.

## Surcharge, cess and the cap that does not apply

Interest climbs the ordinary surcharge scale. The registry records surcharge as nil up to **₹50 lakh** of total income, 10% above **₹50 lakh**, 15% above **₹1 crore**, 25% above **₹2 crore**, and above **₹5 crore** 25% under the new regime or 37% under the old regime. Health and education cess is charged at **4%** of income tax plus surcharge in both regimes.

The **15% cap** that limits surcharge on income chargeable under the concessional capital-gains provisions, and on dividend income, does not extend to interest. That asymmetry is one of the strongest tax arguments in favour of a capital-gains route over an interest-bearing route for a high-income holder, and the registry states it in those terms: interest can face 25% or 37%, whereas the capped categories face a maximum of 15%. Marginal relief operates at each surcharge band boundary so that crossing a threshold does not reduce net income.

## Explain simply

Interest you earn on a bond or debenture is treated as ordinary income and taxed at your normal rate, with no special low rate and no annual allowance. If you hold a cumulative debenture, the interest is taxed as it builds up each year even though you receive the money only at maturity. Since 1 April 2023, market-linked debentures and unlisted bonds have lost long-term capital-gains treatment: hold them for a decade and the gain on sale is still treated as short-term and taxed at your slab rate. And because interest is ordinary income, at high income levels it attracts the full surcharge, not the 15% cap that applies to equity capital gains.

## Worked example

This is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast or an investment recommendation. It uses the slab, surcharge and cess rates stated above and assumes a resident individual below 60 in the old regime whose other income already places them in the 30% band.

Bond and debenture interest for the year is **₹1,20,000**, all of it on instruments held for their coupon and not sold.

- Interest added to total income: **₹1,20,000**.
- Tax at the 30% slab rate: ₹1,20,000 × 30% = **₹36,000**.
- Health and education cess at 4%: **₹1,440**.
- Total tax attributable to the interest: **₹37,440**, an effective rate of **31.2%** on the coupon.

Three qualifications belong to the example. If total income were below the basic exemption, the interest would still be taxable but the liability would fall to nil after the exemption and rebate. If total income were above **₹50 lakh**, surcharge would be added on top of the 30%, taking the effective cost higher still. And if the same economic return had been delivered as a long-term gain in a 15%-capped category, the surcharge on it would have been capped at 15% regardless of total income — which is the asymmetry, not a rate difference in the headline figure.

A second, smaller illustration concerns timing. If the same **₹1,20,000** were the accrued interest on a cumulative debenture received only at redemption in a later year, the accrual basis would still bring it into charge as it builds. The cash-flow mismatch, not the rate, is the cost.

## Tax deduction at source on bond interest

Tax may be deducted at source on interest paid or credited. The registry records the deduction provision for interest other than securities, and records that tax is deducted at the prescribed rate once interest exceeds a monetary threshold that differs between banks, co-operative banks, post-office schemes and other payers, and that is higher for a senior citizen. That entry is marked **provisional**, because the thresholds differ by payer category and have been revised repeatedly. This article therefore states no current rate and no current threshold; the operative provision and the current Finance Act figures should be checked for the year in question.

Three consequences are more durable than the exact threshold. First, the threshold is a deduction threshold, not an exemption: interest below it remains fully taxable and must be reported. Second, a deduction at a flat statutory rate is not a final tax — a holder in the 30% band will have a shortfall, while a holder below the taxable threshold may have overpaid and need to claim a refund. Third, the amount deducted is creditable against the final liability and should be reconciled with the annual information statement rather than filed away. Where no deduction is made because each payer stays below the threshold, the resulting shortfall feeds the advance-tax machinery.

The Income-tax Act, 2025, effective from **1 April 2026**, consolidates the tax-deduction provisions for Tax Year 2026-27 onwards. The registry records the consolidation, and the site flags the individual section mapping between the 1961 and 2025 Acts as **pending verification**. Readers relying on a specific section reference should confirm it against the official mapping utility.

## Accrued interest, the purchase price and the record date

A bond bought between coupon dates usually carries an accrued-interest component in the settlement price, representing interest the seller has earned but not yet received. Whether that component is taxed to the seller when it is paid, and how the buyer treats it against the coupon received, is a real question of timing rather than an obvious one. The registry carries no rule for it, so this article describes the issue and does not resolve it; the matter is left in the open questions below and in the linked article on deduction at source and the declaration forms.

What the registry does carry is that the entitlement to a payment depends on the terms of the instrument, which is why the offer document's stated record date and payout option matter to tax as much as to cash flow. Detaching the tax question from the instrument's own terms is where period mismatches begin.

## What this does not tell you

It does not state the current rate or threshold for deduction at source, because the registry marks that entry provisional and the figures differ by payer category. Each of those is a reason to check the primary source and the holder's own facts.

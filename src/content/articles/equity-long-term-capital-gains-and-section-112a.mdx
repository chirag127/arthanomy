Of all the tax rules applying to Indian investors, section 112A is the one most people get approximately right and exactly wrong. They know "12.5% and a lakh twenty-five free" and stop there. The conditions around that sentence — what qualifies, when the exemption applies, what stacks on top, and what happens to losses — decide the actual cheque in cases the summary misses.

This article states the position as verified against the cited sources for **AY 2026-27 (FY 2025-26)**, with every figure dated. It is a reference page: the numbers live in the tax registry that powers this site's calculators, and the primary sources are one click away.

## The rule in one paragraph

Where a resident taxpayer transfers a listed equity share, an equity-oriented fund unit, or a business-trust unit on which **securities transaction tax (STT) has been paid**, and the asset was held for **more than 12 months**, the resulting long-term capital gain is taxed at **12.5%**, with the first **₹1,25,000 of aggregate annual gain exempt** — no indexation, no currency-translation benefit. Surcharge applies but is capped at 15% for this class of income; cess applies at 4% on tax plus surcharge.

Every clause of that paragraph is load-bearing. Take them in order.

## What qualifies

Three asset types: listed equity shares, units of equity-oriented funds, and units of a business trust (the REIT/InvIT wrapper). The private, direct test in each case is whether STT was paid on the *transfer*. Deliveries of listed shares through a recognised exchange carry it; certain off-market transfers and some other transactions do not, and the concessional treatment can fall away. Physical-share scenarios and off-market gifts are the classic places where the "of course I sold shares" assumption breaks.

The ₹1,25,000 exemption is available to **resident individuals and HUFs** for this purpose. Non-resident treatment differs, which matters to NRIs returning or investing across residency changes.

## The ₹1,25,000 exemption: once a year, all assets pooled

The exemption is a single annual threshold applied to the **aggregate** of eligible long-term gains, not a per-transaction allowance, and not per asset or per platform. Gains across your demat account, two mutual fund folios and a REIT position all consume the same ₹1,25,000 for the year. Plans built around "sell just under a lakh in each account" mistake the arithmetic for a reason worth understanding: the exemption is applied once to the total.

Used well, the exemption is a small annual gift: a disciplined investor can realise a measured slice of long-term gains each year, pay nothing on the first ₹1,25,000, and continue — but each sale re-enters the base at the new price, so it is a choice between deferral and exemption, not a free lunch. For someone in the accumulation phase, deferral is often worth more; for someone rebalancing anyway, the exemption is waiting to be used.

## Surcharge, capped — and why that cap matters

Surcharge on ordinary income rises with total income; it is why a ₹51 lakh salary pays more per extra rupee than a ₹49 lakh one. For capital gains taxed under the special regimes — section 112A among them — the surcharge is **capped at 15%** regardless of income level, because the enhanced rates are not applied to this class of income. An investor with ₹5 crore of salary income and ₹10 lakh of 112A gains does not pay 25% or 37% surcharge on those gains. On top of tax and surcharge, cess is 4%.

## Worked example, with every figure shown

A resident investor buys listed shares for **₹10,00,000** and sells after **20 months** for **₹18,00,000**, with STT paid.

- **Gain**: ₹18,00,000 − ₹10,00,000 = **₹8,00,000** (long-term; held more than 12 months).
- **Taxable** after the annual exemption: ₹8,00,000 − ₹1,25,000 = **₹6,75,000**.
- **Tax at 12.5%**: ₹6,75,000 × 12.5% = **₹84,375**.
- **Cess at 4%**: ₹3,375. **Total**: **₹87,750** (no surcharge at this income level).

Effective rate on the full ₹8,00,000 gain: about **11.0%** — the exemption doing its work. Prefer the calculator to this paragraph: the same computation runs on the **post-tax return calculator**, with the exemption, surcharge cap and cess applied inline from the dated registry.

## Losses: narrow set-off, long carry-forward

A long-term capital loss on 112A-class assets may be set off only against long-term capital gains, and a 112A loss cannot be used against gains outside the class — a point many portfolios discover in a correcting market. Unused long-term losses carry forward **up to eight assessment years**, provided the return for the loss year was filed by the due date. That last condition is the one people miss: filing late, even with an otherwise clean position, can extinguish the carry-forward.

The asymmetry is deliberate: the government is willing to wait eight years, but only for those who file on time, and only within the corridor of long-term gains.

## Advance tax: due as you realise, not at filing

Large capital gains are not invisible to the instalment system. If your tax liability for the year exceeds the threshold, advance tax is payable in four instalments as the year runs — and a gain realised in, say, September puts two instalments in play immediately. Waiting until filing to pay invites interest on the shortfall. The **advance-tax schedule calculator** on this site lays out the due dates and computes the instalment effect of gains realised in any quarter. This is also where short-term gains, taxed at their own higher rate, tend to collide with reality: a trading-heavy year can create an instalment obligation long before the money is "kept".

## Filing mechanics — and the Act transition

The gain is reported in the return for the relevant year; the Income Tax Department's AY 2026-27 guidance confirms the s.112A treatment and the exemption threshold in the context of ITR eligibility. Keep the trail: broker contract notes, the brokers' capital-gains statements, and the transaction-level dates — the day-counting and STT evidence live there.

One genuine forward-looking caveat, recorded plainly: the **Income-tax Act, 2025** takes effect from **1 April 2026**, replacing the 1961 Act with restructured provisions and renumbered forms (the familiar Form 16 and 26AS give way to new numbers in the new architecture). The capital-gains provisions are carried forward in substance; the individual section mapping for the new Act is flagged in this site's tax registry as **pending verification**, and this article will be updated — with its updated date changed and the correction logged — as official guidance settles it.

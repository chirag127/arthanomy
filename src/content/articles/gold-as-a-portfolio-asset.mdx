Gold is simultaneously the oldest financial asset and the strangest one. Equity represents a claim on future profits, debt represents a contractual right to interest and principal, and property represents the right to use and let a space. Gold represents none of these. It pays no coupon, generates no earnings and produces no cash flow of any kind. The entire economic return to owning it is the price at which someone else will later take it off your hands.

That single fact reorganises the whole discussion. Once gold is understood as a price-only asset, the arguments for and against it become arguments about *why* the price moves, *what it costs* to hold, and *what tax* attaches — three separate questions that marketing material tends to merge.

## Explain simply

Gold is a lump of metal that does nothing. It has no earnings, pays no interest, and cannot pay you anything however long you hold it. The only way you make money is if someone later pays more for it than you did. That is not a defect — an asset whose value does not depend on anyone's profit can behave differently from shares when the outlook is dark, which is the whole basis of the insurance argument. But because there is nothing underneath the price, you should treat the return you hope for as a hope, not a rate, and you should count the costs of owning it, because those are certain.

## An asset that pays nothing

In the language this library uses elsewhere, an investment's return is income plus price change, measured over a stated period. For gold, the income term is zero by construction. There is no coupon to clip, no dividend to receive, no rent to collect. A gold position's return over any period is exactly its price change, expressed in the currency of the investor.

Two consequences follow immediately.

First, **there is no compounding engine.** A bond reinvests its coupons; a business reinvests its profits; a property reinvests its rent if you choose. Gold does nothing while you hold it, so it cannot compound. A portfolio's growth comes from the assets that do produce and reinvest cash flow, and gold can only participate by being sold.

Second, **there is no valuation anchor.** The price of a bond can be reasoned about from its cash flows and prevailing rates; the price of a share can be reasoned about from its earnings. Gold has no such anchor. Its price is a function of demand for a non-yielding store of value — monetary conditions, real interest rates, currency expectations, official-sector buying and the broad appetite for safety. That does not make the price irrational; it makes it *unfixed*, which means the range of outcomes is wide and the confidence you can place in any forecast is low.

## The four lanes, applied to gold

This site insists that returns quoted in different registers must never be blended silently. Applying the discipline to gold is unusually clarifying, because two of the four lanes are empty.

- **Historical realised return.** The record exists, and it can be examined — but the number depends almost entirely on the window chosen, the currency of measurement and whether the series is nominal or inflation-adjusted. A window ending at a high flatters gold as much as it flatters equities. The packet for this article does not carry a gold return series, so no historical figure is quoted here; what is stated is the method for reading one critically.
- **Contractual yield.** There is none. Gold has no issuer and therefore no promise. This is the lane where deposits, bonds and annuities live, and gold simply is not in it.
- **Model-estimated expected return.** Weak for gold, because the standard machinery discounts cash flows and there are none to discount. Models of a non-yielding asset's expected return are essentially models of the real interest rate and of other investors' preferences, which is a far less settled exercise than pricing a bond.
- **Speculative upside.** This is where the substantive case lives — the belief that monetary conditions, currency debasement or a crisis will push gold higher. Stated as a belief, it is legitimate. Stated as an expectation, it is a category error.

The practical rule is blunt. If someone tells you gold will return a particular percentage, ask which lane that number came from. If the answer is "history", ask which window and which currency. If the answer is "the long run", ask what was happening to real interest rates in both the starting and ending periods. Usually the number does not survive the second question.

## What you own when you own gold

"Gold" is not one product, and the differences between the vehicles matter more than most buyers assume.

- **International spot metal** is quoted in US dollars per troy ounce. A troy ounce is the standard unit of the international market and equals 31.1034768 grams — appreciably heavier than the everyday ounce. Indian prices are conventionally quoted in rupees per ten grams, a conversion of the same quantity.
- **Domestic prices embed duty.** Gold and silver imports attract customs duty, including any applicable cess, as prescribed by the central government from time to time. That duty is one reason international and domestic prices do not move rupee-for-rupee, and it is adjusted in the budget and by notification.
- **Physical bars, coins and jewellery** sit at the end of a long chain of fabrication, purity and storage costs, and their resale value is generally computed on the metal content rather than on what you paid.
- **Exchange-traded gold funds** hold gold or gold-related instruments within a regulated mutual-fund structure, whose units are listed and tradeable, at the cost of annual expenses and, for some products, custody and insurance charges. Price discovery can deviate modestly from the underlying metal.
- **Sovereign gold bonds** are government securities denominated in grams of gold, issued on the sovereign's credit, paying a fixed rate of interest and redeemable on terms linked to the gold price. They avoid the storage and purity risks of physical metal, but they are securities with their own issue terms, interest treatment and secondary-market availability.

The same exposure therefore arrives with very different cost, purity, liquidity and tax profiles. The companion article on vehicle selection works the comparison; the point here is that "gold" is a family, not one instrument.

## The crisis-insurance argument, stated honestly

The serious case for holding gold is not that it will make you rich. It is that its price has, in some historical episodes, moved in a way that cushioned a portfolio when other assets fell — and that its value is not tied to the solvency of any government, bank or company.

That argument is a claim about *correlation under stress*, and it must be stated with its limits:

- Behaviour differs by the *kind* of stress. An episode in which the fear is inflation has a different effect on a non-yielding metal than an episode in which the fear is a scramble for cash, when investors may sell whatever is liquid.
- Correlation is *estimated from history*, and history has few genuinely independent stress episodes. The sample is small, so the confidence interval around any correlation claim is wide.
- Gold is *not uncorrelated with the world*. Its price responds to real interest rates, to the dollar and to official-sector demand, all of which move with the same macro forces that move other assets.

So the honest version of the insurance argument is: gold has sometimes helped, it is not contractually obliged to help, and the amount that is prudent rests on how much you are willing to pay for a hedge whose payoff is uncertain.

## The cost of holding gold: four bills

Here the distinction from a non-yielding asset becomes concrete. Costs are known with far more certainty than returns.

1. **Fabrication and making charges.** Converting bullion into jewellery costs a fabrication fee, quoted as a lump sum or as a percentage of the metal value depending on design and retailer. Making charges are typically not recovered on resale.
2. **Tax on the purchase.** The purchase price of jewellery includes the applicable tax, which is a real cost that never appears in a quoted gold price.
3. **Storage and insurance.** Either you pay a custodian, or you accept the risk of holding it yourself. Either way, there is a cost being incurred every year.
4. **Fund and product expenses.** A gold fund or exchange-traded product carries an annual expense, and some products charge separately for custody and insurance.
5. **The duty wedge.** Because imports attract duty, the domestic price is not simply the international price converted at the exchange rate. This is not a cost you can avoid by shopping, only a structural feature of the market.

Every one of these reduces the price rise required just to break even. The next section makes the arithmetic explicit.

## The tax position

Tax is the fourth bill, and it depends on the vehicle.

- **Physical gold and gold funds** produce gains that fall under the capital-gains rules applicable to the asset and the holding period, with the rate and holding-period threshold set by the law of the period. The relevant treatment depends on whether the gain is classified as short-term or long-term.
- **Sovereign gold bonds** pay a fixed rate of interest that is taxable as income, while the treatment of the gain on redemption is governed by the issue terms and the tax law, including any exemption specified for mature redemption. Whether a particular gain qualifies depends on the terms of that tranche and the law in force.
- **Jewellery** raises its own questions, both because the acquisition includes making charges and tax, and because disposal is typically at a rate that reflects the metal only.

This library does not improvise tax rules. The dated position — rates, holding periods, exemptions and the section references — belongs in the tax reference on this site, which is reviewed against the law and carries its own verification status. ## Worked example: the break-even arithmetic of physical gold

This is an illustrative calculation, not observed data, and the assumptions are hypothetical numbers chosen only to make the arithmetic visible. They are not market rates.

Suppose an investor buys a hallmarked item whose metal content is worth ₹100,000 at the prevailing rate. Assume a making charge equal to one-tenth of the metal value, a purchase tax of one-thirtieth of the subtotal, and a resale deduction of one-twentieth of the metal value for purity loss. Let the metal value be $M$, the making-charge fraction $m$, the purchase-tax fraction $g$ and the resale deduction $d$.

The break-even multiple of the metal price is:

$$k = \frac{(1+m)(1+g)}{1-d} = \frac{(1.10)(1.0333)}{0.95} = 1.196$$

So the metal price must rise by roughly **19.6%** before the round trip returns the original outlay. The calculation is illustrative and depends entirely on the assumed figures; what is structural rather than hypothetical is the direction — the purchase costs and the resale deduction both have to be overcome, and none of them produces any cash flow while the position is held.

The purpose of the exercise is not to predict a break-even, but to show that "gold went up" and "I made money on gold" are different statements.

## The speculative case, labelled

The case that gold will rise rests on a set of beliefs, each of which may be right or wrong. In compressed form:

- **Monetary debasement.** If the supply of money grows faster than the supply of goods and services, the real value of money falls, and an asset in fixed supply is a candidate refuge. The mechanisms are contested, and the timing has repeatedly defeated confident forecasts.
- **Real interest rates.** When the real return on cash and bonds is low or negative, the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset falls. This is the most mechanically defensible part of the case.
- **Currency and capital flows.** For an Indian investor the rupee price of gold has a second driver besides the dollar price, and rupee weakness can raise the local price even when the metal is flat internationally.
- **Official demand and the crisis bid.** Central-bank purchases and investor demand during stress episodes influence the price, but both are unpredictable in size and timing.

None of these is a promise, and none is falsifiable in the short run. Label the case for what it is: a speculative position in a price-only asset, sized so that being wrong is survivable.

## Where gold fits in an allocation

The allocator's question is not "is gold good?" but "what does adding it do to the whole?" Because a non-yielding asset behaves differently from a productive one, adding a small position can change a portfolio's return *dispersion* more than its average return. Whether that is worth doing depends on the investor's purpose — a hedge against a specific risk, a store of value for a specific liability, or simply a preference for holding something the banking system cannot create.

Three honest framing points:

- **Size it as a hedge, not a core.** An asset with no cash flow cannot fund a goal on its own; the cash-flowing assets have to carry that load.
- **Count the costs in the decision.** A hedge whose carrying cost and tax bill eat a meaningful part of its benefit is a worse hedge than the headline suggests.
- **Decide in advance what would make you sell.** Because there is no valuation anchor, the temptation to hold through any decline is strong. A stated rule — a rebalancing band, a fixed percentage — replaces the anchor that the asset does not provide.

## What this does not tell you

This article deliberately quotes no gold return, no storage cost, no making-charge rate, no tax rate and no duty level. It does not tell you what gold will do, whether it is "cheap" or "expensive", or how much of it any particular household should hold.

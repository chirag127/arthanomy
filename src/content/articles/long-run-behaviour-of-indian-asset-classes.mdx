Calling an asset class a long-run performer is a sentence with three missing pieces: which asset, which period, and which definition of long. Fill those in and the claim usually becomes narrower and more fragile than it sounded. This article is about the structure of long-horizon evidence for Indian asset classes — how much of it exists, what it can carry, and how an expectation should be built when the record itself is thin.

The aim is not cynicism. It is the opposite. Expectations built as ranges survive an unlucky decade; expectations built as a single confident number are abandoned the moment reality disagrees with them.

## Explain simply

Every long-run return number for equity, gold, property or debt is a summary of one stretch of history, measured one way. Change the start date, the end date, the index, or the treatment of dividends, costs and taxes, and the number changes. India's usable record is also short compared with the claims made from it, and parts of it are estimates rather than direct observations. None of this makes long-horizon investing unwise; it makes single-number promises unwise. The useful question is not what return to expect, but which returns a plan must be able to survive, and what makes some of them more plausible than others.

## What long run has to mean first

Three ambiguities hide inside the phrase.

The first is the window. A return measured over a horizon depends on both endpoints. Showing an overlapping series of many windows, rather than a single window, is the honest way to display long-run behaviour, because it shows the distribution of outcomes rather than the one outcome that happened.

The second is the asset. Equity here can mean a broad index, a subset of large companies, or a basket of funds; debt can mean short government paper, long government paper, or corporate credit; property can mean land, a self-occupied house, a rented flat, or a listed trust. These are not the same asset, and their records are not comparable.

The third is the measurement. Price return, total return with income reinvested, return net of costs, and return net of tax are four different objects. A comparison that sets a price index against a total-return series is not a comparison at all.

## The record is shorter than the question

Questions about the long run are usually questions about thirty or forty years: the length of a working life and of the compounding that a retirement corpus needs. India's continuous, tradeable and well-documented history for several asset classes is shorter than that, and the further back any series goes, the more of it is reconstructed rather than observed.

This matters in a specific statistical way. The number of non-overlapping thirty-year stretches inside a fifty-year record is one or two, not ten. With one or two independent observations there is no meaningful precision: the gap between a good and a bad assumption is far smaller than the uncertainty around either. A long-run average is therefore a description of one road, not a measured constant.

Two families of official series help, and they help differently. The Reserve Bank of India's statistical publications carry long-run series on savings composition, deposits and nominal versus real growth of financial assets — the context in which household returns are actually earned. Long-horizon academic series of real returns and valuation ratios offer a way to think about how the horizon changes the character of an outcome. Neither, used carelessly, produces a forecast.

## Survivorship: the market that is still here

Survivorship bias is easiest to see in individual companies. A list of today's large firms says nothing about the firms that failed, merged or were delisted along the way, so ranking the survivors by their realised returns flatters any strategy that happened to hold them.

The same bias operates at the level of markets, exchanges and instruments. A study of a market that still exists is, by construction, a study of a market that survived. Listed equity in India has a long and continuous existence, but that continuity is itself an outcome, not something that was visible in advance. Countries whose markets were closed, expropriated or restructured are absent from the sample that current expectations are drawn from, and their absence makes the surviving sample look steadier than the set of all possible histories.

There is a personal version too. The asset classes you are able to consider are the ones that did not disappear. Products that were mis-sold and collapsed quietly, deposit-takers that failed without a rescue, and schemes that were simply wound up leave gaps in collective memory while remaining in the loss column of the people who held them.

## Look-ahead bias: knowing the ending

Look-ahead bias is the use of information that would not have been available when a decision had to be made. It creeps into long-run narratives constantly.

A planner in any past year had to allocate without knowing which asset would lead the next decade, and had to choose a house, a job and a savings rate without knowing how inflation, interest rates and regulation would evolve. A reconstruction written today knows all of it. When such a reconstruction concludes that a particular allocation would have worked, it is measuring the past with today's information and calling the result a lesson.

The practical test is to ask what the decision-maker could actually have observed at the time: the valuations then prevailing, the rates then on offer, the rules then in force. Anything beyond that is hindsight wearing the costume of evidence.

## Data mining and the file drawer

Every long-run claim is a selection from many possible comparisons: many asset classes, many start and end dates, many measures, many sub-periods. Run enough comparisons and something will look impressive by chance alone. The findings that get published or repeated are, disproportionately, the ones that looked impressive; the ones that did not are the file drawer.

This is not a scandal about any particular study. It is a base-rate problem. If a hundred independent comparisons are made on noisy data and only the five best are reported, the reported five will look far more convincing than the underlying reality. The defence is not to abandon data but to demand discipline: was the relationship specified in advance, does it hold across markets and periods, and is there a mechanism that explains why it should exist at all?

## A single path is not a base rate

A base rate is the frequency of an outcome across many independent trials. A realised return series is one trial. Reporting the compound return of that one trial as the expectation for the future commits a quiet category error: it takes the path that happened and treats it as the centre of the distribution, when it may sit anywhere within it.

The base-rate question is answered differently: across the widest set of genuinely comparable situations, how often did an allocation held for this long produce at least this much? That question needs many situations, not one history, and its answer is a frequency, not a rate.

## Regime dependence

Any historical average is conditional on the environment that produced it. Interest rates sat at particular levels; inflation sat at particular levels; valuations began at particular levels; the rules taxed and regulated particular instruments in particular ways. None of these is a permanent feature of the economy. When one of them changes — and they do change, sometimes abruptly — the average computed under the old regime stops describing the new one.

The practical implication is that an assumption should be tied to the observable conditions that plausibly generate it, not merely to the fact that it was true in the past. A statement such as equity delivered a certain return in a period when starting valuations were low and rates were falling is a conditional statement. With the conditions removed, it is a slogan.

## Worked example: same endpoint, different journeys

Suppose, purely as invented arithmetic, an asset returns fifty per cent in one year and a fifty per cent loss in the next. Starting from 100, multiply by 1.5 to reach 150, then by 0.5 to reach 75. Over the two years the compound return is

$$ \sqrt{1.50 \times 0.50} - 1 = \sqrt{0.75} - 1 \approx -13.4\% $$

a year, even though the simple average of the two annual returns is zero. Reverse the order — a fifty per cent loss, then a fifty per cent gain — and the endpoint is identical, because multiplication does not care about order. Two very different journeys produce one identical summary.

Extend it: a third year of about a third of a gain takes 75 back to 100, and the three-year compound return is zero. A headline reporting no return over three years would be compatible with a collapse and a recovery, with a gentle drift, or with any path in between. The endpoint cannot tell them apart, and the endpoint is usually all that is reported.

The same arithmetic disciplines a range. Suppose an analyst claims a real return of between two and six per cent a year for thirty years. As invented arithmetic, one rupee grows to about

$$ 1.02^{30} \approx 1.81 \quad \text{and} \quad 1.06^{30} \approx 5.74 $$

so the claim spans a difference of more than three times in terminal purchasing power. The width is the honest part of the statement; the midpoint is the part that gets quoted. Every figure in this section is invented arithmetic, not observed market data and not a forecast.

## What the long-run record does require to be true

Strip away the numbers and something useful remains. Whatever the realised figures turn out to be, several things must hold for long-run returns to be positive at all, and these are structural rather than statistical.

First, a real return has to be paid for. The return on equity comes from profits; the return on debt comes from interest; the return on property comes from rent, or from someone else's willingness to pay more later. Over long horizons, a claim that an asset class returns more than the economy can generate has to name where the money comes from.

Second, a premium over the safe rate has to be earned by bearing something. That something is uncertainty: about profits, about inflation, about liquidity, about being forced to sell. The mean-variance framing of portfolio choice is built on exactly this point — that risk which cannot be diversified away is what the market compensates, while risk that can be removed earns nothing extra. A high expected return without a corresponding exposure is either a real mispricing, which is rare and quickly competed away, or a misunderstanding.

Third, costs and taxes are closer to certainties than returns are. Expense ratios, brokerage, transaction levies, and tax on income and gains accrue in nearly every outcome, good or bad. A long-run plan can treat them as given even while it treats returns as a range.

Fourth, the ability to stay invested is a precondition, not a detail. Whatever the average return over forty years, an investor forced to sell in year eight receives the year-eight price. The long-run number is available only to those who are still present at the long-run end.

## What this does not tell you

This article deliberately quotes no historical return, index level, inflation rate or growth figure, because a single quoted number would contradict its own argument. It also does not tell you which asset class will do best, and it cannot: that is precisely the question the record is too short and too conditional to answer.

It does not say that long-run averages are useless. They are the correct way to summarise what happened over a stated window, and they are indispensable for comparing one window with another. The error lies in the verb: a historical average describes, it does not predict.

It does not resolve the choice of benchmark. Whether the honest comparison for an Indian investor is a domestic index, a global basket, or a blended measure is itself contested, and the answer changes the numbers without reducing the uncertainty.

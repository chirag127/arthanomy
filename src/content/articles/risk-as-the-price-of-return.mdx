A return is a price. When an investor receives an unusually high one, somebody is paying it, and the ordinary reason is that the thing being bought is unpleasant to hold. That is the least glamorous idea in finance and the most load-bearing: expected return is the compensation for bearing risk, and where no risk is visible, the compensation is usually being paid for something the buyer cannot yet see.

This article takes that proposition seriously and then qualifies it. Some risks are compensated and some are not; some are measurable and some are not; and a risk that can be removed by holding something else is, in the ordinary course, not paid for at all. It closes with the arithmetic that makes risk expensive even when the average outcome is unchanged, and with an honest account of what a risk premium does not promise.

## Explain simply

If you want a bigger return, you generally have to accept the chance of a worse outcome. Someone has to hold the assets other people do not want to hold, and the extra return is the payment for standing there. That payment is not a guarantee; it is what the market requires in advance for taking the chance. The useful skill is not picking the asset with the highest number. It is working out what could go wrong, deciding whether anyone is actually paying you to carry that particular risk, and noticing when a high return is offered with no visible risk at all, which usually means the risk has been left out of the description rather than out of the deal.

## The proposition, stated precisely

The idea can be written down. A default-free short rate, the return available for merely waiting, is the base line. Anything above it is a premium for something:

$$ E[R] = R_f + \text{compensation for risk} $$

In the simplest version, compensation scales with the volatility of the return:

$$ E[R] = R_f + \lambda \sigma $$

where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of returns and $\lambda$ is the price the market puts on a unit of that volatility. Three properties of this expression matter, and all three are easily forgotten.

It is an **expectation**, formed before the outcome. Nothing in it obliges the market to deliver.

It is a **premium, not a rate**. The risk-free part is a rate; the rest is a price for uncertainty, and prices change.

It says nothing about **which** risks are being paid for. That is the part that needs the most care, and it is the subject of the next section.

## Risk is not one thing

The word does too much work. When an offer is described as risky, the description is usually incomplete, because at least seven distinct risks behave differently and are compensated differently.

| Risk | What it is | Normally compensated? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market or systematic | The whole market falls, whatever you hold | Yes, in expectation, because it cannot be diversified away |
| Specific or idiosyncratic | A single asset, tenant, project or issuer fails | Usually not, because it can be diversified away |
| Credit | A borrower or counterparty does not pay as promised | Yes, if the credit risk is genuinely priced |
| Liquidity | You cannot sell, or can only sell at a discount | Sometimes, and not reliably |
| Leverage | Borrowing magnifies both gains and losses and can force selling | No; it scales a position rather than earning a premium |
| Concentration | Too much depends on one asset, employer or region | No, if the concentration was avoidable |
| Governance and fraud | Those running the arrangement act against your interest | No; it destroys value rather than commanding a premium |

The table is a classification exercise, not a valuation. Its purpose is to stop a single word from carrying seven different meanings, because the question that follows — is anyone paying me for this? — has a different answer for each row.

## Where risk is not compensated

The most important row in that table is the second. Risk that could have been removed by holding something else is, in the ordinary working of markets, not paid for. If an investor can eliminate a risk simply by diversifying, then the market has no reason to offer extra expected return for carrying it, and an investor who carries it anyway has enlarged the spread of outcomes without raising the average.

Three consequences follow, and they are practical.

- **Concentration is a choice, not a premium.** Holding a large position in a single employer, a single property or a single borrower is not rewarded with a higher expected rate; it is rewarded with a wider distribution of results, which is a different thing.
- **Borrowing does not create expected return.** Leverage multiplies exposure to whatever is already there. It raises the volatility and can force a sale at the worst moment, but it does not add a premium that the unlevered asset did not already carry.
- **Governance and fraud are never compensated.** There is no premium on offer for the risk that a manager, a sponsor or a promoter will act against the investor. The return exists in the hope that they will not, and when they do, losses are realised rather than priced in advance.

The distinction between compensated and uncompensated risk is the difference between being paid to be uncomfortable and simply being exposed. A portfolio can be made considerably safer without giving up any expected return, by removing the second kind.

## Risk versus uncertainty

A second distinction is narrower and more uncomfortable. Risk, in the useful sense, is exposure to a range of outcomes whose shape can be described at least approximately: historical data, a contract, a distribution of possible defaults. Uncertainty is different. It is exposure to outcomes whose range cannot be described at all — a new regulation, a change in the rules for a concession, a business model that has no precedent, or a counterparty whose commitments cannot be verified.

The practical difference is what you can do with each. Risk can be sized, priced and diversified against. Uncertainty can only be avoided, insured, or borne with humility. Offers that dress uncertainty in the language of risk — a probability, an average, a track record — are borrowing the vocabulary of measurement for a situation that does not support it. A long and clean history is evidence about risk; it is not evidence about the arrival of something unprecedented. When a claim rests on an average that could not have captured the scenario that would hurt most, the average is not reassurance.

## Measuring the price: reward per unit of risk

Because expected returns are not observable and risk comes in several kinds, practitioners need a single comparison. The standard one divides the excess return of a portfolio over the risk-free rate by its volatility, giving a **reward-to-variability ratio**:

$$ S = \frac{\bar{R}_p - R_f}{\sigma_p} $$

where the portfolio average return is measured against $R_f$, the risk-free rate, and divided by $\sigma_p$, the standard deviation of the portfolio returns. The ratio was introduced in this form precisely as a way of ranking portfolios, and the original treatment was explicit about its caveats. It is a **relative** device: it ranks portfolios over the same period and against the same reference rate. It is sensitive to the period chosen, because an average return and a volatility both depend on the window. It presumes that volatility is an adequate summary of risk, which it is not for strategies whose losses arrive in rare, large events rather than as continuous scatter. And it is silent about direction: a fund with a negative average return produces a negative ratio, which tells you about its performance and not about its risk.

Used with those caveats, the ratio is genuinely useful. It answers one question: for each unit of movement I had to endure, how much return above the risk-free rate did I receive? Used without them, it becomes a competition for a single number, which is exactly the error the earlier articles in this series describe.

## Worked example: two portfolios, same average, different risk

The figures below are illustrative. They contain no historical returns, no fund records and no forecasts; they exist to show the arithmetic.

Assume a risk-free rate of six per cent. Portfolio A has an expected arithmetic average return of ten per cent and a volatility of five per cent. Portfolio B has the same expected arithmetic average of ten per cent and a volatility of twenty-five per cent.

Using the approximation that compound growth is roughly the arithmetic average minus half the variance,

$$ g \approx \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} $$

Portfolio A compounds at approximately $10\% - (0.05^2 / 2) = 9.88\%$ per year, while Portfolio B compounds at approximately $10\% - (0.25^2 / 2) = 6.88\%$ per year. The arithmetic average is identical; the growth of wealth is not. Over a thirty-year horizon, one rupee becomes roughly:

| Portfolio | Arithmetic average | Volatility | Approximate compound growth | Illustrative value after 30 years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 10% | 5% | ≈ 9.88% | ≈ ₹16.90 |
| B | 10% | 25% | ≈ 6.88% | ≈ ₹7.35 |

That gap is the practical cost of risk, and it is not a cost that appears in the average. The same arithmetic explains why promised returns are so often quoted as arithmetic averages: the higher-volatility path looks equally attractive in that form and is materially worse in wealth.

Now turn the comparison into a price. Suppose Portfolio A offers a seven per cent expected return with five per cent volatility, and Portfolio B offers eleven per cent with eighteen per cent volatility, against a six per cent risk-free rate.

$$ S_A = \frac{7\% - 6\%}{5\%} = 0.20 \qquad S_B = \frac{11\% - 6\%}{18\%} = 0.278 $$

In this illustration the higher-risk portfolio is the better-paid per unit of risk, even though it is the one more likely to show a loss in a given year. The reverse is equally possible: a low-volatility portfolio can deliver more reward per unit of risk, in which case the apparent safety was not costing anything. The ratio does not tell you which portfolio is right for anyone; it tells you which one paid more per unit of discomfort over the period measured.

## Why a safe high return invites suspicion

Put the pieces together and a simple heuristic follows. A return is a price for risk. If a return is high and the risk is genuinely low, that combination is a bargain, and bargains of that kind are competed away. So when an offer presents a high return alongside an assurance of safety, the most economical explanation is that the risk has been left out of the description. Four explanations recur.

1. **The risk is hidden, not absent.** Liquidity that does not exist until you need it, credit to a counterparty whose obligations are unenforceable, or a guarantee from an entity with no assets behind it.
2. **The return is projected, not contractual.** A model estimate or an illustration is presented in the vocabulary of a promise. The first article in this series separated those lanes for exactly this reason.
3. **The safety claim is about a different thing than the return.** Nominal safety in rupees is not purchasing-power safety, and a guaranteed nominal return can be a guaranteed real loss.
4. **It is not an investment at all.** Opaque structures that pay earlier participants out of later ones eventually stop paying, which is the subject of the fraud series rather than this one.

None of these is proven by a high number. But a high number with no visible risk is a request to look harder, and the classification work in the table above is what to look with.

## What a risk premium does not promise

This is the section that has to be stated plainly, because the arithmetic of the previous section is easy to read as an assurance.

- **Bearing risk does not oblige the market to pay you.** A premium is an expectation formed in advance. It can be earned, partly earned, or not earned at all, and the investor who bears risk discovers which only afterwards.
- **Volatility reduces compound growth even when the average does not change.** As the worked example shows, two portfolios with identical arithmetic averages compound to very different wealth, and the difference is not recoverable by patience.
- **Compounding does not promise to repair a loss.** A fall of fifty per cent requires a gain of a hundred per cent to return to the starting point. The mathematics of recovery is asymmetric in the direction that hurts.
- **A ratio does not travel.** A reward-to-variability figure computed over one period does not carry over to the next, and a portfolio that looks well compensated in a rising market may look poorly compensated in a falling one.
- **Safety in rupees is not safety in purchasing power.** An instrument can be certain in nominal terms and still lose real value, as the inflation article in this series sets out.

The honest summary is symmetrical. Expected return is paid for risk, which means a higher expected return is not a gift and a lower one is not a failure; and the work of investing is largely the work of identifying which risks are being paid for, which are being avoided at a cost, and which are simply being taken for nothing.

## Open questions

- If volatility reduces compound growth even when the arithmetic average is unchanged, how should an investor weigh a genuinely higher expected return against the growth it costs to hold it?
- Can governance and fraud risk ever be identified before a loss, given that by construction they carry no premium and no distribution of past outcomes?
- When an offer presents a high return and a strong counterparty, what specific evidence would distinguish a genuine mispricing from a hidden liquidity, credit or opacity risk?
- Is the reward-to-variability ratio still the right comparison now that losses increasingly arrive in rare, large events, or does that shift make the measure systematically reassuring?

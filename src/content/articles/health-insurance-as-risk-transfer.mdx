Health insurance feels like the simplest purchase in personal finance: pay a premium, get treatment paid for. In practice it is an indemnity contract with a ceiling and a long list of conditions attached to that ceiling. The insurer does not promise to pay a fixed amount; it promises to reimburse the cost actually incurred, up to the sum insured and after the policy's limits, sub-limits, exclusions and waiting periods have been applied. Two policies with the same headline cover can produce very different payouts for the same hospital bill, and the difference is entirely in the structure. This article sets out that structure — what an indemnity policy actually promises, and what decides whether a claim is paid in full, in part, or not at all.

## Indemnity versus fixed benefit

The first distinction is between two ways a health contract can pay.

- **Indemnity.** The insurer reimburses the covered medical expenses actually incurred, up to the sum insured and subject to the policy's limits. The payout is measured by the bill, not by the diagnosis. This is the dominant design for hospitalisation cover.
- **Fixed benefit.** The insurer pays a stated amount when a defined event occurs, regardless of what the treatment cost. A hospital-cash policy pays a daily amount during a hospital stay; a critical-illness policy pays a lump sum on diagnosis. The payout is measured by the event, not by the bill.

Most household health cover is indemnity, and the rest of this article is about indemnity design, because that is where the gap between expectation and payout is created. The reader who expects a fixed benefit from an indemnity policy will be disappointed, and the disappointment will arrive during a hospitalisation.

## What the sum insured means in an indemnity policy

The sum insured is a **ceiling on the insurer's liability for the policy period**, not a promise to pay that amount. What the policy pays is the **admissible claim**: the covered expenses, minus everything the policy disallows or shares.

Four things stand between the hospital bill and the amount received.

1. **Non-payable items.** Certain costs — some consumables, administrative charges and items specified in the policy — are excluded.
2. **Sub-limits.** Caps on specific treatments, procedures or categories, which can be far below the overall sum insured.
3. **Co-payment.** A stated share of the admissible claim that the policyholder bears, sometimes applied only to certain ages or conditions.
4. **Waiting periods and exclusions.** Which remove treatment from cover entirely, either for a time or permanently.

The sum insured matters, but it only sets the boundary. Inside the boundary, the payout is decided by the items above. A policy with a high sum insured and strict sub-limits can pay less than a policy with a lower sum insured and fewer limits.

## Room-rent limits and proportionate deduction

The room-rent limit is the most consequential single clause in many health policies, and its effect is widely underestimated because it does not look like a limit on the whole claim. It usually reads as a cap on the daily room charge, expressed either as a percentage of the sum insured or as an absolute amount.

The damage is done by the **proportionate deduction** that often accompanies it. If the room rent exceeds the eligible limit, the policy does not simply disallow the excess rent. It treats the excess as evidence that the entire treatment was more expensive than the policy intended to cover, and it reduces the **associated medical expenses** — surgeon fees, nursing, operation-theatre charges, investigations — in the same proportion that the actual room rent bears to the eligible room rent.

The arithmetic is stark, and it is set out in the worked example below. The practical rule is that the room-rent limit is not a limit on the room; it is a lever on the whole claim, and its true cost is several times the excess rent itself.

## Sub-limits and co-payment

Beyond the room-rent clause, two other mechanisms shrink a claim.

- **Sub-limits** cap particular treatments or categories. A policy may place a lower ceiling on a specific procedure, or on a category such as modern treatments, than on the overall cover. A claim for a capped treatment is limited by the sub-limit even when the sum insured is far from exhausted.
- **Co-payment** is a stated percentage the policyholder must bear. It may apply to the whole claim, or only to claims above a certain age, or only to treatment at non-network hospitals. Co-payment converts a portion of the loss back onto the household, which is precisely what an indemnity cap does.

Both are legitimate levers for pricing risk, and both should be read from the policy rather than assumed. The question to ask is not "what is my sum insured?" but "what will this policy pay for the treatment I am most likely to need?"

## Waiting periods

Waiting periods remove cover for a defined time, and they exist in three forms.

- **The initial waiting period.** A period at the start of the policy during which most treatment is not covered, apart from accidents, which are typically covered from the outset.
- **Specific-disease waiting periods.** Longer periods attached to named conditions, so that treatment for those conditions is not covered until the period has elapsed.
- **Pre-existing-disease waiting periods.** A period during which treatment related to a pre-existing condition is excluded, after which it may become covered.

The definitions here have the same problem described in the article on disclosure and claim denial: the policy's definition of a pre-existing condition, and the conditions attached to any waiting period, decide the outcome. A condition that the policyholder regarded as minor, or as fully resolved, may still fall within the definition. Waiting periods are a major reason the same policy can feel generous after several years and inadequate in the first year.

## Cashless and reimbursement mechanics

Health policies settle claims in one of two ways, and the distinction is about mechanics rather than cover.

- **Cashless settlement.** At a hospital in the insurer's network, the insurer settles the admissible amount directly with the hospital, and the policyholder pays the non-admissible portion. The admissibility tests — limits, sub-limits, non-payables, co-payment — apply exactly as they would otherwise.
- **Reimbursement.** The policyholder pays the hospital and claims the admissible amount back, submitting the prescribed documents. For treatment outside the network, or where cashless approval is not obtained, this is the usual route.

Neither route changes what is covered. A common misreading is that cashless means "fully paid"; it does not. It means the insurer pays directly, up to the admissible amount, and the household settles the rest. The documents, the pre-authorisation requirements and the time limits for a reimbursement claim are set by the policy and the insurer's process, and they need the same attention as the coverage clauses.

## Explain simply

Health insurance pays for treatment, but it pays up to a limit and after a set of rules. The big one is the room-rent limit, which can quietly reduce the whole bill, not just the room charge, if you stay in a more expensive room than the policy allows. Then there are sub-limits on particular treatments, a co-payment share you always bear, and waiting periods before certain conditions are covered. The sum insured is only the outer boundary; what you actually get depends on how the policy treats each part of the bill. Compare policies on those rules, not on the headline cover alone.

## Worked example: how a room-rent limit shrinks a claim

An illustrative calculation of one common design, not a term of any product. Sum insured ₹5,00,000. The policy's room-rent limit is stated as a daily cap, and for this illustration it equals ₹5,000 a day. The policy applies a proportionate deduction to associated expenses when the actual room rent exceeds the cap.

The hospitalisation runs for five days. The actual room rent is ₹10,000 a day, so the room-rent component of the bill is ₹50,000 and the associated medical expenses — surgeon fees, nursing, operation theatre, investigations — are ₹3,00,000. The total bill is ₹3,50,000.

The policy's treatment is as follows.

- The eligible room rent is ₹5,000 a day for five days, or ₹25,000. The excess room rent of ₹25,000 is disallowed.
- The proportionate deduction ratio is the eligible room rent divided by the actual room rent: ₹25,000 divided by ₹50,000, which is one half.
- The associated expenses of ₹3,00,000 are reduced in the same proportion, to ₹1,50,000.
- The admissible claim is therefore ₹25,000 plus ₹1,50,000, or ₹1,75,000.

The household bears ₹1,75,000 of a ₹3,50,000 bill under this illustrative policy, even though the sum insured of ₹5,00,000 was never reached. Choosing a room that cost ₹5,000 more a day than the cap added ₹25,000 to the room-rent bill but ₹1,75,000 to the amount retained. The method used here is one common design; a specific policy's method must be read from its own wording, because some policies apply proportionate deduction differently or not at all.

## Comparing policies on substance rather than premium

The comparison that matters is not premium against premium, or sum insured against sum insured. It is a line-by-line reading of what each policy does to a realistic bill.

| Feature | The question to ask | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sum insured | Is it enough for a realistic hospitalisation? | It is the outer ceiling only |
| Room-rent limit | Is it a percentage or an absolute amount, and is there a proportionate deduction? | It can reduce the whole claim |
| Sub-limits | Which treatments are capped, and at what level? | A capped treatment is limited regardless of the sum insured |
| Co-payment | What share is borne, and does it vary by age or hospital? | It reduces every eligible claim |
| Waiting periods | How long for initial, specific and pre-existing conditions? | It removes cover at the start and for named conditions |
| Non-payables | What is excluded from the admissible amount? | It shrinks the claim before limits apply |
| Settlement | Network access and the reimbursement process | It determines how quickly the money arrives |

Reading down this list for two candidate policies is the discipline the brief asks for. It is slower than comparing premiums, and it is the only comparison that reflects what happens when a claim is made.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes how indemnity health policies are structured and works one illustrative claim on assumed limits. It does not state the terms of any product, does not recommend any policy or insurer, and does not predict what any particular policy would pay. Room-rent limits, sub-limits, co-payment levels and waiting periods are product-specific, change with product design and regulation, and must be read from the policy document in force.

The interaction between health cover and other protection — critical-illness riders, hospital-cash policies, employer group cover — is addressed elsewhere in this series, and each of those contracts has its own definitions. Tax treatment of premiums and reimbursements is governed by the Income-tax Act as amended by successive Finance Acts and is deliberately not restated here. The formal route for a dispute about a claim remains the insurer's grievance process and, where the matter is one of mis-selling or deficiency in service, the consumer commissions under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.

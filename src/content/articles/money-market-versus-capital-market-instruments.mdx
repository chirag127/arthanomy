A household with money it will not need for three months and a household with money it will not need for ten years are making different decisions, even if both are described as investing. One is parking cash; the other is committing capital. The market draws the same distinction, and it draws it with a single line: instruments that repay within a year sit on one shelf, and instruments that repay later sit on another. The shelves have different instruments, different conventions and different risks. This article explains where the line is, what sits on each side of it, and how to map a surplus to the right shelf rather than to the highest quoted number.

## What separates the two markets

The money market is the market for short-dated debt. It exists so that institutions and, indirectly, individuals with temporary cash can lend it for days or months to those who need it briefly, with the money returned on a known date. The capital market for debt is the market for longer obligations, where the borrower commits to periodic interest and a redemption years away, and the lender accepts that the value of the commitment will move with interest rates before it matures.

The dividing principle is horizon, not size and not credit quality. A very large short borrowing is money-market; a very small long bond is capital-market. What differs is the risk the holder is taking. On the short shelf the dominant risks are credit and rollover: will the borrower repay at maturity, and at what rate can the money be redeployed afterwards? On the long shelf the dominant risk is price: what will the obligation be worth if the holder has to sell before maturity, given that yields will have moved?

## The one-year line

The convention is that the money market covers instruments with an original maturity of up to one year, and the capital market covers longer ones. For some instruments the boundary is not merely conventional but written into the regulatory framework, because the instrument exists under specific directions. For others, such as call and repo money, the maturity is by definition overnight or very short, so the boundary is not in question. Where a regulator sets a maximum maturity for a specific instrument, that limit is a matter of current directions and should be confirmed before it is relied on rather than recalled.

The line is a convention about *original* maturity. A ten-year bond with nine months left to run is not thereby a money-market instrument; it is a capital-market security that happens to be near its redemption and whose price is now relatively insensitive to yield changes. That distinction matters, because the instruments on the two shelves are governed by different quoting conventions even when they mature on the same day.

## The money-market shelf

- **Treasury bills.** Short-dated obligations of the Central Government that pay nothing until maturity. They are issued at a discount to face value and repay face value, so the return is the difference between the two. Because the issuer is the sovereign, they are the domestic reference for a short-term risk-free rupee return, and retail investors can hold them directly.
- **Commercial paper.** An unsecured short-dated promissory obligation issued by a corporate borrower, typically at a discount. The absence of collateral is why the creditworthiness of the issuer, and any credit enhancement, does most of the work in pricing it.
- **Certificates of deposit.** Negotiable short-dated obligations issued by banks. They are bank liabilities, distinct from an ordinary term deposit because they are tradeable, and their treatment for deposit-insurance purposes is a specific question that should be checked against the deposit-insurance framework rather than assumed.
- **Call and repo money.** Very short collateralised or uncollateralised interbank lending, where the repo variety is a secured borrowing against securities and the call variety is typically unsecured. This is where the policy rate transmits most directly.

## The capital-market shelf

Above one year the instruments carry coupons and trade at prices that move. Dated government securities are the sovereign benchmark; state development loans are the state-level equivalent; corporate bonds and debentures are the obligations of companies, listed and disclosed under the securities framework. A debenture issued under a trust deed, listed on an exchange and rated by a credit rating agency is a capital-market instrument in every respect, including the disclosure obligations that come with the label.

The shelf's defining feature is not duration but exposure. Because the cash flows are distant, a holder is exposed to the level of yields at every moment before maturity, and a sale at the wrong moment converts a temporary price movement into a realised gain or loss. The coupon is contractual; the price is not.

## Explain simply

The money market is the short shelf and the capital market is the long shelf. On the short shelf, you lend for a few weeks or months and the borrower gives the money back on a date you already know, so what the money earns is largely settled in advance. On the long shelf, you buy a promise to pay interest for years and to return the principal at the end. The promise is firm, but the price of the promise moves around, so the value of the position on any day before maturity is uncertain. The shelves are not ranked. They answer different questions: the short shelf answers where to keep money that is due soon, and the long shelf answers where to put money that is not needed for a long time.

## Where the boundary is a convention and where it is a rule

It is worth separating the two kinds of boundary, because only one of them can be looked up.

Where an instrument exists under specific directions, its eligible maturity, eligibility criteria and issuance mechanics are set out in that framework, and the boundary is a rule. Where an instrument is simply a bond issued in the market, the one-year line is a convention of vocabulary: the market calls a nine-month instrument short and a fifteen-month instrument long because the first behaves like cash and the second behaves like a bond. A holder should not treat the vocabulary as if it were a rule, and should not treat a rule as if it were merely vocabulary. When a figure matters for a decision, it belongs to the current framework and the current issuance calendar, not to a remembered summary.

## The conventions differ, and that is why the numbers are not comparable

Two quibbles make cross-shelf comparison harder than it looks.

The first is the quoting convention. Short instruments are frequently quoted on a discount basis or on simple interest, while bonds are quoted on a compounding yield to maturity. A quoted discount rate is calculated against face value, and it is not the same as the return earned on the price actually paid. Comparing a discount rate with a bond yield without converting them is comparing two different quantities.

The second is the day-count and compounding basis used to annualise a short return. Short instruments may be annualised on different day-count bases, and the choice changes the annual figure even when the cash amounts are identical. Both conventions are defined in the relevant market practice and should be taken from the framework rather than inferred.

## Matching an instrument to a horizon

The useful discipline is to work backwards from the date the money is needed.

1. Write down the date the cash is required and the amount, net of anything already committed.
2. Identify instruments that mature on or before that date, so that the position does not have to be sold to raise the cash.
3. Check the credit of the borrower on each candidate, since short instruments still default.
4. Compare the converted, like-for-like returns on the same convention, after any fees and after tax, rather than comparing headline quotes.
5. Only if the horizon is genuinely long should the longer shelf be considered, and then the holder should be able to hold to maturity or to tolerate a sale in a bad market.

A ladder that places principal to return each year reduces the reinvestment-timing problem; a single long bond concentrates it. Neither is wrong in the abstract, but only one of them matches a short horizon.

## Worked example

The following is a constructed arithmetic illustration, not a quotation and not a forecast. Take ₹5,00,000 with a 91-day horizon, and suppose two hypothetical placements are available.

**A short instrument** priced on a simple-interest basis at 6.50 per cent per annum for 91 days on a 365-day year. The maturity value is:

$$ 500000 \times (1 + 0.065 \times 91 / 365) = ₹5,08,102.74 $$

The gain is ₹8,102.74, and it is essentially certain if the borrower is the sovereign and the instrument is held to its 91-day maturity.

**A long bond**, with a 7.00 per cent coupon, bought at a yield of 7.00 per cent, and sold after 91 days. The interest accrued over 91 days is:

$$ 500000 \times 0.07 \times 91 / 365 = ₹8,726.03 $$

which is slightly more than the short instrument earned. Now suppose that, over those 91 days, the yield rises by one percentage point. If the bond's modified duration is about seven years, the first-order price change is about 7 per cent of price, or roughly ₹35,000 on ₹5,00,000:

$$ \Delta P \approx -P \times D_{Mod} \times \Delta y = -500000 \times 7.0 \times 0.01 = -₹35,000 $$

So the same money on the long shelf earns a little more interest and bears a price swing several times larger than that interest. The point is not that the bond is a bad instrument. The point is that the bond is answering a different question. Over a 91-day horizon the short instrument delivers a known amount, while the bond delivers a distribution that includes this possibility. The estimate above is a model-estimated, first-order figure; it ignores convexity and assumes a parallel move in the yield curve, and the true realised outcome would depend on the whole curve and on the exact exit date.

## Credit risk does not disappear on the short shelf

The most common error in this comparison is to read "short" as "safe". Shortness removes interest-rate risk for a holder who waits to maturity; it does not remove the risk that the borrower does not pay. A Treasury bill is an obligation of the sovereign and is treated as the risk-free short anchor. Commercial paper is an unsecured corporate obligation, so its credit is the whole question, and unsecured short paper has defaulted in stress episodes. A certificate of deposit is a bank obligation whose risk depends on the issuing bank. Each instrument's credit sits with a different borrower, and only the sovereign instrument carries the sovereign's standing.

## The four lanes

- **Contractual.** The discount, the coupon and the redemption amount are fixed by the terms of the instrument; holding to maturity delivers them.
- **Historical realised.** Past returns on either shelf depended on the entry yield, the exit price and the reinvestment of any coupons, and are not a guide to the next period.
- **Model-estimated.** The change in the long bond's value in the worked example is a first-order estimate from an assumed yield move, not an observed outcome.
- **Speculative.** Any statement about where short rates or long yields will be at a future date is a forecast and should be labelled as one.

## What this does not tell you

- **Current rates or limits.** No live rate, tenor or regulatory threshold is stated here. Take those from the official framework and the current issuance calendar.
- **Tax.** The tax treatment of discount income, interest and any capital gain differs by instrument and belongs to the tax reference.
- **Liquidity in a stress episode.** Whether the short shelf stays tradeable for a small holder during a squeeze is an empirical question that averages do not answer.
- **Deposit-insurance treatment.** Whether a negotiable deposit instrument is covered, and to what extent, is set by the deposit-insurance framework and should be checked there.

## Open questions

- For a one-to-three-year horizon, is the yield premium on a longer instrument ever large enough to compensate for the probability that the money is needed earlier than planned?
- How much of a quoted discount rate on a short instrument is genuine return and how much is the quoting convention speaking?
- In a funding squeeze, does liquidity remain available down the shelf to a small holder, or does it retreat to the sovereign benchmark alone?

The two shelves are not a ranking. They are a map of time, and the map exists to keep a cash-management decision from being mistaken for an investment decision. Choosing the shelf that matures when the money is needed is most of the work; the rest is reading the convention carefully enough that the two shelves can be compared at all.

A gold loan is a secured loan in which the borrower pledges gold ornaments — typically jewellery — to a lender in exchange for funds. The lender holds the gold as security for the duration of the loan and returns it when the loan is repaid. If the loan is not repaid, the lender has a mechanism to recover the outstanding amount by selling the pledged gold.

Gold loans are one of the oldest forms of secured lending in India and remain widely used, particularly by households that own jewellery but need short-term funds. The market includes banks and non-banking financial companies (NBFCs), both of which operate under the regulatory framework set by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). This article explains how the mechanics work: how the gold is assessed, how the loan amount is determined, how interest is computed, and what happens when a loan goes bad.

## What a gold loan is

A gold loan is a secured, short-term loan backed by physical gold ornaments. The borrower delivers possession of the ornaments to the lender — or to a custodian acting for the lender — and receives a loan amount that is a fraction of the gold's assessed value. The loan typically has a fixed tenure, ranging from a few months to a few years, and the borrower repays the principal plus interest either at maturity or through periodic payments.

The key parties are the borrower, the lender (a bank or NBFC) and, in some cases, a custodian who holds the gold. The RBI's master directions on lending govern the conduct of these loans, and KYC requirements apply to all borrowers. NBFC gold lenders additionally operate under the NBFC regulatory framework, which includes specific norms on lending practices and borrower protection.

## How lenders assess the gold

Before granting a gold loan, the lender assesses the pledged ornaments to determine their value. The assessment has two components:

1. **Purity.** The fineness of the gold determines how much pure metal the ornament contains. A hallmark — the purity mark applied under the Bureau of Indian Standards regime — provides a statement about the gold's purity to a declared grade. Lenders may test the purity themselves or rely on the hallmark, depending on their procedures and the value of the pledge.
2. **Weight.** The weight of the ornament, measured in grams, is multiplied by the prevailing price of gold at the assessed purity to arrive at the metal value.

The assessed value is typically based on the metal content only. Making charges — the fabrication fee that was paid when the jewellery was purchased — are not recovered on resale and are generally not counted in a gold loan assessment. This is an important distinction: the amount a borrower can raise against an ornament is based on the value of the gold in it, not the price that was originally paid for it.

## Loan-to-value: how much you can borrow

The loan amount is determined by applying a loan-to-value (LTV) ratio to the assessed value of the gold. The LTV ratio is the fraction of the assessed value that the lender is willing to advance. For example, if the assessed value of the pledged gold is ₹100,000 and the lender applies an LTV of 75%, the loan amount would be ₹75,000.

The specific LTV cap is set by RBI master directions on lending and may vary by lender type and by the regulatory framework in force. The exact cap is not reproduced in this packet and must be verified against current RBI directions. What is structural is the logic: the lender advances less than the assessed value to create a buffer against a fall in the gold price over the loan tenure. If the gold price falls, the pledged metal may no longer cover the outstanding loan, and the buffer protects the lender.

The LTV also has a time dimension. As interest accumulates on the loan, the outstanding balance rises while the value of the collateral may stay flat or fall. A loan that was well-covered at origination may become under-covered by the end of the tenure, which is one reason lenders prefer shorter tenures for gold loans.

## Interest computation

Interest on a gold loan is charged on the outstanding balance. The rate is set by the lender within the regulatory framework, and the computation method determines the effective cost to the borrower. The main methods are:

- **Simple interest.** Interest is charged only on the original principal for the tenure of the loan. The total interest is the principal multiplied by the annual rate multiplied by the tenure in years.
- **Reducing balance.** Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, which reduces as the borrower makes periodic payments. The total interest is lower than simple interest for the same rate and tenure.
- **Accumulated interest.** Interest is added to the principal and repaid at maturity. The effective cost is similar to simple interest, but the borrower does not service the interest during the loan.

The effective annualised cost depends on the rate, the method and any additional charges the lender may impose, such as processing fees, valuation charges or insurance costs. The specific rate ceilings and the exact regulatory limits on charges are not reproduced in this packet and must be verified against current RBI directions and the lender's tariff.

## What happens when a loan is not repaid

If the borrower fails to repay the loan by the due date — the maturity date or the date specified in the loan agreement — the lender has a mechanism to recover the outstanding amount. The process generally works as follows:

1. **Demand notice.** The issuer issues a notice to the borrower demanding repayment of the outstanding amount, including principal, interest and any charges. The notice typically specifies a period within which the borrower may repay and reclaim the gold.
2. **Auction.** If the borrower does not repay within the specified period, the lender can auction the pledged gold. The auction is conducted through a prescribed process, and the gold is sold to the highest bidder.
3. **Application of proceeds.** The auction proceeds are applied first to the costs of the auction, then to the outstanding principal, interest and charges. Any surplus after these deductions is returned to the borrower.
4. **Shortfall.** If the auction proceeds are insufficient to cover the outstanding amount, the shortfall may remain the borrower's liability, depending on the terms of the loan agreement and the applicable regulatory framework.

The auction process is the lender's remedy of last resort. It is also the mechanism that makes gold loans relatively low-risk for lenders: the collateral is a liquid asset that can be sold quickly, which is why gold loans typically carry lower interest rates than unsecured lending.

## Explain simply

A gold loan is money borrowed by handing your gold jewellery to a lender as security. The lender looks at how much your gold is worth and lends you a fraction of that amount — not the full value, so that if the price of gold falls they still have enough security. You pay interest on the amount you borrowed, and when you repay the loan you get your gold back. If you do not repay, the lender can sell your gold at an auction, take back what you are owed, and give you whatever is left over. If the sale does not cover what you owe, you may still owe the difference.

## Worked example: interest, auction and surplus

This is an illustrative calculation, not observed data, and the figures are hypothetical numbers chosen only to make the arithmetic visible. They are not the actual rates or terms of any specific lender.

Suppose a borrower pledges ornaments with an assessed gold value of ₹100,000 and borrows ₹75,000, which represents a 75% loan-to-value ratio. Assume, purely for illustration, that the annual interest rate is 12%, the tenure is one year, and interest is accumulated and repaid at maturity.

The total interest for one year is:

$$75000 \times 0.12 = 9000$$

The total amount owed at maturity is:

$$75000 + 9000 = 84000$$

If the borrower does not repay, the lender auctions the pledged gold. Suppose the auction realizes ₹95,000 after costs. The lender applies the proceeds to the outstanding amount:

$$95000 - 84000 = 11000$$

The surplus of ₹11,000 is returned to the borrower. If the auction had realized only ₹70,000, there would be a shortfall of ₹14,000, which may remain the borrower's liability depending on the terms of the loan agreement.

The calculation is illustrative and depends entirely on the assumed figures. What is structural is the sequence: the lender recovers from the auction first, returns any surplus, and pursues the borrower for any shortfall. The borrower's economic loss is the difference between the value of the gold pledged and the amount borrowed plus interest — the cost of having used the gold as security.

## Risks for the borrower

Gold loans are often marketed as quick, low-cost credit, but several risks deserve attention:

- **Interest cost.** The effective annualised cost can be significantly higher than the headline rate once processing fees, valuation charges and other costs are included. The borrower should compute the total cost of borrowing, not just the interest rate.
- **Auction risk.** If the gold price falls during the loan tenure and the borrower cannot repay, the auction may realize less than expected, potentially leaving a shortfall.
- **Liquidity of the collateral.** Gold is liquid, which is precisely why the lender can recover quickly. The borrower should not assume that the lender will negotiate leniently.
- **Terms of the loan.** Prepayment charges, minimum tenures and the treatment of surplus or shortfall vary by lender and must be read in the loan agreement before signing.

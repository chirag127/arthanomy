There are two genuinely different ways for an Indian resident to own a foreign security, and everything else in this article is a variation on the choice between them. One is to hold units in an Indian fund that itself invests in foreign securities; the other is to earn and remit foreign currency, open an account abroad or a locaLly operated account that routes abroad, and buy the securities directly. The first keeps the regulatory complexity inside the fund. The second puts it on the investor's own desk. Each is legitimate, each is used by millions of people, and neither is free.

This is the third article in the Global Investing series. It treats the operational mechanics of getting capital out legally, the documentation each route requires, the compliance layer that sits on top, and a comparison of the routes by cost type rather than by cost magnitude. Magnitudes need current market data and are therefore outside this article's scope.

## The two routes in principle

**Route one: an Indian international fund or fund of funds.** The Indian fund accepts rupees, invests in foreign securities directly or through a foreign counterpart fund, and issues units in India. The investor buys units in rupees, holds them in a Indian mutual fund account, receives foreign-driven returns converted back into rupees, and reports the holding as a domestic security for most practical purposes. The fund is the entity that has to stay within the overseas-investment limits that apply to Indian mutual funds, hire a foreign custodian, handle the foreign taxes and claim on offset evidence, and deal with the compliance committee. The investor sees a domestic unit, not a foreign position.

**Route two: a direct overseas brokerage account.** The investor uses the Liberalised Remittance Scheme to fund an account at a broker operating outside India, or a domestic platform that routes through such a broker, with foreign currency. The account holds the securities in the investor's own name at the foreign depository or broker. The investor owns the asset directly, receives distributions in the foreign currency, files the foreign-asset disclosures in India, and handles the foreign-tax credit and treaty computation on the Indian side.

The decisive legal distinction is whether the individual used their own remittance limit. In route one the fund is the remitter, and no individual annual limit is touched. In route two every incremental purchase within the year draws on the individual's remaining limit for that year. This is why route one is attractive for smaller, regular additions and route two is attractive for larger, less frequent ones.

## Route one: the domestic international fund

An Indian fund that invests abroad is subject to the foreign-investment limits prescribed for Indian mutual funds, under which the aggregate and per-fund overseas exposure is capped. These caps are the practical reason that a domestic fund sometimes suspends new inflows or moves to a feeder route when the limit is approached, and the reason that the availability and pricing of this route change with time rather than being structural.

Mechanically, the fund receives rupees, converts them to the foreign currency through its own conversion arrangement, invests in an underlying foreign fund or directly in securities, and reports the position to its Indian investors in rupees. Distributions, dividends and capital gains are realised or accrued inside the fund, and the units' net asset value reflects the foreign asset and the exchange rate. The investor does not receive foreign currency and does not have a personal custodian abroad.

The paperwork for the investor is the same as for any Indian mutual fund: a permanent account number, a know-your-customer record, a bank account for dividends or redemptions, and the standard application form. The fund handles the rest. The trade-off is that the investor's choice of underlying exposure is only as broad as the fund's stated mandate, the expenses are fund-level and recurring, and the foreign exposure is intermediated in both directions.

## Route two: direct overseas brokerage

The direct route uses the Liberalised Remittance Scheme. A resident individual may remit funds abroad up to the prescribed annual limit for permitted current and capital account transactions, including investment in securities, and the remittance must be routed through an authorised dealer bank. The permitted and prohibited purposes of the scheme, and the consequences of breach, are set out in the Reserve Bank's master direction and the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) framework. This is the master switch for the entire route: a remittance that does not fit the permitted purpose categories does not go through.

Once the money is abroad it can be used to fund an account at a foreign broker or a domestic platform that itself routes the order to a foreign broker. The investor then holds securities in the foreign market, in its settlement system and currency, and the domestic platform, if any, is an intermediary rather than the owner of the asset.

The direct route's appeal is precision: the investor can choose the specific securities, control the timing of foreign-exchange conversion and avoid the fund-level expense. Its cost is the paperwork, which is real, and the compliance, which is permanent.

## Documentation: route one

Documentation for the domestic international fund is minimal relative to owning abroad directly, and it is the standard Indian mutual fund set.

- **Permanent account number.** Required for any mutual fund investment in India and for tax reporting.
- **Know-your-customer completion.** The standardised Indian KYC record, including identity and address proof, held by the mutual fund or its registrar.
- **Bank account.** For subscriptions, redemptions and distribution payments, in the investor's own name.
- **Application and declaration.** Including the standard risks and product-specific declarations for an international fund.
- **Tax declarations where applicable.** Such as declarations relevant to withholding where specified.

None of these crosses the border. The fund's own overseas investment, custody and tax handling is its own obligation and is reported through its regular Indian regulatory filings rather than through the individual's.

## Documentation: route two

The direct route has two parallel documentation chains: one with the authorised dealer bank for the remittance, and one with the broker for the account. Both have to be completed, and both have to stay current.

With the authorised dealer bank:
- **Permanent account number and the standard KYC set.** The bank already holds these for the customer's existing relationship.
- **The remittance application and form.** Specifying the amount, the currency and the purpose, drawn from the scheme's permitted purpose categories.
- **Source-of-funds declaration.** The bank needs to be satisfied about the origin of the rupees being remitted, particularly for larger amounts.
- **Tax-collection set-up.** Because collection at source applies to certain remittances, the account has to carry the mechanism by which the bank computes and records it, which feeds into the investor's annual return. The collection effect is treated separately, in the article on tax collected at source on foreign investment remittances.
- **Reporting and record-keeping.** The bank's obligations under the foreign-exchange reporting route require that the remittance is tagged to the correct purpose and that records retained. The investor retains the remittance advice, which is the single document that reconciles the Indian and foreign sides.

With the broker and the foreign trail:
- **Broker onboarding and account opening.** Including identity verification, which the broker performs under its own regulator's rules and which may repeat, rather than accept, the Indian documents.
- **A tax-declaration form required by the foreign broker.** This affects both the rate at which dividends are withheld in the foreign jurisdiction and the way the investor establishes that they are a non-resident of the issuing country for the purposes of the applicable tax treaty.
- **Funding instructions.** The form and reference by which the remitted foreign currency lands in the correct sub-account and currency.
- **Custody and dealing terms.** The broker's own terms set the charges, the interest on cash balances and the conditions of use of margin, if any.

A prudent practice is to keep a single file with the remittance advices, the collection records, the broker statements and the foreign tax vouchers, because every one of these feeds the Indian annual return.

## Compliance once the money is abroad

Owning foreign assets as an individual Indian resident carries a continuing compliance layer, and it does not end with the account opening.

- **The annual return requires disclosure of foreign assets.** Holders of foreign assets must declare them in the schedule in which foreign assets are reported, in the return for the year in which the assets were held, with details of the holding, the income earned and the nature of the interest. The schedule covers foreign bank accounts, custodial holdings, foreign equity and immovable property, among others.
- **Non-disclosure carries severe consequences.** The penalties include a multiple of the tax on the undisclosed income and prosecution, and the risk of permanent-disability of estate planning on undisclosed assets. This is one of the few areas of investor compliance where the documentation habit is worth more than the investment.
- **Foreign dividends and income are taxable in India.** They have to be reported and are taxed at the applicable rates for the head of income they belong to. Foreign tax withheld on those dividends may attract foreign tax credit, computed under the rules in the Income-tax Act read with the applicable treaty, and limited in the usual way to the Indian tax attributable to that income. The credit has to be claimed with the foreign tax payment evidence and through the prescribed procedural route.
- **Tax residency evidence.** Where treaty benefits are claimed, evidence of tax residency, such as a tax residency certificate, and any required declarations about permanent establishment or beneficial ownership are raised in advance rather than after the income arrives.
- **The foreign account has to be kept live and compliant.** Dormancy rules, inactivity fees, and the broker's own periodic re-verification requirements apply, and the investor retains responsibility for an account in their own name regardless of how easy the platform makes the interface feel.

The compliance layer is the reason some investors resolve to keep foreign exposure below the level at which they were fully confident of completing it every year. A half-reported foreign position is worse than none, because it combines the exposure with the penalty risk.

## What each route typically does better

Comparison of magnitudes would require current market data and is therefore deliberately not attempted here. Comparison by type is more useful because it does not go out of date.

| Dimension | Domestic international fund | Direct overseas brokerage |
|---|---|---|
| Currency conversion control | None: handled by the fund | Full: chosen by the investor |
| Underlying choice | Limited to fund mandate | Broad: individual securities and funds |
| Custody location | India, through the fund and its counterpart | Abroad, in the broker and foreign depository |
| Foreign tax handling | Inside the fund and its tax claim | On the investor and the broker's withholding |
| Indian compliance load | Low | High: foreign-asset disclosure each year |
| Cash cost type | Recurring fund charges | Up-front and recurring brokerage, conversion and custody |
| Incremental complexity with size | Low | Rises with the complexity of the portfolio |
| Regulatory exposure | Fund's, broadly | Investor's, directly |
| Entry documentation | Indian mutual fund set | Bank remittance plus broker account plus tax declarations |

The domestic route is the lower-compliance option. The direct route is the lower intermediation option. Neither is universally cheaper: recurring fund charges compound silently, conversion margins and custody fees do the same, and the value of each depends on the holding period and the size of the position.

## Behavioural costs: the part that is not in the table

Two costs do not show in any charge schedule but decide whether the route is actually used well.

The first is attention. A direct foreign account has its own news flow, its own tax calendar, and its own reconciliation, and an investor who does not run those processes reliably will incur penalties, miss credits or leave cash earning nothing in a foreign currency. The domestic fund reduces all of these to a single line on an Indian account statement.

The second is the home bias it creates against using the route at all. The compliance layer is not trivial, and the investor who decides to hold a small recurring foreign exposure through a fund is not necessarily making a cost error; they may be correctly pricing their own bandwidth. The opposite, an investor who opens a direct account, makes one purchase and then neglects it, is paying running costs for a dead position.

## Operational sequencing if you choose the direct route

The order of operations reduces errors.

1. **Confirm the permitted purpose.** Read the scheme's permitted and prohibited purposes against the intended transaction before approaching the bank; the authorised dealer will apply them anyway.
2. **Set up the Indian side first.** Verify the bank account, the tax number record and the KYC before the remittance, so that the remittance itself is the only moving part.
3. **Open the broker account.** Many platforms allow this in parallel, and an incomplete application blocks funding, not the other way round.
4. **Make a pilot remittance.** A small amount, where the economics allow it, goes through the whole chain once and exposes the frictions, such as a mismatched reference or a timing gap between the remittance and the broker's credit, while the cost of the error is small.
5. **Record the chain.** Remittance advice, collection record, foreign currency credit, purchase confirmation and settlement, in one file.
6. **Schedule the compliance.** Diarise the annual foreign-asset disclosure in the same way as the tax return; it has no separate prompt and no separate reminder, which is precisely how it gets missed.

The single most common operational failure at step four is a timing mismatch in which the remittance is made on a date that does not align with the broker's funding window, so that the money sits earning nothing in a foreign currency while a trade is being prepared. A pilot remittance eliminates most of this.

## Explain simply

There are two ways to own foreign assets as an Indian resident, and the difference is where the administrative work is done. A domestic international fund keeps the work inside the fund, limits the choice and removes the individual's remittance and foreign disclosure burden. Funding an overseas brokerage account directly gives far more choice and lower intermediation, but every step of the foreign-exchange and reporting layer becomes a personal obligation, and the money has to leave under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme through an authorised dealer bank. Neither is universally cheaper; the correct comparison is between recurring fund charges on one side and conversion, custody, brokerage and a substantial annual compliance obligation on the other. The realistic determinant is the investor's size, frequency and willingness to run a foreign paperwork process every year, which is something people routinely underestimate.

## Worked example

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast. The figures are invented to show the sequencing and the idle time.

Suppose an investor decides to fund a direct overseas account with Rs 12,00,000 in a single financial year, in three equal remittances of Rs 4,00,000 each. The authorised dealer bank needs two working days to clear a remittance against the application, the foreign broker typically credits the funds one working day after the remittance arrives, and the investor intends to buy within five working days of the first credit. The money therefore sits idle in a foreign currency account earning the broker's default rate for approximately that week at each tranche, and for the full duration of any tranche that is not immediately invested.

If the annualised hurdle rate is 7 percent, idle time of one week per tranche on Rs 4,00,000 costs roughly Rs 540 per tranche at simple interest, and Rs 1440 on the assumption that the uninvested third tranche waits until the next buying window. The collected amount applied at an illustrative 10 percent is Rs 40,000 per tranche and is withheld from the invested amount altogether during the year. The buying window also interacts with the foreign market's trading hours, which are offset against Indian time, and execution therefore happens on the next available market session. The total arithmetic is small but the model shows the method: compare the fund route's recurring charge, computed on the full expected position, to the direct route's conversion, custody and idle-time cost, and to the value that the investor places on their own compliance time.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes structure and process. It deliberately states no annual remittance limit, no foreign-investment ceiling for Indian mutual funds, no brokerage or custody figure, no trading-hours conversion or tax rate, because each of these is set by a regulator or a platform and changes, and quoting one would create false precision.

It also does not evaluate any specific platform, broker or market, and does not cover the taxation of foreign dividends and capital gains in detail beyond the compliance layer. That treatment belongs in the articles on buying a foreign equity, currency risk and the foreign-tax credit, which are linked below. And it does not cover the treatment of a foreign position on death or succession, which raises cross-border questions outside the scope of this piece.

A life insurance claim is decided twice. The first decision happens when the policy is sold, in the underwriting process, where the insurer prices a risk it cannot fully see. The second happens years later, when a claim is made and the insurer tests the death against the answers given at the start. The gap between those two moments is where claim-denial risk lives, and it is created almost entirely by the information exchanged at the proposal stage. This article explains what underwriting is for, what the duty of disclosure requires, how pre-existing conditions are defined and where those definitions disagree, what actually voids a claim, and what routes exist when a policy was mis-sold. It does not — and cannot — tell anyone how to answer a particular medical question; that is a factual matter.

## Why underwriting exists: adverse selection and moral hazard

An insurer prices a promise to pay on a contingency, and the person buying the policy knows more about that contingency than the insurer does. Two forces follow.

- **Adverse selection.** People who suspect they are unwell are more likely to seek cover, so a pool priced as though everyone were average would attract a disproportionate share of bad risks and deteriorate. Underwriting is the tool that prices each applicant on their own characteristics, holding the pool together.
- **Moral hazard.** Insurance can change behaviour. A contract that pays on an event may make the event marginally more likely, so insurers attach exclusions and conditions to keep the incentives aligned.

Underwriting and disclosure are the two halves of one mechanism. The insurer asks a set of questions, may require medical tests, and assigns the applicant to an underwriting class that determines the premium, the exclusions and sometimes whether cover is offered at all. The applicant supplies the answers. The price and the promise are built on those answers, which is why the answers matter enormously later.

## The duty of disclosure

The duty of disclosure requires that material facts be given to the insurer accurately and completely. The operative word is **material**. A fact is material if a prudent insurer, applying the insurer's own underwriting judgement, would treat it as relevant to the decision to accept the risk or to the premium and terms offered. Materiality is judged from the insurer's perspective, not the applicant's, which is why "I did not think it was important" is not a defence to a non-disclosure.

Three consequences follow, and they are structural rather than advisory.

- **The printed questions are not the boundary of the duty.** The form is how the insurer gathers most of the information, but the obligation concerns facts, and a fact the form does not explicitly ask about can still be material.
- **The answers are the basis of the contract.** Because the premium was set on them, an inaccurate answer changes the terms on which the insurer agreed to carry the risk.
- **The duty is continuous in spirit at the proposal stage.** If circumstances change between the proposal and the issuance of the policy, the insurer is entitled to a picture that remains accurate. Whether, and how, a later change must be reported is a matter for the policy's terms.

This is where the temptation to treat the form as paperwork becomes expensive, because the cost is not paid at the point of sale. It is paid at claim time, by the family, when the insured is no longer present to explain.

## Pre-existing conditions: where definitions disagree

The pre-existing condition is the single largest source of disclosure disputes, and the reason is that the phrase has several meanings that are close enough to be confused.

- **The medical meaning.** A condition that existed, whether or not it had been diagnosed or treated, before a given point in time.
- **The policy definition.** Most policies define a pre-existing condition by reference to a window before the policy — typically a period during which the insured had a diagnosis, showed symptoms, sought or received medical advice, or underwent treatment for a condition. The exact window and the exact triggers are set by the contract.
- **The disclosure definition.** What the proposal form asked, and what the applicant understood themselves to be reporting.

The gaps are where disputes happen. A symptom that was investigated but never diagnosed as a disease may fall within a policy definition that speaks of symptoms or medical advice. A one-off consultation that the applicant did not think of as a condition may be material. A condition correctly disclosed may still be excluded from cover for a period by a specific waiting period attached to it. And a condition that is covered may be subject to a definition of severity that the claimant's diagnosis does not satisfy.

None of this is trickery; it is the ordinary consequence of three different definitions being used by three different parties. The practical lesson is that the policy's own definition of a pre-existing condition, and any waiting period attached to it, is the wording that governs — not the medical understanding, and not a verbal reassurance at the point of sale.

## Non-disclosure and the consequence at claim time

When a claim is made, the insurer assesses it against the policy and the disclosures made at proposal. If a material fact was not disclosed, the insurer has grounds to question the policy. The possible outcomes depend on the terms and the framework in force, and can include declining the claim, treating the policy as void, or adjusting the benefit.

It is worth being precise about the mechanism, because it explains why disclosure risk is asymmetric for the policyholder.

- **The insurer holds the record.** The proposal form and any medical reports are preserved, and they are what the insurer consults when a claim arises.
- **The claimant bears the practical burden.** The nominee must often explain an omission that the insured made years earlier, with no ability to ask the insured what happened.
- **Timing amplifies the problem.** A claim is made precisely when a household has lost an earner, which is the worst possible moment to be arguing about a form.

IRDAI's framework cushions this in two ways. It provides a free-look period, during which a newly issued policy can be returned, and it provides a moratorium period after which a policy cannot ordinarily be called in question for non-disclosure except on grounds of fraud. Both are real protections, and both have durations set by regulation; those durations must be verified from the current text, because they are exactly the kind of figure that is dangerous to recall from memory. Where a claim is rejected or delayed, the framework also prescribes a grievance-redressal route that runs through the insurer and, if needed, the regulator.

## What actually voids a claim

Pulling the threads together, a claim fails for reasons that are almost always about the contract's conditions rather than about the loss.

| Cause | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Material non-disclosure | A relevant fact was inaccurate or omitted at proposal |
| Pre-existing condition exclusion | The condition falls within the policy's definition or a waiting period |
| Definitional mismatch | The event does not satisfy the policy's definition of the insured event |
| Exclusion clause | The cause of death or illness falls within a stated exclusion |
| Lapse | Premiums were not paid and the policy had lapsed before the event |
| Fraud | A claim or disclosure found to be fraudulent rather than merely mistaken |

The table is not a list of insurers' misdeeds. It is a list of the places where a household's expectation and the contract's terms can diverge, and every row except the last is a reason to read the document before relying on it.

## Worked example: how a concealed diagnosis surfaces at claim time

An illustrative scenario, hypothetical and not drawn from any real claim. A person takes cover and, at proposal, omits to mention a consultation two years earlier at which a chronic condition was investigated. The omission is not deliberate; the person did not think an investigation that ended without treatment counted as a condition.

Ten years later the insured dies of an unrelated cause, and the nominee claims. The insurer retrieves the proposal form and the medical records it can obtain, and finds the earlier consultation. Under the policy's definition, the condition was pre-existing at the time of proposal and was not disclosed. Whether the claim is paid now turns on questions that the family cannot easily answer: was the undisclosed fact material to the insurer's decision, does the framework's moratorium or fraud exception apply, and what remedy is available if the parties disagree.

The scenario has no fabricated statistics in it, and it does not need any. Its point is structural: the dispute was created at proposal and settled at claim, with the insured absent from the second conversation. That is why the disclosure obligation is treated, throughout this series, as the foundation of the contract rather than as a formality.

## Explain simply

Insurance is priced on what you tell the insurer about yourself. If something relevant is left out or answered inaccurately, the insurer can question the policy when a claim is made — years later, when the family is the one arguing. Pre-existing conditions are defined differently by different policies, so it is worth checking the policy's own definition and any waiting period. There are protections in the rules, including a free-look period and a moratorium, but the safest position is a proposal that was accurate when it was signed.

## Mis-selling and the route to a remedy

Not every claim denial is about disclosure. Some disputes are about how the policy was sold: a benefit described that the contract did not contain, a risk understated, a premium presented as a return. Where a consumer has been misled by a representation or received a deficient service, the **Consumer Protection Act, 2019** provides a remedy. It sets up a three-tier structure of district, state and national commissions to hear complaints, with the forum determined by the value of the claim, and it covers unfair trade practices and deficiency in service alongside misleading representations.

Two practical implications follow. First, the record matters: a mis-selling complaint is easier to establish when the representation was in writing — a brochure, a benefit illustration, a message — than when it was made verbally in a sales meeting. Second, the remedy is separate from the claim assessment. A claim can be assessed on the policy's terms while a mis-selling complaint addresses how the policy came to be sold, and the two can proceed on different tracks.

## What this does not tell you

This article explains the structure of the underwriting and disclosure process and how a claim is tested against it. It does not advise on how to answer any question, does not identify any insurer's practice, and does not predict the outcome of any dispute. The durations of the free-look period and the moratorium, the precise grounds on which a policy may be questioned, and the conditions attached to any exception are set by IRDAI's regulations in their current form and must be verified from the current text.

Tax and regulatory consequences of a policy being avoided or a claim being paid are outside the scope here and are governed by the applicable law in force at the time. Where a real dispute arises, the insurer's grievance process, the regulator's redressal route and the consumer commissions are the formal channels, each with its own procedure and time limits.

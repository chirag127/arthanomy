The phrase that sells a project most effectively is also the one most often misread: "RERA registered". Registration is not a certificate, a rating or a guarantee. It is the gateway into a framework that imposes continuing obligations on the promoter — to disclose, to keep sale collections in an escrow, to define areas on a legal footing, and to deliver on a declared timeline. Understanding what those obligations are is the difference between relying on a badge and relying on a rule.

This article explains the registration requirement, the duties that follow from it, and the enforcement path a buyer actually holds.

## Explain simply

When a developer wants to sell flats in a project above a certain size, the law requires the project to be registered before it is advertised or sold. To register, the developer has to file the facts about the project: the land, the approvals, the plans, the completion date and the size of each unit. After registration, the developer must put a prescribed share of the money collected from buyers into a special account and take it out only as construction actually progresses. Shorter flats are described by carpet area, not by a padded super area. If the developer is late, or stops, or changes the plan without permission, the buyer has defined remedies before the state regulatory authority. The category of the project's registration matters less than whether the obligations are being met.

## When registration is triggered

The framework requires a promoter to register a project with the state real-estate regulatory authority before advertising, selling or offering for sale. Registration is not universal: it applies to projects above the size that the Act and rules prescribe, measured in terms such as the number of apartments, plot area or developed area, and certain categories of work — repairs and renovations that involve no marketing, for instance — fall outside. Where a project is phased, the phases and their separate registration are governed by the rules, and a promoter cannot use a phase boundary to escape disclosure of the whole. The precise triggers are set in the Act and in state rules and should be read from the current text.

Three practical implications follow:

- **No registration, no lawful sale.** If a project that falls within the framework is being sold without registration, that is itself the first finding, and it changes the entire assessment.
- **Registration is public.** It exists as a record with a number, a promoter, a declared timeline and filed documents. It is designed to be checked.
- **Registration is per project, not per promoter.** A sound earlier project says nothing about the registration status of the one being sold now.

## What the registration must disclose

The value of the record lies in what the promoter is forced to put on it. The framework requires disclosure of the project's particulars: the promoter's identity and the registered details; the land and its title and encumbrances; the approvals and the sanctioned layout and building plans; the declared date of completion; the number and carpet area of the units; the arrangements for civic infrastructure and common areas; and the names of the professionals engaged, such as the architect and engineer. Where the promoter has other projects in the state, the record links them.

The point of the disclosure is not the filing. It is that the disclosed facts become the standard the promoter is later held to. If the approval relied upon is not there, or the sanctioned plan differs from what is being built, or the timeline declared at registration is not the one being quoted verbally, the record supplies the discrepancy. This is why a buyer should read the portal entry rather than a brochure: the same fact is stated in both places, and only one of them is enforceable.

## The escrow obligation

The single most important continuing duty is the escrow requirement. A prescribed share of the amounts collected from allottees must be deposited in a designated account maintained for the project, and withdrawals from that account are permitted only in proportion to the construction actually completed, certified as the regulations require.

The mechanism answers a specific failure. Before the framework, sale proceeds could be collected and spent on land elsewhere, on another project, or on anything at all, while the building being paid for stood incomplete. Tying withdrawal to construction does not guarantee completion — a promoter with insufficient resources can still fail — but it removes the largest single channel through which buyers' money disappeared without a building.

For a buyer, the escrow has two practical consequences. It makes the construction-linked payment schedule more than a schedule: each call for money should correspond to progress. And it explains why a promoter asking for an informal, off-schedule payment is a warning, not a convenience. The named mechanics — the proportion, the certification, the withdrawal procedure — are in the Act and rules and change with amendments.

## Carpet area and the disclosure standard

Before the framework, the same flat could be sold as a larger or smaller number depending on how much lobby, wall thickness and balcony was included in the total. The framework addresses this by defining carpet area for disclosure purposes and requiring units to be described by it in the records and the agreement.

The consequence is that two units of the same physical size can no longer be made to look different by arithmetic. The buyer gains a comparable number, and the promoter loses the room that super-area loading once provided. What the framework does not do is police the *ratio* between the carpet area and the built-up or super area charged for; that ratio remains a commercial term, and understanding it is a separate exercise. But the disclosed base number becomes a fixed reference point, and the agreement must match it.

## The agreement for sale and the timeline

The framework standardises the next step after booking. A promoter cannot collect beyond a prescribed amount before the agreement for sale is executed, and the agreement must follow within the prescribed period. The agreement is required to reflect the statutory particulars: the carpet area, the declared timeline for possession, the payment schedule, the description of the common areas, and the consequences of default by either side.

The timeline in the agreement is the one that counts. The framework declares a completion date at registration and makes it the promoter's obligation to deliver by then. Extensions are not free: they are granted on an application to the authority on the grounds the framework allows, and an extension that exists only in a conversation or a revised brochure does not replace the statutory timeline. A buyer who has not looked at the declared date has no way to know whether a delay has already begun.

## Penalties and enforcement

The framework is enforced by the state regulatory authority, with an adjudicating officer for matters of compensation and interest and an appellate tribunal above it. Contravention of the registration and disclosure obligations attracts penalties, and for defined contraventions the Act provides for imprisonment. For a continuing breach, an additional penalty accrues for each day the contravention continues. The specific amounts and periods are set in the Act and are amended; the shape of the mechanism is that the penalty is designed to exceed the benefit of non-compliance.

Two structural features of enforcement deserve honest emphasis. First, the authority's remedies are monetary and declaratory; they do not build the building. Second, and more gravely, the value of a remedy depends on the promoter's ability to pay it. An award against a promoter in insolvency is a claim, not a recovery. The framework improves the buyer's legal position substantially; it does not transfer the promoter's execution risk onto the state.

## The remedies a buyer actually holds

Beyond penalty, the framework gives the allottee specific rights.

- **Interest for delay.** Where possession is delayed beyond the declared date, the allottee is entitled to interest for the period of delay, computed at the rate the Act prescribes by reference to a notified lending-rate benchmark plus a margin. The same rate applies to the promoter if the buyer defaults on payment.
- **Withdrawal and refund.** In defined circumstances — a delay exceeding the period the framework allows, or the promoter's failure to comply with the agreement — the allottee may withdraw and claim a refund of the amount paid with interest.
- **Defect liability.** After possession, the promoter remains liable for defects in workmanship or materials, and for structural defects, for a period the framework specifies, with the obligation to repair within a stated time.
- **Compensation for false representation.** Where a buyer suffers loss because of an advertisement, prospectus or representation that was false, the promoter is liable.

Each of these is pursued by complaint to the authority, which has the powers of a civil court for the purpose, or through the adjudicating officer for compensation. The remedy is a process, not an automatic payment, and it is worth retaining the evidence — receipts, the agreement, the correspondence, the payment schedule — that a claim requires.

## Extension, completion and deregistration

Three further mechanisms complete the picture. Where the promoter applies, the authority may grant an extension of the registration on the grounds the framework permits, and where the promoter fails to complete as required, the registration can lapse or be revoked. Once a project is completed and the promoter has fulfilled the obligations, the registration can be closed out. Each of these is a formal act, and a project whose registration status changes without any notice to the allottees is a red flag rather than an administrative event.

## Worked example: delayed possession arithmetic

An illustrative calculation, not an award. Suppose an allottee has paid ₹50,00,000 towards a unit whose declared possession date has passed, and possession is now eighteen months late. The interest rate the Act prescribes is not stated here; for the arithmetic only, assume a compensation rate of 10% a year — this is an assumption chosen to show the mechanism, not a statement of the statutory rate.

$$ \text{compensation} = 50,00,000 \times 10\% \times \frac{18}{12} = 7,50,000 $$

The structure of the calculation is what matters: a base amount, a prescribed rate, and time measured in months of delay. The rate is not the buyer's to choose — it is fixed by the framework — and the base amount is the sum actually paid, which is why retaining payment receipts through the construction period is not administrative tidiness but the foundation of a claim. The same arithmetic would run against a buyer who defaulted on a scheduled payment, because the framework applies a single rate in both directions.

## What RERA does not do

- **It does not guarantee title.** Registration requires disclosure of title and encumbrances; it does not cure a defective one.
- **It does not guarantee completion.** The escrow constrains how money is spent; it does not supply money the promoter never raised.
- **It does not guarantee quality.** The occupancy certificate and the defect-liability period are the mechanisms for construction quality, and neither is the same as the buyer's satisfaction.
- **It does not set the price or the area ratio.** Those remain commercial terms.
- **It does not make the promoter solvent.** A monetary award against a promoter who cannot pay is worth what recovery is worth.
- **It does not apply to every transaction.** Projects below the prescribed size, and certain non-marketed works, sit outside the framework.

## Open questions

- How much of the protection in the framework is realisable in the state where a given project sits, and how does an investor in a far-off project verify the authority's capacity?
- If the escrow constrains a promoter's cash flow, does it displace risk onto slower completion rather than removing it?
- Is the defect-liability period long enough for structural problems that emerge slowly, and is it enforced in practice?
- How should an allottee combine the statutory remedy with the civil remedy, and which should be pursued first when a project stalls?

Registration is a gate. What protects a buyer is not passing through it but the obligations that sit behind it, and how strictly they are kept.

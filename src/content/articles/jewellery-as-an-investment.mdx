Jewellery is one of the largest stores of gold in India, and it is almost never counted correctly. A family buys a piece for a wedding, tells themselves it is an investment, and discovers decades later that the resale value is a fraction of the price paid. The gap is not a mystery and not a scam; it is the arithmetic of making charges, stones and purity, and it is predictable. This article explains why the gap exists, how large it is, and how to separate the decision to buy ornament from the decision to invest.

## Explain simply

When you buy a gold necklace, you pay for three things: the gold in it, the work that went into making it, and any stones set in it. When you sell it back, you are paid for the gold at today's rate, minus a little for testing, and whatever the stones are worth. The money you paid for the work — the making charge — is not paid back. It is the cost of turning metal into a designed article, and it is gone the moment the piece is finished. So the price you paid and the price you can get back are separated by the making charge, and that gap is why jewellery rarely recovers its cost.

## The two decisions welded together

The root of the jewellery-as-investment problem is that two different decisions are made in a single transaction. The ornamental decision asks: do I want to wear this, is the design right, is the craftsmanship worth the price? The investment decision asks: what will this be worth when I sell it, and what is the round-trip cost? A piece of jewellery can be an excellent answer to the first question and a poor answer to the second, and the trouble begins when the buyer treats the two answers as one.

The ornament is a consumption good. Its value is the utility of wearing it, the beauty of the design and the social meaning of the occasion. The investment is a store of value. Its value is the metal it contains and the price at which it can be sold. These are different questions with different answers, and the purchase price of a piece of jewellery answers the first but not the second. The making charge is the price of the ornament; the resale value is the price of the investment; and the difference between them is the cost of having welded the two together.

## Making charges: the non-recoverable fabrication fee

Making charges are the jeweller's fabrication fee for converting bullion into jewellery, quoted either as a lump sum per gram or as a percentage of the metal value, and they vary with design complexity and retailer. A simple chain carries a low making charge; a complex bridal piece carries a high one. The making charge is typically non-recoverable on sale and is excluded from the resale price of jewellery, which is usually based on the prevailing metal rate with deductions for purity loss. The share of making charges plus GST in the total cost is why jewellery is a poor vehicle for short-term gold-price exposure.

The making charge is not a hidden cost; it is a visible cost that is mentally reclassified as value. The buyer sees a price, knows a making charge is included, and then behaves as if the whole price were metal value. The reclassification is the error. The making charge is the price of the design, and it is a real cost that is incurred at purchase and never recovered. A buyer who understands this will judge a piece of jewellery the way they judge any other designed object — by whether the design is worth the price — and will stop expecting the design cost to come back on resale.

## Stones, purity and the resale deduction

In stone-set jewellery, the gap between purchase price and resale value widens further. The purchase price includes the metal, the making charge and the full retail value of the stones. The resale price is based on the prevailing metal rate with deductions for purity loss, and the stones are valued separately, usually at a fraction of their retail price. The result is that the buyer of a stone-set piece recovers the metal, a fraction of the stone value and none of the making charge.

Purity adds another layer. The hallmarking regime administered by the Bureau of Indian Standards — the Hallmarking Regulation 2018, the Mandatory Hallmarking Order and their amendments, with jeweller registration and assaying centres — certifies that a piece is the carat it claims to be. A 22-carat hallmarked piece is guaranteed to be 22 carat. That guarantee protects the buyer from being sold under-carated metal, but it does not protect the buyer from paying a high making charge or from losing that charge on resale. The hallmark is an authenticity mark, not a value mark, and the distinction matters most at the moment of sale, when the buyer discovers that the guarantee covers the metal and nothing else.

## The round trip: an illustrative calculation

The following is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast and not a live quote. It shows how the making charge creates the gap.

Suppose a buyer purchases a twenty-gram necklace for an illustrative ₹1,50,000. The metal value of twenty grams at the prevailing rate is an illustrative ₹1,40,000, and the making charge is an illustrative ₹10,000, about 7.1 per cent of the purchase price. The buyer wears the necklace for five years and then sells it. The metal price is unchanged, and the resale price is the metal value less an illustrative 1 per cent purity and testing deduction, so ₹1,40,000 less ₹1,400, or ₹1,38,600.

- Purchase price: ₹1,50,000.
- Resale proceeds: ₹1,38,600.
- Round-trip loss: $150000 - 138600 = ₹11,400$, about 7.6 per cent of the purchase price.

The metal price did not move, and the buyer lost more than seven per cent. The loss is the making charge, plus the purity deduction, and it would be the same in a rising or falling metal market unless the rise was large enough to cover it. For the buyer to break even, the metal price would have to rise by the making charge plus the deduction — an illustrative ₹11,400, or about 7.6 per cent of the metal value — and that is before counting the time value of the money tied up for five years. The same money in a sovereign gold bond, which carries no making charge and no purity deduction, would have a very different round-trip result.

## Separating the ornamental from the investment

The practical conclusion is not that jewellery should never be bought. It is that the buyer should know which decision they are being asked to make. If the goal is ornament, buy the piece as ornament, judge it as a designed object, and accept the making charge as the cost of the design — the same way one accepts the price of a painting or a car. If the goal is investment, buy the metal in the form with the lowest round-trip cost: a coin, a bar or a spot-referenced vehicle such as an exchange-traded fund or a sovereign gold bond. The mistake is not buying jewellery; it is buying jewellery and calling it an investment, because the two have different economics and different round-trip costs.

A useful test is to ask, before buying, whether the piece would still be worth buying if its resale value were zero. If the answer is yes, the piece is ornament and the making charge is the price of the ornament. If the answer is no, the piece was being bought as an investment, and the buyer should reconsider the form. The test is simple and it prevents the most common error in Indian gold buying: paying an ornamental price and expecting an investment return.

## The jewellery decision checklist

Before buying, answer these in order:

1. **Decision.** Is this ornament or investment, and which one am I actually making?
2. **Metal value.** What is the prevailing metal rate for the weight and carat of the piece?
3. **Making charge.** What is the fabrication fee as a rupee amount and as a percentage of the metal value?
4. **Stones.** What are the stones worth at resale, and what fraction of their retail value is recovered?
5. **Hallmark.** Is the piece hallmarked, and is the hallmark from a licensed source?
6. **Round-trip.** What must the metal price do, over the intended holding period, to cover the making charge and the purity deduction?
7. **Alternative.** What would the same money buy in a coin, a bar or a spot-referenced vehicle, and what is the difference in round-trip cost?

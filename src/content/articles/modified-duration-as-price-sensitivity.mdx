A holder who owns a bond wants to know one thing before anything else: if interest rates move against me, how much do I lose? That question has a precise first-order answer, and it is modified duration. The measure is short enough to fit in a sentence, and it is quoted for almost every debt fund, portfolio and security in India. It is also misunderstood in a consistent way, because it is usually presented as a fact about the bond rather than as the first term of an approximation with a stated domain. This article derives it, works the estimate in both directions, and states exactly where the linear reading stops being usable.

## From Macaulay duration to price sensitivity

Start with the price as the sum of discounted cash flows, with the yield as the single discount rate that reconciles the cash flows to the observed price:

$$ P = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{C_k}{(1+y)^{t_k}} $$

Differentiating the price with respect to the yield brings a factor of the time down from each exponent:

$$ \frac{dP}{dy} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} -t_k \, C_k \, (1+y)^{-t_k-1} $$

The factor of one plus the yield is common to every term, so it can be taken outside:

$$ \frac{dP}{dy} = -\frac{1}{1+y} \sum_{k=1}^{n} t_k \, C_k \, (1+y)^{-t_k} $$

The sum that remains is exactly the numerator of Macaulay duration, the present-value-weighted sum of the times. Dividing it by the price produces Macaulay duration, so the sum equals the price multiplied by Macaulay duration:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n} t_k \, C_k \, (1+y)^{-t_k} = P \times D_{Mac} $$

Substituting this back gives the sensitivity result:

$$ \frac{dP}{dy} = -\frac{P \times D_{Mac}}{1+y} $$

Define modified duration as Macaulay duration divided by one plus the yield per compounding period:

$$ D_{Mod} = \frac{D_{Mac}}{1 + y} $$

Then the derivative becomes the statement that the proportional change in price is modified duration times the change in yield, with the sign reversed:

$$ \frac{dP}{P} = -D_{Mod} \, dy \qquad \text{and therefore} \qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{Mod} \, \Delta y $$

That is the whole mechanism. Modified duration is Macaulay duration rescaled by one plus the yield so that it converts a yield change into a proportional price change directly.

## Explain simply

Macaulay duration tells you the average time you wait for the money, measured in years. Modified duration turns that waiting time into a price sensitivity by dividing it by one plus the yield. The practical reading is simple: a modified duration of 2.6 means that if the yield rises by one percentage point, the price falls by roughly 2.6 per cent; if the yield falls by one percentage point, the price rises by roughly 2.6 per cent. It is an estimate, and the word roughly is doing real work, but for small moves it is a very good estimate. For big moves, and for bonds with unusual features, it stops being good, and that is what the rest of the article is about.

## The per-period adjustment

If a bond pays coupons more than once a year, the derivation runs in periods rather than years and the adjustment changes to match the period yield. With a quoted annual yield and a compounding frequency of several periods a year, modified duration is Macaulay duration divided by one plus the yield per period:

$$ D_{Mod} = \frac{D_{Mac}}{1 + y / m} $$

where the frequency is the number of coupons a year and the yield is quoted on the same annual basis as the cash flows. The two measures must be expressed on the same convention before any price change is computed. Indian dated government securities, for instance, commonly pay semi-annually, so the per-period yield is half the quoted annual yield and the divisor is one plus half the annual yield. Using an annual divisor on a semi-annual bond is a routine arithmetic error that produces a duration that is too short.

## Worked example

Take a constructed bond: ₹1,000 face value, a 7.00 per cent annual coupon, three years to maturity, priced at par because the yield is also 7.00 per cent. This is an illustrative calculation, not a quotation and not a forecast. Its Macaulay duration is 2.808 years, so its modified duration is:

$$ D_{Mod} = \frac{2.808}{1.07} = 2.624 $$

Now apply the formula to three yield scenarios. The exact price in each row is obtained by discounting the fixed cash flows at the new yield.

| Yield moves to | Estimate | Duration-only price | Exact price | Gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8.00 per cent, up 100 bp | −2.624 per cent | ₹973.76 | ₹974.23 | +₹0.48 |
| 6.00 per cent, down 100 bp | +2.624 per cent | ₹1,026.24 | ₹1,026.73 | +₹0.49 |
| 10.00 per cent, up 300 bp | −7.873 per cent | ₹921.27 | ₹925.39 | +₹4.12 |

Three things are visible. The estimate works well for a hundred basis points in either direction, with a gap of under 50 paise on a ₹1,000 bond. The estimate is too pessimistic on both sides, because the duration-only price sits below the exact price in every row, which is the effect of convexity. And the gap grows faster than the shock: tripling the move from 100 to 300 basis points increases the gap from about ₹0.48 to about ₹4.12, an increase of more than eight times, which is the quadratic growth of the omitted term.

The same arithmetic on a longer bond shows why duration matters more as maturity extends. A constructed ten-year bond with a 7.00 per cent coupon at a 7.00 per cent yield has a Macaulay duration of about 7.52 years and a modified duration of about 7.02. A hundred basis point rise is estimated to cost about 7.02 per cent of price, or roughly ₹3.12 more than the exact fall, which is already a larger absolute error than the three-year bond produced under the same shock.

## The price value of a basis point

A related and convenient form of the same number is the price value of a basis point, often called the price value of a basis point or DV01. It is the change in price for a one basis point move, obtained from the same relationship:

$$ PVBP = P \times D_{Mod} \times 0.0001 $$

For the three-year bond above, the price value of a basis point is about ₹0.2624, or roughly 26 paise per ₹1,000 of face value. For the ten-year bond it is about ₹0.70. The measure is useful because it is additive across positions, so a portfolio's sensitivity is the sum of the price values of its holdings. It also has the same domain of validity as modified duration: it is a first-order, parallel-shift estimate and carries the same convexity error.

## Why the linear estimate fails as the move grows

The price-yield relationship is curved, and duration captures only the slope of the tangent at one point. A fuller expansion adds a second-order term built from convexity:

$$ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{Mod} \, \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} \, C \, (\Delta y)^2 $$

where the convexity term is positive for an option-free bond. Because that term is positive, the true price lies above the duration-only line for any nonzero move. The duration estimate therefore overstates the loss when yields rise and understates the gain when yields fall, and the size of the misstatement rises with the square of the yield change. For a hundred basis points on a short bond the omitted term is a rounding matter. For three hundred basis points it is visible. For a long-duration bond under a large shock it can be the difference between two materially different numbers, and at that point the honest approach is to revalue the bond at the shocked yield rather than to patch the approximation.

## Where the approximation breaks, precisely

Four conditions mark the boundary of the estimate.

- **The size of the move.** The error grows quadratically in the yield change. The estimate is useful for small parallel moves and progressively misleading for large ones, especially at long duration. There is no universal threshold, because the error also scales with duration, but any move large enough to be described as a rate cycle rather than a rate wobble should be accompanied by a convexity term or a full revaluation.
- **A non-parallel shift.** Modified duration assumes every point on the curve moves by the same amount. When the curve twists, with the short end moving differently from the long end, one duration number aggregates the change incorrectly even if the average move is small.
- **Changing cash flows.** The derivation holds the cash flows fixed. Floating-rate notes reset their coupon at each reset date, so their price sensitivity collapses to the time to the next reset rather than to the life of the instrument. Inflation-linked bonds have cash flows that move with realised inflation. In both cases the analytic formula is the wrong tool.
- **Embedded options.** A callable bond gives the issuer the right to repay early when rates fall. As yields fall toward the call level, the price stops rising the way an option-free bond would, and the bond behaves as though its duration shortened. The correct measure is effective duration, computed by revaluing the instrument under an upward and a downward shock and taking the difference. Callable, putable, sinkable and prepayable structures all require it, and the result can be negative, which is precisely what a negative effective duration describes. A duration quoted for a portfolio that holds such assets should be read as a model-conditional effective duration and not as a Macaulay duration observed from a fixed schedule.

## Spread risk is not duration risk

Modified duration measures sensitivity to the yield used to discount the cash flows. For a sovereign bond that yield is essentially the benchmark. For a corporate bond the yield is the benchmark plus a credit spread, so a change in price can come from either. If the benchmark is unchanged but the issuer's spread widens, the bond loses value, and duration does not distinguish the two sources because it takes the whole yield as its input. A rate-shock estimate built on duration is therefore a statement about the curve, not about credit, and the two should be reported separately rather than netted into a single number. The same caution applies to liquidity: a widening bid-offer spread reduces the amount a seller receives without appearing anywhere in a duration figure.

## Running a first-order rate-shock estimate

The useful procedure is short and its discipline lies in the labelling.

1. Take the current price and the modified duration of the position, on a consistent period convention.
2. Choose a shock and state it as a scenario. A scenario is not a forecast.
3. Compute the estimated proportional change as modified duration times the shock, with the sign reversed, and convert it to an amount using the position value.
4. If the shock is large, or the duration long, add the convexity term or revalue the position outright.
5. For a portfolio, use the market-value-weighted average modified duration and the summed price value of a basis point, and report the result as an estimate conditional on a parallel shift.
6. Report the result in the model-estimated lane, keep spread and liquidity effects separate, and never present the estimate as an observed loss.

## The four lanes

- **Contractual.** The coupon and redemption are fixed, and the initial price is an observed transaction.
- **Historical realised.** A past period's actual return is the sum of income, price change and reinvestment, and it is not implied by any current duration.
- **Model-estimated.** The estimated price change from duration, and any convexity correction, is a model output conditional on a parallel shift.
- **Speculative.** Any view that the policy rate will be cut or raised, or that yields will move by a particular amount, is a forecast and belongs in this lane.

## What this does not tell you

- **Any current yield, price or duration.** All inputs here are constructed. Take market levels from a published series.
- **Credit and liquidity outcomes.** Duration is silent on default, on rating migration and on the price impact of selling an illiquid line.
- **The direction of rates.** Duration sizes a move; it does not predict one.
- **Tax.** The after-tax treatment of income and capital gains is a separate question for the tax reference.

## Open questions

- At what size does a parallel shock make the convexity correction essential rather than a refinement for a retail portfolio report?
- How should duration be aggregated when holdings sit at very different tenors and the expected move is a twist rather than a parallel shift?
- For a fund holding callable and prepayable assets, how stable is the disclosed effective duration across scenarios?

Modified duration earns its prominence because it compresses an entire bond into one number that answers a practical question with serviceable accuracy. Its limitation is equally simple: it is a tangent, and a tangent describes a curve accurately only close to the point where it touches. Used within its domain, with the sign, the convention and the size of the move all stated, it is one of the most efficient risk summaries available. Used past that domain, without a convexity check or a revaluation, it is a number that is precisely wrong.

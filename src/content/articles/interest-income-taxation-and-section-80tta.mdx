Interest is the least glamorous return in an Indian portfolio and one of the most reliably taxed. Savings-account interest, fixed-deposit interest, recurring-deposit interest and post-office interest are all charged to tax as income from other sources and are added to total income at the applicable slab rate. There is no concessional rate for deposit interest, no indexation and no exemption for having held the deposit for a long time. The only relief a non-senior resident gets is a small, narrowly drawn deduction under section 80TTA — and it survives only in the old regime.

This article states the position as verified against the cited sources for **FY 2025-26 (AY 2026-27)**, with the deduction limits and the tax-deduction mechanics stated separately because they carry different review statuses. It is an explainer: the arithmetic is simple, and the errors are not in the arithmetic but in what counts as interest, when it is taxed, and who may claim the deduction.

## The rule in one paragraph

Interest is taxable under the head income from other sources. It is added to total income and taxed at the slab rate applicable to the taxpayer, in both regimes: fixed-deposit interest is fully taxable and there is no special rate. A resident individual below the senior-citizen age may deduct up to **₹10,000** of **savings-account** interest under section 80TTA in the old regime. A resident senior citizen may instead deduct up to **₹50,000** of interest on deposits under section 80TTB, also in the old regime. Neither deduction is available in the new regime. Tax may be deducted at source on interest above a statutory threshold, but that deduction is an instalment, not a final tax.

Every clause after the first sentence is where returns go wrong, so take them in order.

## Interest is ordinary income

The starting point is unflattering: deposit interest gets no special treatment. It is not a capital gain, it is not business income unless the taxpayer is in the business of lending, and it is not exempt. It sits with total income and is taxed at the marginal slab rate. For a taxpayer in the 30% band, a fixed-deposit coupon is worth roughly its headline rate less 30% and the surcharge and cess that apply on top; for a taxpayer below the basic exemption, the same interest is taxable but the liability may be nil after the rebate and exemption.

Two structural points follow. First, because interest is added to total income, a large interest receipt can push other income into a higher slab, and the marginal cost is the rate at the top of the stack rather than an average. Second, because interest is ordinary income rather than a special-rate gain, it carries the full surcharge scale: the 15% surcharge cap that protects short-term and long-term equity gains and dividend income does not protect interest. At the highest income levels that difference is material. The **taxable equivalent yield calculator** turns that into the useful question: what a fully taxable deposit would have to yield in order to match an instrument whose return is already tax-free.

## The accrual rule, and the cumulative-deposit trap

Interest is taxed on accrual or receipt, according to the method of accounting the taxpayer follows. For most individuals that means accrual, and the practical consequence is that a bank or post office reports interest accrued on a cumulative deposit annually even though no cash has been credited. The tax is payable on the accrual; the cash arrives only at maturity. A taxpayer in a high band who has chosen a cumulative option for compounding reasons can therefore face a liability on income that has not yet been received, and the interest that compounds on top of it is itself taxed as it accrues. This is a cash-flow trap rather than a rate trap, and it is the reason a taxable deposit should be judged on the after-tax accrual, not on the maturity value.

## Section 80TTA, and where 80TTB fits

Section 80TTA allows a resident individual who is not a senior citizen a deduction of up to **₹10,000** against **savings-account interest**. Three limits are built into that sentence. It is a deduction against savings-account interest specifically, so fixed-deposit interest does not qualify. It is capped at ₹10,000 in total, so holding savings accounts at several banks does not multiply it. And it is available only to a resident individual below the senior-citizen age, and only in the old regime.

Section 80TTB is the senior-citizen counterpart, and it is broader and larger: a resident senior citizen may deduct up to **₹50,000** of interest on deposits. It is not confined to savings-account interest as section 80TTA is, which is why it is the more valuable of the two for the cohort it covers. Like section 80TTA, it is available only in the old regime.

The regime point deserves emphasis because it is a real cost of the default. A taxpayer who switches to the new regime gives up both deductions, and the same interest becomes fully taxable. For a senior citizen with a large deposit book, the ₹50,000 deduction is the single most concrete reason to compute the old regime rather than assume the new one. The comparison is set out in the companion article on [choosing between the regimes](/library/tax-regimes-and-the-investor-choice).

## Explain simply

Interest you earn on a savings account or a fixed deposit is treated as ordinary income and taxed at your normal rate. There is no special low rate for it. If you are below senior-citizen age, the old regime lets you deduct up to ₹10,000 of interest earned in a savings account; if you are a senior citizen, it lets you deduct up to ₹50,000 of interest on deposits. The new regime gives you neither. Remember that interest on a cumulative deposit is taxed every year as it builds up, even though the bank pays you only at maturity.

## Worked example

This is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast or a recommendation. It uses the deduction limits and rates stated above.

A resident individual below 60 is in the old regime and, on other income, is already in the 30% slab. For FY 2025-26, the taxpayer has savings-account interest of **₹22,000** and fixed-deposit interest of **₹68,000**, giving total interest of **₹90,000**.

- **Section 80TTA deduction:** the lower of ₹10,000 and savings-account interest — that is, **₹10,000**. The fixed-deposit interest does not qualify for this deduction.
- **Taxable interest:** ₹90,000 − ₹10,000 = **₹80,000**.
- **Tax at the 30% slab rate:** ₹80,000 × 30% = **₹24,000**.
- **Cess at 4%:** ₹960. **Total:** **₹24,960**.
- **Value of the deduction:** without it, the tax on ₹90,000 at 30% would be ₹27,000, plus ₹1,080 cess, or ₹28,080. The section 80TTA deduction is therefore worth **₹3,120** in this case — the ₹10,000 allowance multiplied by the 30% marginal rate and the 4% cess.
- **In the new regime:** no section 80TTA is available, so the ₹10,000 is not deductible and that ₹3,120 saving disappears.

Two qualifications belong to the example. If the taxpayer's total income were below the basic exemption, the interest would still be taxable but the liability would fall to nil after the exemption and rebate, and the deduction would be worth nothing in cash terms. And the marginal-rate arithmetic depends on the other income: at a lower slab the same ₹10,000 deduction is worth less.

## Tax deducted at source is not a tax

Tax may be deducted at source on interest under the relevant provision, at the prescribed rate, once interest exceeds a monetary threshold that differs between banks, co-operative banks, post-office schemes and other payers, and that is higher for a senior citizen. The tax registry marks the current thresholds as **provisional**, because they differ by payer category and have been revised repeatedly; the article on TDS and Forms 15G and 15H takes the mechanics further.

Three consequences are more reliable than the exact thresholds. First, the threshold is a deduction threshold, not an exemption: interest below it remains fully taxable and must be reported. Second, tax deducted is not a final tax. Because it is deducted at a flat statutory rate rather than at the taxpayer's marginal rate, a taxpayer in the 30% slab will have a shortfall, while a taxpayer below the taxable threshold will have paid too much and will need to claim a refund. Third, the deducted amount is creditable against the final liability, so it should be reconciled rather than filed away. Where the deduction does not cover the liability — for example, where interest is spread across payers each below the threshold — the shortfall feeds into the advance-tax machinery and interest can accrue on it. The **advance tax schedule calculator** lays out the instalment dates and the interest exposure.

It is worth recording that the Income-tax Act, 2025, which takes effect from 1 April 2026, consolidates the tax-deduction provisions and revises the interest thresholds for Tax Year 2026-27. The individual section mapping between the 1961 and 2025 Acts is flagged in this site's tax registry as **pending verification**, and this article will be updated as official guidance settles it.

## What interest is not

The relief discussed here applies to taxable deposit interest. Interest that is genuinely exempt is a different case and is not reported as taxable interest at all. Interest credited in a Public Provident Fund is free of tax, and so is the interest in a Sukanya Samriddhi Account, in both regimes; the exempt treatment of the interest and the maturity proceeds is not confined to the old regime, and only the deduction on contribution is. Interest on an Employees' Provident Fund balance is exempt subject to the conditions and monetary thresholds that the scheme prescribes, a rule the tax registry records as provisional. Sovereign Gold Bond interest sits on the taxable side: it is paid at 2.50% per annum, tranche-dependent, and is taxable at the applicable slab rate, even though redemption of the bond by an individual at maturity is exempt.

A second category to keep separate is interest that is deducted against the cost of earning it. Interest paid on a loan taken to invest in a deposit can be deducted against that interest income where the borrowing is for the purpose of earning the income, subject to the specific provision and its limits. That is a netting of expenses against a taxable receipt, not an exemption, and it does not enlarge the section 80TTA or 80TTB allowance.

## What this does not tell you

This article states the rate and the deduction limits but does not compute a taxpayer's liability, because that requires the full income picture and the slab in which the interest lands. It does not list the current tax-deduction thresholds by payer category, because those are recorded as provisional and vary; it describes their structure instead. It does not cover the special position of a non-resident, whose interest may attract different withholding treatment, or the treatment of interest that is business income because the taxpayer is in the business of lending. And it does not resolve the 1961-to-2025 section mapping. Each of those is a reason to check the primary source and the taxpayer's own facts rather than to rely on the summary.

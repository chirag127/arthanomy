Group cover is the insurance most people hold without having chosen it. It arrives with a joining letter, is deducted quietly from a payslip, and lives in a benefits portal that few employees reopen. Its attraction is real: pooling a large group and rating it as a whole costs a fraction of buying the same protection individually, because the insurer underwrites hundreds of lives at once and earns its margin without the expense of selling to each person separately. Its limitation is just as real. The contract belongs to the employer, and the employee is a member of the group rather than a party to it. Reading group cover correctly means holding both facts at the same time.

This article is part of the Insurance and Pensions Decoded series. It explains what employer-provided life and health cover contains, how the employer-as-policyholder relationship works, what happens to the cover when employment ends, and the portability and continuity questions a reader should raise before relying on it.

## Why an employer can buy cover more cheaply

Individual protection is priced against one person's age, health, habits and occupation. Group protection is priced against a population. The insurer receives a census of the workforce, applies a group rate, and typically does not medically assess each member on entry — which is precisely why an employee with a health condition that would load an individual premium can still be covered under the group scheme.

The trade is straightforward. The employer gains affordable protection for its staff and, in many cases, a liability that would otherwise sit with the family after a death in service. The employee gains cover that would be expensive or unobtainable on individual terms. What the member does not gain is control. The sum insured, the insurer, the schedule of benefits and the renewal terms are the employer's decisions, renegotiated each year, and the member is notified rather than consulted. That is the structural feature to internalise before treating workplace cover as a foundation.

## What a group scheme usually contains

Employer-arranged cover is a bundle of separate contracts rather than a single product, and each part behaves differently.

- **Group term life cover** pays a sum on the death of a member during service. Because there is no maturity or investment element, it is the cheapest way to buy a large death benefit, and it is usually the largest single piece of protection many households hold.
- **Group health cover** reimburses or settles hospitalisation costs for the member and, where the design allows, dependants. This is the part most people actually use, and the part whose continuity rules matter most.
- **Personal accident cover** pays on death or disability arising from an accident, and is written on a different trigger from life cover.
- **Critical illness or hospital-cash add-ons**, where offered, pay a fixed amount on a defined diagnosis or per day of hospitalisation rather than reimbursing actual cost.

Each of these is a distinct policy line with its own conditions, exclusions and renewal. A member who reads the bundle as one promise will misjudge which risks are actually covered.

## The employer is the policyholder

The single most important structural fact is that the employer holds the master policy and the employee holds a certificate of membership under it. That single fact produces most of the consequences.

Because the employer is the policyholder, it is the employer that deals with the insurer, provides the member data, decides the benefits and receives the renewal quotation. The employee's relationship is with the employer's scheme, and any claim travels through that scheme and its administrator or third-party administrator before it reaches the insurer. Insurable interest sits with the employer, and the employer also determines what happens to the cover when the scheme is redesigned or the insurer changed.

For the member, three practical consequences follow. First, a query about cover, a claim or a correction goes through the scheme's channel, so the member should keep the certificate, the schedule of benefits and the policy wording rather than only a portal login. Second, the cover can change without the member's consent at renewal. Third, the protection a member might think of as personal is, legally, a benefit of the employment relationship — and it therefore stands or falls with that relationship.

## What happens when the job ends

Membership is normally tied to active employment. When the member leaves, retires or is made redundant, the cover generally ceases or is reduced on the exit date or shortly after, subject to whatever the contract says about a grace period or a run-off.

Some master policies include a **conversion facility** that lets a departing member buy an individual policy without fresh medical evidence, usually within a stated window and at individual rates. Others offer a **continuation or retiree cover** option for health insurance. These are terms of the particular contract, not general rights, and their details — the window, the age limits, the price, the conditions — are exactly the things a member should confirm in writing well before the exit date. Because the window can be short and the facility may require active election, a member who waits until the last week of employment may find the route already closed.

The health side deserves separate attention. Group health cover is renewed annually, and a member who leaves mid-year may have cover that stops partway through the policy year. Whether waiting periods already served under the group policy carry across to a new individual policy depends on the applicable health-insurance rules and on what the receiving insurer offers. The existence of portability in principle does not settle what credit a particular insurer will give, so it should be checked rather than assumed.

## Group life and group health are different contracts

The two halves of workplace cover have different continuity stories, and it is worth keeping them apart.

Life cover is a death benefit; its continuity question is whether the member can convert to an individual policy and at what price, given that age and any change in health since joining may now matter. Health cover is a reimbursement contract; its continuity question is whether the waiting periods already served are recognised and whether any pre-existing conditions are excluded afresh. A member who loses group life cover typically needs to replace it with individual term cover sized to the household's obligations. A member who loses group health cover typically needs an individual or family floater policy, and the date of the transition can decide what is covered.

The distinction between a *protection* contract and any *savings* element bundled into employer arrangements matters too. Where a scheme includes a savings or unit-linked component, it is governed by the same product rules that apply to individually sold versions, including the rules on charges, surrender and discontinuance. A member should read that element as an investment that happens to sit inside a benefits programme, not as part of the protection.

## The questions a reader should ask

The discipline with workplace cover is to convert assumptions into written answers. Before relying on it, a reader can ask the following.

1. What is the exact sum insured, and does it change with salary, grade or age?
2. Does the cover include dependants, and which ones?
3. Does the scheme continue for a period after I leave, and on what terms?
4. Is there a conversion facility to an individual policy, and what is the window and price?
5. For health cover, which waiting periods have been served, and will a new insurer credit them?
6. Who administers claims, and what documents does a claim need?
7. Is any part of the cover a savings or unit-linked product, and what are its charges and surrender terms?

These are not adversarial questions. They are the questions that decide whether a household's protection survives a change of job, and the answers belong in a file rather than in memory.

## How much to rely on workplace cover

The honest answer is: less than most households do. Employer cover is sized by the employer, not by the household. It rarely reflects the mortgage outstanding, the years of income a dependant would need, or the cost of education commitments. It is also contingent, and the contingency — losing the job — often coincides with the moment the cover is most needed.

That is why individually held term cover is usually described as the foundation and employer cover as an addition to it. The framework is simple: size the protection the household needs against its obligations, then subtract the cover it actually controls, then treat the employer scheme as a buffer on top rather than as the base. Where the employer provides a large sum insured, counting it reduces the individual cover needed — but only for as long as the employment lasts, which is an assumption worth stating explicitly when the sum is chosen.

## Explain simply

Your employer buys life and health insurance for its staff as one big contract, and you are covered as a member of that group rather than as the owner of your own policy. It is cheap because the insurer prices the whole workforce together, but it belongs to your job: when the job ends, the cover usually ends too, unless the contract lets you convert it to a policy of your own. Group life and group health end differently and have different rules. Treat workplace cover as a helpful extra, not as the protection your family is relying on, and ask in writing what happens to it when you leave.

## Worked example: an illustrative gap

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast or a recommendation. The figures are deliberately round and are not taken from any employer's scheme.

Suppose a household's obligations are assessed on an illustrative basis. Annual income is taken as ₹12,00,000. Using a simple income-replacement multiple of ten as an illustrative assumption, the income-replacement need is ₹1,20,00,000. Add the mortgage outstanding of ₹40,00,000 and subtract assets already earmarked for the same purpose of ₹20,00,000, and the illustrative protection requirement is ₹1,40,00,000.

Now suppose the employer's group life cover is three times annual salary, an illustrative structure rather than a quotation, giving ₹36,00,000. The gap between what the household needs and what it controls through employment is therefore about ₹1,04,00,000.

The arithmetic is not the point; the direction of the result is. The employer cover looks substantial in absolute terms and covers roughly a quarter of the illustrative requirement. Change the income multiple or the liabilities and the gap moves, but it rarely closes — and it widens the moment the employment ends. The purpose of the calculation is to show that the sizing question is separate from the question of what the employer happens to provide.

## What this does not tell you

This article describes structure. It deliberately states no sum insured, premium, entry age, waiting period, conversion window or portability condition, because those vary by scheme and are set in the master policy or the applicable rules rather than in any general account. A reader holding employer cover should read the certificate, the schedule of benefits and the policy wording, and confirm the exit and conversion terms in writing. Where a figure is needed and not available, it should be obtained from the policy and the insurer rather than inferred.

Tax treatment of employer-provided cover is not restated here. Whether a benefit is taxable, and how it is reported, is governed by the Income-tax Act as amended by successive Finance Acts, and the position should be verified before it is relied on. Nothing here is advice, no insurer or scheme is recommended, and no protection outcome is promised.

The first thing a fake advisory group produces is not a trade, or a tip, or a product. It is an atmosphere. The member who joins a Telegram or WhatsApp group under a broker-like name, or under the personal name of someone introduced as a market veteran, walks into a room that appears to be winning: green screenshots, congratulatory messages, slot sizes filled, "target achieved, please withdraw and re-enter", and a moderator who answers quickly and calmly. Nothing about that room is expensive to build, and nothing about it is evidence of what it looks like.

This article describes how that atmosphere is manufactured, which parts of it are genuine and which are decoration, and the checks that can be performed from outside the group in a few minutes, none of which depend on the group's cooperation.

## Explain simply

A real advisory firm in India is a registered intermediary, named as such on the regulator's published list, with a registration number that matches the name and the activity. Its advice may be good or bad, costly or cheap, but it is the entity that is accountable for what it says, and there is somewhere to go if it goes wrong. A fake advisory group borrows the vocabulary, the urgency and the screenshots of that world without any of its accountability. It exists in a messaging app where messages can be deleted, members can be removed, and names can be changed overnight, and its economics do not depend on being right; they depend on converting one paying member into several paying members, on collecting a fee before the first trade is made, and on moving its audience from free tips to premium levels to paid products that are progressively less regulated and more profitable for the operator. The checks that matter are all external: the registration number, the entity that receives the fee, and the destination of the money once the member is moved on.

## The anatomy of a group

Most such groups share a construction that is closer to a sales funnel than to a community, and recognising the stages is half of the defence.

Stage one, the public channel. A free channel or group with a name that sounds like an established brand or a famous investor, posting market calls, charts and a running commentary that reads as confident. Entry is free because the group's job at this stage is volume. Member counts are inflated by members who have been added without their consent, using imported contact lists or shared group links, so a high member count says nothing about engagement. The posts are a mix of plausible-sounding market commentary and occasional striking calls. The striking calls are the ones that matter: they are selective. A ten-call performance record is easy to present as four wins when six losses are never posted, and when the deleted losses leave no trace in a chat that anyone can search.

Stage two, the premium step. Members are moved to a smaller group, often by a direct message from an "assistant" or "relationship manager", on payment of a fee described as a membership, a mentorship, a signalling service, or an algorithmic subscription. The fee itself is the product's main revenue; the advice is secondary. The premium group's distinguishing feature, predictably, is that the tips are asserted to be more accurate, and the losses, if mentioned at all, are blamed on an external force — the market, news, manipulation — that the operator could not have been expected to foresee.

Stage three, the escalation. The group stops pretending to be an advisory service and becomes the doorway to something else: a platform the member should deposit into, a block deal before it is listed, an IPO allotment, an OTC desk, a "fund" that compounds monthly at a fixed rate, or an unregulated product sold through a private form and a UPI link. The move is gradual, delivered in the language of friendship and opportunity, and by the time it arrives most members have stopped asking why they are reading it.

Stage four, the extraction. The product asks for money. Withdrawals fail. The original group is archived, renamed or deleted, and the operator starts again under a different name, often with the same members who do not know they have been moved.

## How respectable screenshots are manufactured

Screenshots are the currency of these groups, and nearly all of them can be produced without anybody making or losing money.

A trading screenshot can be a genuine cropped image from a genuine account, presented without the trades that lost money and without the total column. It can be a screenshot of a demo account that contains demonstration funds rather than deposited funds. It can be an image of a paper trading or simulation account that no brokerage connection ever touched. It can be a fabricated image produced with freely available software, with realistic-looking identifiers placed in it. Or it can be a genuine screenshot whose numbers are true but describe a period chosen to show the best result, from a population of traders that happens to survive this week while the ones who did not survive are not in the picture. Each of these is cheaper than running a genuine business, and most groups rely on the first kind.

The surrounding theatre completes the illusion. Members are encouraged to post their own results, which creates two effects. The members with wins post; the members with losses stay quiet or leave. The operator also has members of its own staff, under pseudonyms, posting favourable comments, asking apparently innocent questions like "when is the next premium batch" or "how much should a beginner start with". These messages serve as social proof and as delivery of the sales message through a seemingly independent voice. A group whose discussion is always upbeat, always agrees, and never features a member saying "this did not work" has been curated, and curation is visible once the possibility is recognised.

Testimonial videos and audio clips circulate in similar groups. A video of a member saying that a fixed monthly profit arrived on time is evidence that the member believed it, which is not the same thing as evidence that it happened. When no independent document — a statement from a regulated entity, a UPI history, a contract with a counterparty — is available, the testimonial is the only thing being offered, and that should be read as what it is.

## What the group is actually selling

The apparent product is tips or signals. The actual product is a sequence of escalations that converts a free reader into a paying member and then into a depositor into an unregulated arrangement, and each stage is more profitable and less accountable than the last. Stated that way, the deception is obvious, but in practice each stage is presented as a reward — better tips for VIP members, an exclusive opportunity for the inner circle, an early entry into a fund that the group "front-runs" for the benefit of its members. The language is designed so that the member experiences the escalation as access rather than as a sales pitch.

The group also serves a defensive function. Members who were recruited through a series of escalating commitments are more committed psychologically to the narrative than a person who simply invested money, and their objections, when they arise, are handled inside the group by the group. A private channel where dissent is removed has no correction mechanism at all, which is why a group that deletes sceptical questions is the most reliable sign available that something is wrong, more reliable than any screenshot or certificate.

## Verification that does not depend on the group

Four checks, each of which takes a few minutes and uses only sources that are independent of the group.

First, the registration number. If the group claims to be or to represent an advisory firm, the name has to appear on the regulator's own published list of registered intermediaries, with the registration number matching the claims. A certificate displayed in the group's pinned message, or a PDF link, is not verification; the number has to be searched on the regulator's site. Two common fraud techniques are worth knowing: the entity does exist and is registered, but for a different activity than the one being sold; and the entity does exist but the person in the chat is not it. The registration number plus the exact legal name plus the permitted activity, matched on the regulator's own site, is the only verification that counts.

Second, the money trail. Fees collected by UPI or bank transfer should go to an account in the name of the registered entity. A payment handle that belongs to a personal name, a different firm, or a "director's" account is a failure, and the displayed beneficiary name at the moment of payment is the evidence, not the explanation offered in chat. The same test applies to any ultimate product: a fund subscribing to a "block deal" through a personal account is not a fund.

Third, the public face. A legitimate intermediary has a website that is reachable without logging in, with a published fee schedule, the names of the responsible persons, a grievance officer and the registration details printed on it. The group may mention the website without the website mentioning the group; the absence of any mention in either direction is itself a notable fact. A firm whose public presence is exhausted by a chat channel has no public accountability.

Fourth, the return claim. If any promise or strong implication of a fixed return is made anywhere in the group's history, that belongs to the analysis of return language more fully elsewhere: market-linked outcomes cannot be lawfully promised in India, and the presence of such a promise identifies the activity as either something other than adviser, or something that is not what it says. A legitimate adviser is authorised to give an opinion, not to underwrite an outcome.

## The aggregator pattern

A specific variant is worth singling out, because it defeats the simple check of whether the name is registered. Fraudulent operators sometimes recruit genuinely registered small intermediaries as a front, or present themselves as the research arm of a regulated firm, or arrange to collect fees through an entity that the member has heard of and trusts. In those structures the registration check passes, the public website looks credible, and the member concludes that the group is safe, even though the value actually being delivered and the money being collected have nothing to do with the registered entity.

The indicators are specific. The fee lands somewhere other than the registered entity. The person posting in the group is not named as a director, principal officer or associate of that entity anywhere on the firm's public site or regulatory filings. The group's advice contradicts, or has nothing to do with, the firm's published research or stated mandate. And the firm's grievance officer, if contacted, does not know that the group exists. Each of these checks is independent of the chat, which is why the chat alone can never be the complete basis for a decision.

## Red flags that appear in the group before any money moves

A few features recur so predictably that they are worth treating as disqualifying regardless of how professional the group otherwise looks.

- New members are added without their consent, particularly to groups that share a name with an established entity.
- The group disallows voice notes or external links, or restricts members to reactions only, so that the only text is the operator's.
- Sceptical questions are deleted, muted, or answered with reassurance rather than with substance.
- Screenshots are the only performance evidence, and no document exists that connects a number to a regulated counterparty.
- The group promises that members who act quickly get priority allocation, which converts urgency into a payment.
- Members are asked to keep recommendations secret from family, spouse or accountant.
- The same mobile number posts under multiple names, which is visible when the same identifier recycles across member messages and an admin responds identically.
- Payouts or account credits are offered as evidence of success without any document from a regulated entity.

## What genuine advisory service looks like

For contrast, the structure it is trying to imitate has properties that a group cannot fake for long. The intermediary is registered and its name matches what it calls itself. The scope of advice is defined in writing, including what it will not do. The fees are disclosed in advance, including how they are charged and on what basis. Performance, where quoted, is drawn from audited or at least independently verifiable sources rather than from screenshots. Dispute resolution is described in the firm's public documents rather than improvised. And a member who is unhappy has somewhere to take the complaint that is independent of the firm, through the complaint routing described below.

None of this makes a genuine adviser worth paying. It makes them identifiable, and identification is the thing a fake group cannot survive once a member applies it.

## Worked example: two groups, same transcript

An illustrative comparison of two groups with superficially similar pitches, neither of which is based on a real entity.

Group one has a name that resembles a known brokerage, and an administrator sends a message: "Our research desk has identified a re-rating candidate. Target Rs 250 from Rs 150 in three months. Registered research analyst. Premium batch open at Rs 15,000. Seats limited, pay by this evening." The group has 12,000 members and a pinned screenshot of a Rs 4 lakh gain.

Group two shares a name with a small firm, and its administrator posts: "Research report released today on company X. Read the full note at our website. For subscription options and our research analyst certificate details, visit the fees page. Disclaimers attached." No screenshot. No urgency. No seat limit.

The verification that matters is not the comparison, but what it reveals about how each group wants members to decide. Group one is optimised for a payment decision within minutes, made on social proof and a deadline, with no external document to check before paying. Group two is optimised for a documented decision, made at the member's pace, with the firm's identity and scope of service inspectable in advance. The first group's life depends on stopping the member from verifying; the second's does not. That difference, rather than any registration document, is the signal, and it can be read from the first few messages.

## Reporting and recovery

The complaint route depends on what was sold, just as it does in every case, and in most of these cases the sales effort deliberately obscures that question.

Where the group collected fees for research, advisory or analytical services without being registered for that activity, the complaint about the operator sits with the securities regulator, which publishes alerts on unregistered advisers and tip providers and acts against them. A complaint in respect of a registered intermediary runs first to that entity and then to SEBI's SCORES grievance portal.

Where the operator collected fees through a bank account and failed to deliver or mis-sold, the banking route is relevant, and where the funds moved through a UPI transaction to a personal handle, the grievance route through the payment operator may record the complaint even if it cannot reverse it.

Where fees were collected for a product rather than for advice — a deposit into a fund, an allotment in an IPO, a block deal — the matter is a deposit or securities matter, depending on its shape, and the route follows accordingly: the RBI route for bank or NBFC entities through the centralised complaint portal at cms.rbi.org.in, where filing is free and the scheme's time limits and compensation ceilings apply, or the SEBI route for a securities product.

Where a misleading representation caused the loss, a consumer commission route exists in parallel under the consumer protection framework, for mis-sold services as well as products.

Underlying all of this is a criminal complaint. The conduct involved — cheating, criminal breach of trust, forgery of certificates — falls under the criminal provisions in force since July 2024, and states' Economic Offences Wings investigate precisely these patterns. A civil claim against the operator is available, in theory, whenever assets can be identified and frozen early enough to matter.

For the victim, priority should be given to preserving evidence rather than to analysis. The group will be deleted, members will be removed, and names will be changed. Screenshots of the full message history, the download links, the payment requests, the member counts and the administrator identifiers, saved to a device the operator does not control, are worth more than a detailed complaint sent weeks later.

## Reading a group from its language

A handful of phrases recur so often in these groups that their presence is itself diagnostic, and they are worth listing because the repetition is the evidence. "Seats limited" appears in groups whose membership is unlimited. "First comers only" appears in groups whose entry threshold is a payment, which is not a capacity but a conversion. "Pay by this evening" makes a deadline that serves the operator. "We front-run" claims coordination with an order book that no such group has access to. "Target achieved" invites redeposit rather than withdrawal, and usually appears at a moment when the member has made a gain on paper that withdrawal would convert into a real position, and withdrawal is the one thing the group discourages. "Withdraw and re-enter" is the mechanism by which profit is shown once and risked again.

The point of assembling these phrases is not stylistic. Each one is a response to a specific reluctance in the listener, and a group that needs all of them has been built around reluctance, not around advice. A genuine adviser does not need to tell you that seats are limited, because a genuine advisory capacity is not scarce and a client's gain from acting within a day is not a deadline imposed by the market. The deadlines in fraud are manufactured by the operator, because the operator's model requires cash on a schedule.

## The second-wave targeting of group members

Members of a discovered-as-fraudulent group often remain in touch, in successor groups started by the same or a related operator under a new name. That second-wave targeting is worth understanding because it is the same mechanic as advance-fee fraud, delivered to people who have already demonstrated that they will pay for unverified promises.

The lure is recovery or escalation. A newer group promises to win back what was lost in the older one, to give access to the same trades under better terms, or to introduce a "safer" premium tier. The member who lost once is a known prospect, and the loss itself is the recruitment asset, because nothing increases willingness to accept a shortcut more reliably than a recent loss. The verification discipline that should have been applied the first time — registration, money trail, public face — should be applied again, more strictly, because the operator has already proven willing to mislead this exact member. The correct response to a pitch that leads with "we can get your money back" or "this is the upgrade that fixes the previous provider" is the one that ends the interaction.

## What this does not tell you

This article does not tell you that every informal-sounding adviser or every active community is a fraud. There are registered firms with conversational styles and there are communities whose members genuinely help each other. It tells you that registration attaches to an entity and to an activity, that screenshots are not evidence, and that the operator's economic model — fees charged in advance, escalating products, urgency, secrecy — is the thing that does not appear in any genuine advisory relationship.

It does not tell you the outcome of any complaint to any regulator or to the police, because outcomes depend on evidence, on jurisdiction, on whether the operator can be identified, and on whether frozen assets exist. It does not tell you what to do with a registered adviser who is merely bad at their job, because that is a different question, settled in the relationships and exit rights defined by the engagement letter rather than by regulation. And it does not predict which name the next group will borrow, because the method is the vulnerability and the method is public.

## Open questions

Three questions remain open. Should messaging platforms be required to show, at first view, who administers a group and where that administrator is registered? Could a single regulator-facing search be embedded at the payment step, so that anyone being asked for money sees the registration status of the entity named in the request in the same interaction? And how could an advisory firm that is genuinely skilled at explaining complex products distinguish itself in a market where its vocabulary is shared by operators whose product is the vocabulary itself?

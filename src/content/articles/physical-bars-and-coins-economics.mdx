Physical gold is the simplest asset to understand and one of the most expensive to own. The buyer sees a coin or a bar, pays a price, and takes possession. Nothing in that sequence reveals what the metal will cost to hold, what it will fetch on resale, or how the purchase price compares with the value of the metal inside it. This article takes the economics of physical ownership apart: the purity standard, the premium over spot, the storage cost and the resale haircut, and then combines them into the round-trip that is the true cost of holding metal.

## Explain simply

When you buy a gold coin, you pay for the gold inside it, plus a premium for making the coin, plus the shopkeeper's margin, plus any tax. When you sell it back, you are paid for the gold inside it, minus a deduction for testing and purity. The premium you paid is gone. That gap — the premium on the way in and the discount on the way out — is the round-trip cost of physical gold, and it exists even if the price of gold itself does not move at all. On top of that, storing the coin costs money or carries risk. So the real question is not "what is the price of gold" but "what does it cost to own this coin from the day I buy it to the day I sell it."

## Purity and hallmarking: what the stamp guarantees

The first thing a physical gold buyer needs is confidence that the metal is what it claims to be. In India, that confidence comes from the hallmarking regime administered by the Bureau of Indian Standards. The regime rests on the Hallmarking Regulation 2018, the Mandatory Hallmarking Order and their dated amendments, together with jeweller registration and a network of assaying and hallmarking centres. A hallmarked piece carries a mark that certifies its purity — that a 22-carat article is 22 carat, that a 24-carat bar is 24 carat — and the BIS maintains the register of licensed jewellers and the list of approved assaying centres.

The guarantee is about purity, not price. A hallmark tells the buyer the metal is genuine; it does not tell the buyer the metal is worth what was paid for it. A hallmarked coin can still carry a large premium over the spot price, and a hallmarked bar can still be sold back at a discount. The hallmark removes the risk of being sold fake metal; it does nothing to reduce the round-trip cost of owning real metal. The two risks — authenticity and value — are separate, and hallmarking addresses only the first.

## Premiums over spot: why a coin costs more than its metal

The spot price of gold is the price of the raw metal in the international market, converted to rupees and adjusted for the import duty. A coin or bar sells for more than that. The premium covers fabrication — the cost of turning metal into a stamped, finished product — distribution, the retailer's margin, and any applicable tax. The premium is not a single number: it varies with the product, the retailer and the size of the purchase. A small coin carries a higher percentage premium than a large bar because the fabrication cost is spread over less metal, and a well-known brand carries a higher premium than a generic refiner because the brand is selling trust as well as metal.

The import duty is part of the premium. Gold and silver imports attract customs duty, including any applicable cess, as prescribed by the central government from time to time, which drives a wedge between international and domestic prices. The duty level is a key input to jewellers' pricing, to smuggling incentives and to the local premium or discount on the metal, and it is adjusted in the Budget and by notification. Domestic futures and ETF prices embed this duty, so they do not track international quotes rupee-for-rupee. The current duty rate is not carried in the packet, so it is not stated here, but its role in the premium is structural: it is one of the reasons a domestic coin costs more than the international metal price converted at the exchange rate.

## Storage: the recurring cost nobody quotes

Once the metal is bought, it has to be kept somewhere, and every option has a cost.

- **Home storage.** A home safe or a hidden location has no recurring fee, but it carries the risk of theft, loss and damage, and that risk is uninsured unless the holder specifically insures it. The cost is the expected value of the loss, which is small in probability but large in consequence.
- **Bank locker.** A bank locker has an annual fee, limited access hours and a risk that the bank's terms place on the contents. The fee is predictable and the security is professional, but the access is restricted and the fee recurs for as long as the metal is held.
- **Vaulted custody.** Some platforms offer vaulted storage in which the metal is held in a vault and the holder owns a claim on it. This transfers the storage risk to the custodian and usually involves a custody charge, but it also means the holder no longer has physical possession and must trust the custodian's solvency and honesty.

The storage cost is the most neglected line in the physical-gold budget because it is not quoted on the price tag. A coin bought for its metal value and held for a decade in a bank locker has cost the purchase price plus ten years of locker fees, and that total is the number that must be compared with a spot-referenced alternative.

## Resale: the exit haircut

Selling physical gold back is not the mirror of buying it. The buyer of the metal — a jeweller, a refiner or a platform — pays the prevailing metal rate, not the price the original buyer paid. The resale price is based on the prevailing metal rate with deductions for purity loss and any testing, and the making charge or premium paid on purchase is not recovered. The gap between the purchase price and the resale price is the round-trip cost, and it exists even if the metal price is unchanged between purchase and sale.

The size of the haircut depends on the product and the buyer. A large bar from a recognised refiner sells back close to the metal rate because the refiner's mark is trusted and the bar is easy to test. A small coin sells back at a wider discount because the fixed cost of testing and handling is a larger fraction of its value. Jewellery sells back at the widest discount of all, because the making charge is entirely lost and the stones are valued separately. The round-trip is the reason physical gold is a poor vehicle for short-term exposure: the metal price must move enough to cover the premium, the storage and the haircut before the holder sees a gain.

## Worked example: an illustrative round-trip on ten grams

The following is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast and not a live quote. It shows how the round-trip works.

Suppose a buyer purchases a ten-gram coin for an illustrative ₹75,000. The metal value of ten grams at the prevailing rate is an illustrative ₹70,000, so the premium over the metal is an illustrative ₹5,000, about 7.1 per cent. The buyer stores the coin in a bank locker at an illustrative ₹500 a year for five years, a total of ₹2,500. At the end of five years the metal price is unchanged, and the buyer sells the coin back. The resale price is the metal value less an illustrative 1 per cent testing and purity deduction, so ₹70,000 less ₹700, or ₹69,300.

- Total cost: $75000 + 2500 = ₹77,500$.
- Resale proceeds: ₹69,300.
- Round-trip loss: $77500 - 69300 = ₹8,200$, about 10.6 per cent of the total cost.

The metal price did not move at all, and the buyer still lost more than ten per cent. That is the round-trip cost of physical ownership: the premium, the storage and the haircut, combined. If the metal price had risen, the rise would have had to exceed ₹8,200 — about 11.7 per cent of the metal value — just to break even. The same calculation with a sovereign gold bond, which carries no premium, no storage cost and no haircut, would have a very different result, and that comparison is the subject of the vehicle-comparison work elsewhere in this series.

## The physical-gold checklist

Before buying metal, work the numbers in this order:

1. **Purity.** Is the piece hallmarked, and is the hallmark from a licensed jeweller or an approved assaying centre?
2. **Premium.** What is the price as a percentage over the prevailing metal value, and how does that compare with other products and retailers?
3. **Storage.** Where will the metal live, what does that cost each year, and what is the risk?
4. **Resale.** What will the metal fetch when sold back, after the testing and purity deduction?
5. **Round-trip.** What must the metal price do, over the intended holding period, to cover the premium, the storage and the haircut?
6. **Alternative.** What would the same money buy in a spot-referenced vehicle, and what is the difference in total cost?

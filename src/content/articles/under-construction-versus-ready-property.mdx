A completed flat and an under-construction flat in the same locality are not two prices for one product. They are two different contracts. One sells a finished, certified building that can be occupied, rented or resold at once. The other sells a promise: a unit described in an agreement, a construction timeline, and the right to receive that unit when the project is finished. The price gap between them is real, and so is what it buys. The question worth answering is not which is cheaper, but what the cheaper option charges in time, uncertainty and financing cost.

## Explain simply

A ready flat is a finished product. You can see it, register it and rent it out today. An under-construction flat is a contract for a future product: you pay in stages and receive the unit later. Because you are accepting delay and the risk that what is delivered does not match what was promised, the developer prices the unit below a comparable completed one. That discount is not a gift; it is the market's estimate of what the delay and the risk are worth. Whether it is a good trade depends on how long you wait, what you pay while waiting, and how much of the delivery you can actually verify.

## Two products, two definitions

The clean dividing line between the two categories is certification, not construction. A building becomes legally ready for occupation when the local planning authority certifies that it has been built to the approved plans and is fit to occupy. That certificate, the occupancy certificate, is what turns a structure into a habitable, insurable and resellable asset. A unit offered for possession without it leaves the buyer exposed to property-tax penalties, difficulty obtaining utility connections and a resale that a later purchaser's lawyer will question. Courts have treated handing over possession without the certificate as a deficiency in service.

An under-construction unit sits on the other side of that line. It exists as a registered project with a promoter, sanctioned plans, a declared completion timeline and a defined carpet area. The real-estate framework requires promoters to register projects above the prescribed size before advertising or selling, to disclose the project approvals, timelines and sanctioned plans, and to keep a mandated share of the money collected from buyers in a designated account drawn down in proportion to construction progress. None of those obligations makes the building exist. What they do is make the promise checkable and the money harder to divert.

## What the discount actually compensates for

Decompose the gap between the price of a ready unit and that of a comparable under-construction unit, and it carries at least four distinct payments.

- **Time.** Money paid now for an asset received later loses the return it could have earned in the interim. The buyer is lending the developer capital at an implicit rate that is rarely stated and almost never compared with a market rate.
- **Delivery risk.** The project may be delayed, delivered to a different specification, or not delivered at all. The buyer carries that risk through the construction period, when visibility is at its lowest and the buyer's leverage is smallest.
- **Financing cost of waiting.** Most buyers borrow. On a completed purchase the loan is disbursed and the asset is available almost at once. On an under-construction purchase the money is released in stages and interest accrues during construction on a building that cannot yet be used or let.
- **Condition and specification uncertainty.** A rendered show flat and a printed specification conceal what the delivered unit, the finishes and the common areas will actually be. The buyer discovers the answer at the point of least bargaining power.

The gap is not a discount on construction cost. Construction costs broadly the same whether the buyer pays for it in advance or on completion. The gap is the price of the four items above, and the buyer is the one accepting that bundle in exchange for the lower number. Read the discount as a quotation for time and risk, and the two listings stop competing on price alone.

## The delivery-risk mechanics and what they change

Registration is not a quality rating. It confirms that the project is inside the regulated perimeter and that specific obligations attach to the promoter. What matters for delivery risk is how those obligations bite in practice.

- **The designated account and the drawdown rule.** A mandated share of collections from allottees must be held in a designated account and released in proportion to construction actually completed. The mechanism is designed so that the buyer's money tracks the building rather than the marketing calendar. It works for the buyer only if every payment is made against the agreed schedule and receipted.
- **The declared completion timeline.** Disclosure of a completion date fixes a reference against which delay can be measured, instead of leaving the buyer arguing about a moving target.
- **The statutory remedies for delay and defect.** The framework gives the allottee remedies for delayed possession and for defects, pursued through the regulatory authority and its adjudication mechanism.

Those remedies are real, but they are claims rather than insurance. Their practical value depends on the promoter being able to pay. A buyer weighing a large discount from an unknown promoter against a smaller discount from a proven one is, at bottom, pricing the probability that the remedies will ever need to be used. That is a judgement about a party, not about a price.

## The two timelines

The timelines differ more than the prices suggest, and the difference is what the discount is ultimately paid for.

- **Ready property.** Agreement, payment, registration and possession can be compressed into a few weeks. The buyer's exposure to the developer ends at handover, and the asset can begin producing rent almost immediately.
- **Under-construction property.** The buyer typically commits at booking, signs the agreement for sale within a prescribed period, pays against construction stages through the life of the project, and receives possession once the occupancy certificate is issued and the handover formalities are complete.

A shorter timeline is not automatically better. It is a different cash-flow shape, and the two shapes have to be compared on the same axis: what each rupee costs, and when it is committed. The purchase that finishes sooner frees capital sooner; the purchase that finishes later may cost less on paper but immobilise more of the buyer's money for longer.

## Financing the two purchases

The loan mechanics differ because the collateral differs, and the difference shows up as interest paid before the asset exists.

A home loan is sanctioned against the property and repaid from income, and the sanction is conditional on the lender's own valuation and title check. For an under-construction property, disbursement is normally staggered: funds are released in tranches linked to construction stages and to the buyer's own payments, rather than handed to the promoter in one amount. The buyer therefore pays interest on money actually released. Where the loan is structured with a phase in which only interest is payable during construction, the buyer faces a period of outgo with no usable asset at the end of it, and that outgo belongs in the true cost of the unit.

Floating-rate retail home loans are commonly priced against an external benchmark linked to the central bank's policy rate, so the applicable rate resets as the benchmark moves. On a ready purchase that reset applies to a fully disbursed loan secured on a usable asset. On an under-construction purchase it applies while the buyer is still carrying construction and delivery risk. The same benchmark, applied to two different states of the same asset, does not carry the same risk.

## Carpet area and the area you pay for

Under the real-estate framework the carpet area is defined and disclosed, and it is the area figure that the agreement for sale and the promoter's disclosures are built around. It is not the only area number in a property transaction; builders also quote larger measures that fold in common-area loading. A price comparison between an under-construction and a ready unit is meaningless unless both are expressed on the same definition of area. A headline rate per unit of area computed on a loaded figure can look lower than a ready unit's rate computed on carpet area while being higher once the two are placed on the same basis. Normalising the area before comparing the price is one of the few steps that removes a genuine illusion rather than a matter of taste.

## The occupancy certificate as the pricing boundary

The occupancy certificate is where the discount is finally settled. A buyer who has taken possession of a unit that is not certified as fit for occupation has paid a ready price, or lost the benefit of the discount, for an asset with an unresolved legal problem. The framework places the responsibility for obtaining the certificate on the promoter. Until it is in hand, the under-construction price has not fully been earned back, because the two things the discount was meant to buy — time and certainty — have not been delivered.

## Tax and possession: what this packet does not fix

The tax treatment of a unit sold before completion can differ from that of a completed unit, and the point at which a liability attaches can differ as well. This article does not restate any rate or threshold, because the packet it is drawn from carries no dated tax rule for these transactions. The mechanism should be verified against the current position for the specific unit, the specific date and the buyer's status before any cost comparison is treated as final. The same caution applies to stamp duty and registration, which are set state by state and computed on the higher of the consideration or the notified minimum value for the locality. Because those charges are levied on a base that is itself defined by law, the assumption that the tax follows the headline price can be wrong.

## Worked example: the cost of waiting

An illustrative calculation, not a forecast and not advice on any project. Suppose a ready unit is available at ₹1,00,00,000, and a comparable under-construction unit is offered at ₹85,00,000 — a reduction of ₹15,00,000 — with possession expected in three years. Assume, purely for the arithmetic, that the buyer would otherwise rent at ₹25,000 a month and that monthly interest paid on the loan during construction averages ₹20,000. These figures are assumptions chosen to make the structure visible; they are not prices, rents or rates in force anywhere.

| Item | Basis | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline saving on the under-construction price | — | ₹15,00,000 |
| Rent paid while waiting | ₹25,000 × 36 months, assumed | ₹9,00,000 |
| Interest paid during construction | ₹20,000 × 36 months, assumed | ₹7,20,000 |
| Net advantage before tax and time value | Saving less the two costs | −₹1,20,000 |

On these assumptions the discount is fully consumed by the cost of waiting and turns slightly negative, before allowing for the time value of the money paid early or for the possibility of delay. Change one assumption and the sign can flip. If the under-construction discount were ₹25,00,000, or if the waiting period were two years instead of three, the discount would survive the same costs comfortably. The structure, not the specific numbers, is the point: a discount has to be compared against the full cost of waiting, not against the ready price alone.

## What this does not tell you

- It does not value either property or judge whether the headline price is fair.
- It does not predict delay. It identifies the risk and the machinery that responds to it, not the outcome for any project.
- It does not fix the tax, stamp duty or registration treatment, which vary by state, property type and date.
- It does not remove the need for a title search, an encumbrance check and legal review before money moves.
- It does not convert a home purchase into an investment decision. Where real-estate exposure rather than a residence is the objective, listed vehicles are a separate structure with their own liquidity and disclosure profile.

The middle rung of a debt ladder is the one most often sold as a sweet spot. The claim is that a short-duration scheme captures most of the yield available at the short end of the curve while taking much less interest-rate risk than a long-dated fund. The claim is not baseless, but it is commentary rather than a category property, and it holds only for a horizon that matches the band. This article sets out what a short-duration scheme is constrained to hold, why it carries a genuine blend of interest-rate and credit risk, where its yield actually comes from, and how to place it in a ladder without treating the label as a safety rating.

## Explain simply

A short-duration fund holds bonds and short-term instruments whose average time to repayment is longer than a money-market or ultra-short fund's, but shorter than that of a medium or long-duration fund. Because the average repayment date is further out, its unit value moves more when interest rates change, and because it can hold a spread of credit quality, its yield is higher than the safest short category. The result is a middle product: more movement than cash-like money, less than a long bond fund. Its suitability depends on the date the money is needed, not on how the category is advertised.

## Where the band sits

Debt schemes are organised by the framework along a maturity axis, and the short-duration band sits in the middle of the short-term region: above the money-market and ultra-short categories, and below the medium-duration band and the longer gilt and long-duration categories. Each band imposes a constraint on the portfolio's weighted-average maturity, and sometimes on the instruments it may hold, so that a label means roughly the same thing across fund houses.

The consequence is comparison. Two short-duration schemes operate under the same maturity constraint, so any difference in their behaviour comes from choices inside that constraint: where on the curve they sit, what credit quality they accept, how much cash they hold and what they charge. The band fixes the range; it does not fix the outcome.

## What the band constrains

Two structural rules define the category, and neither is restated as a number here.

- **A weighted-average maturity band.** The portfolio's average maturity must fall within the range the framework sets for the category — long enough to earn a term premium and to carry meaningful interest-rate sensitivity, short enough to keep that sensitivity moderate.
- **A credit posture.** The scheme may hold a range of debt instruments of stated minimum quality, and its actual credit position is a choice within that range rather than a fixed feature of the label.

The operative band and credit rules are set in the categorisation circular, which has been amended since the categories were introduced. Any specific figure quoted from memory should be verified against the current text, and the scheme information document states the mandate the particular scheme follows within it.

## Why it is called a sweet spot, and where the claim stops

The sweet-spot description usually rests on three arguments: the term premium is captured; the sensitivity is still moderate; and credit spread can be added on top. Each is true within limits, and each has a cost that the description tends to leave out.

- **The term premium is not a constant.** When the short end of the curve is flat or inverted, lending a little longer earns little or nothing extra, and the category's advantage over the money-market band shrinks.
- **Moderate sensitivity is still sensitivity.** A band whose duration is measured in a small number of years can lose more in a rate shock than a holder expects, precisely because the maturity is long enough to matter.
- **Spread is not free yield.** Credit spread is compensation for default risk, and in this band it is taken in size, which is why two schemes can diverge sharply when one issuer deteriorates.

The honest framing is that the band is a posture, not a promise. It is a reasonable place for money with a horizon of roughly a few years, and an unreasonable place for money that has no horizon at all.

## The mix of credit and rate risk

The distinguishing feature of the category is that neither risk dominates. In a money-market scheme, interest-rate risk is minimal and the credit exposure is confined to very short paper. In a long-dated gilt fund, credit risk is negligible and interest-rate risk is the product. In a short-duration scheme, both are live at once, which is what makes the category harder to characterise and easier to mis-sell.

That blend matters because the two risks behave differently in the same market event. A rise in yields hurts every bond portfolio, but the size of the hurt scales with duration, so a short-duration fund feels less of it than a long one. A credit event is entirely different: it does not care about duration, and it lands as a discrete loss on the specific holding that deteriorates. A scheme that has reached for yield through credit can therefore have a quiet, steady track record punctuated by a sharp single-period loss that its duration profile would never have predicted.

## Where the yield comes from

A short-duration scheme's yield is a sum of parts, and the parts are compensations.

- **Term premium.** Lending for longer than the money-market ceiling earns the difference between the short rate and the rate a little further out on the curve.
- **Credit spread.** Holding issuers that are not sovereign earns the market's price for their default risk.
- **Liquidity premium.** Holding instruments that are less readily traded than government paper earns a premium until liquidity is needed.
- **Roll-down and positioning.** If the curve slopes and time passes, a holding can gain as it ages toward a shorter, lower-yielding point on the curve. This is a real but conditional source of return that reverses when the curve changes shape.

Against these, the **expense ratio** is subtracted every year, and it is charged whether or not any of the compensations was worth taking. A higher yield is therefore a description of what the scheme has accepted, not a measure of management quality.

## The four risks, separated

- **Credit risk** is the risk that an issuer fails to pay. It is a first-order risk here, and the category's credit range is wide enough that schemes differ materially.
- **Interest-rate risk** is the risk that a change in yields revalues the portfolio. It is moderate by construction and is the source of the category's term premium.
- **Liquidity risk** is the risk of selling into a thin market to meet redemptions. It is higher than in the shortest categories because the instruments held are less cash-like.
- **Mark-to-market behaviour** transmits both rate changes and credit news into the NAV. There is no accrual illusion: the unit value shows the market's view of the portfolio each day.

## Worked example: an illustrative rate move and credit event

The following is an illustrative calculation from assumed inputs, not a forecast and not any scheme's observed result. Suppose an investor holds ₹10,00,000 in a short-duration scheme whose portfolio is assumed to have a modified duration of 1.5 years.

**A rate move.** If yields rise by half a percentage point, the approximate change in value is the modified duration multiplied by the yield change, sign reversed:

$$ \Delta P \approx -D \times \Delta y \times P $$

That is 1.5 multiplied by 0.5%, an assumed fall of about 0.75%, or roughly ₹7,500 on ₹10,00,000, before the interest the portfolio continues to earn. If the scheme is assumed to yield 7%, that is about ₹70,000 a year, roughly ₹5,800 a month on that balance, so the assumed price fall is recovered in a little over a month of income — the basis of the claim that the band can wait out a rate move, provided the horizon is long enough.

**A credit event.** Assume separately that a holding representing 5% of the portfolio defaults, with a recovery of 40% assumed, so the loss given default is 60%. The loss to the portfolio is the weight multiplied by the loss given default:

$$ \text{Loss} \approx w \times LGD $$

That is 0.05 multiplied by 0.60, or about 3% of NAV, roughly ₹30,000 on ₹10,00,000 — over five months of the assumed income, and not recoverable by waiting. Both inputs are assumptions, printed beside the result precisely so they can be challenged; the contrast between the two shapes of loss is the point.

## Placing the category in a maturity ladder

A ladder is a way of matching maturities to dates. Read as a ladder, the short-duration band is the rung for money needed in roughly one to three years. Three tests keep the placement honest.

1. **Name the date.** If the money has a date, match the band's maturity profile to it; if it does not, the money belongs lower in the ladder.
2. **Size the credit you can assess.** The band's spread is available in different qualities; the amount of it taken should be bounded by what the holder can independently evaluate.
3. **Check what the scheme actually does.** The monthly portfolio shows the weighted-average maturity and the quality; the mandate on the cover page is not the implementation.

## The four lanes, in this context

- **Contractual.** Coupons and discounts promised by specific issuers are the contractual part of the return.
- **Model-estimated.** The duration arithmetic and any roll-down projection are estimates from stated assumptions, not observations.
- **Historical realised.** What short-duration schemes delivered over a past window, computed from scheme-level NAV data.
- **Speculative.** The gain from falling yields or narrowing spreads and the loss from the reverse. In this band this lane is active, not incidental.

## What this does not tell you

- **It does not state the band.** The weighted-average maturity range and credit rules are set in a circular that has been amended; verify the current text.
- **It does not pick a scheme.** Two schemes in the band can sit at opposite ends of the risk it allows, and the disclosures are where that is checked.
- **It does not rank bands.** Longer is not better and shorter is not safer; the horizon decides.
- **It does not handle tax.** Gains from debt-oriented schemes are taxed under rules that depend on holding period and change over time.
- **It does not promise the term premium.** When the short end of the curve is flat, the category's main rationale weakens, and nothing in the label changes that.
